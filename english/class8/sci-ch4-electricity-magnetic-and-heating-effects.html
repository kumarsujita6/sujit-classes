1. The Magnetic Effect of Electric Current

In 1820, Danish scientist Hans Christian Oersted was giving a physics lecture. By pure accident, a magnetic compass happened to be resting near an electric wire connected to a battery. When Oersted closed the switch, the compass needle suddenly twitched and pointed away from the north! When he turned off the current, the needle swung back to its normal North-South alignment.

The Principle of Magnetic Effect:
Whenever electric current passes through a conducting wire, it behaves like a temporary magnet and creates an invisible magnetic field in the space surrounding the wire. This effect is known as the magnetic effect of electric current.

Figure 4.2: Oersted Experiment — Flowing current produces magnetic concentric lines that force the compass needle to deflect.

2. Electromagnets: Controllable Super Magnets

An electromagnet consists of a long coil of insulated copper wire wrapped tightly around a soft iron core (such as an iron nail). When current passes through the coil, the iron nail turns into a remarkably strong magnet. The moment the current is switched off, the magnetism vanishes almost completely!

Three Factors that Control Electromagnet Strength:

  1. Number of Turns in the Coil: Increasing the number of wire loops wrapped around the core strengthens the magnetic field. A coil with 100 turns is much stronger than one with 20 turns.
  2. Current Flowing (Number of Cells): Connecting two or three cells in series supplies greater current, dramatically increasing the magnetic pull.
  3. Core Material: Soft iron makes an excellent core because it magnetizes instantly and loses its magnetism the moment current stops. Steel is not used for temporary electromagnets because it retains permanent magnetism.
Why are Electromagnets superior to Permanent Magnets?
• Instant On/Off Control: You can drop tons of scrap iron into a truck just by turning off a switch!
• Adjustable Strength: By regulating current with a rheostat, the magnetic force can be made gentle or enormously strong.
• Reversible Poles: Reversing the battery connection instantly swaps the North and South poles.

3. Inside the Electric Bell: Step-by-Step Mechanism

The electric bell is one of the most brilliant practical demonstrations of electromagnetism. It uses an automatic make-and-break contact switch to produce continuous, rhythmic ringing!

Figure 4.3: Inside the Electric Bell — The automated make-and-break circuit creates high-speed repeating impacts on the brass gong.

The 4-Step Working Cycle:

  1. Step 1: Switch ON: Current flows through the coil. The electromagnet becomes active.
  2. Step 2: Hammer Strikes: The electromagnet pulls the soft iron armature towards itself. The hammer attached to the end strikes the gong with a loud DING!
  3. Step 3: Circuit Breaks: As the armature moves forward, its rear tip leaves contact with the adjustable contact screw. This instantly breaks the circuit!
  4. Step 4: Spring Return: With the current stopped, the electromagnet loses its magnetism. The flexible spring pulls the armature back into contact with the screw, completing the circuit again. The cycle repeats at dozens of times per second!

4. The Heating Effect & Electrical Safety Devices

When electric current flows through a conductor with high electrical resistance, the flowing electrons constantly collide with metal atoms, transferring kinetic energy that appears as thermal heat.

  • Heating Elements (Nichrome): Appliances like electric irons, room heaters, water geysers, and toasters contain coils made of Nichrome (an alloy of nickel and chromium). Nichrome has extremely high resistance and does not oxidize (burn) even when glowing red-hot at 800°C!
  • Connecting Wires (Copper): Copper wire has very low electrical resistance, so it stays cool while carrying current to the heater.
Electrical Safety: Fuse vs. MCB
• Electric Fuse: Contains a thin wire made of an alloy of tin and lead with a very low melting point. If current exceeds the safety rating (e.g., 5A or 15A) due to overloading or a short circuit, the wire gets hot, melts, and breaks the circuit, preventing fires!
• MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker): Modern homes use MCBs instead of traditional rewirable fuses. An MCB uses an internal electromagnetic switch that automatically trips OFF within milliseconds during an overload. Once the fault is fixed, you simply flip the switch back up—no wire replacing needed!
Section 3 • High-Yield Fact & Term Bank

Physics Formulae & Core Concepts

Quick-reference facts and exam-favorite terminology.

Oersted Effect

The magnetic deflection of a compass needle caused by the magnetic field generated by an electric current flowing through a nearby wire (1820).

Electromagnet

A temporary magnet made by winding an insulated wire coil around a soft iron core, which acts as a magnet only while electric current is flowing.

Nichrome

An alloy of nickel and chromium having very high resistance and high melting point, used to make heating elements in irons and toasters.

Electric Fuse

A critical safety device with a low melting point wire that melts and opens an overloaded circuit to prevent domestic electrical fires.

MCB

Miniature Circuit Breaker: An automatic electromagnetic switch that trips open when excessive current passes through a home circuit.

Short Circuit

A dangerous condition occurring when live and neutral wires touch directly due to damaged insulation, leading to massive surge current.

Textbook In-Text & Chapter Exercises

Step-by-step model solutions for all NCERT exercises.

Q1: An electric compass needle is placed near a wire. What happens when the switch in the circuit is closed? Explain why. NCERT Core

Topper Explanation:

  • Observation: The magnetic compass needle immediately deflects from its normal north-south position.
  • Scientific Reason: When electric current flows through the wire, it creates a magnetic field around itself (Oersted effect). This artificial magnetic field interacts with the magnetic field of the permanent compass needle, exerting a torque that causes it to swing and point along the resultant magnetic direction.
Q2: Describe how you would construct a simple working electromagnet using common classroom materials. Activity Procedure
  1. Take an iron nail about 8 to 10 cm long and about 1 meter of insulated copper wire.
  2. Wind the insulated copper wire tightly around the iron nail in close loops, leaving 10 cm of free wire at both ends.
  3. Scrape the insulating enamel off both wire ends and connect one end to a switch and an electric cell.
  4. Bring small steel paper clips near the tip of the iron nail and close the switch.
  5. Result: The paper clips cling firmly to the nail tip! When the switch is opened, the clips immediately fall off, confirming the temporary nature of the electromagnet.
Q3: Why is a soft iron core preferred over a steel rod when making an electromagnet for an electric bell? Material Physics
  • Soft Iron Core: Magnetizes quickly when current is switched on, and crucially, loses all magnetism instantly when current is cut off. This allows the electric bell's spring to pull the armature back immediately to re-contact the screw.
  • Steel Rod: Has high magnetic retentivity. Once magnetized, steel remains permanently magnetic even after the circuit opens. If used in an electric bell, the hammer would stick permanently to the gong and stop ringing after a single strike!
Q4: Why does an electric room heater element glow bright red while the connecting cord remains completely cool? Joule Heating
  • Heat generated in a conductor is directly proportional to its electrical resistance for a given current ($H \propto R$).
  • The heating element is made of Nichrome, which has extremely high electrical resistance and a thin cross-section. The heavy collision of electrons generates massive heat, causing the wire to glow incandescent red at nearly 800°C.
  • The connecting cord is made of thick copper wire, which has extremely low electrical resistance. Negligible heat is produced, keeping the cord safely cool to touch.
Q5: Can an ordinary copper wire be used as an electric fuse? Give reasons. Safety Standard
  • Strictly NO! Copper wire must NEVER be used as a replacement fuse wire.
  • Reason: Copper has a very high melting point (approx. 1085°C) and low electrical resistance. During a dangerous electrical surge or short-circuit, a copper wire will not melt. It will allow massive current to surge through the house, melting the appliance circuits and sparking destructive wall fires!
  • A genuine fuse wire must always be made of an alloy with a very low melting point (such as tin-lead alloy) that safely melts and breaks the circuit.

Exam Traps & Key Takeaways

Essential revision pearls for full marks in physics questions.

Common Student Traps to Avoid:
  • Trap 1: Confusing Fuse Wire and Heating Element: A Fuse wire MUST have a low melting point so it melts fast. A Heating element (Nichrome) MUST have a high melting point so it never melts when glowing!
  • Trap 2: Saying electromagnets use Steel: Permanent magnets use steel; electromagnets MUST use soft iron for instant on/off response.
  • Trap 3: Forgetting the direction of compass deflection: Reversing battery polarity reverses the direction of compass needle deflection.
Final 1-Minute Master Checklist:
• Oersted (1820): Current through wire creates magnetic field.
• Electromagnet: Insulated coil + soft iron core; on/off control.
• Electric Bell: Armature pulled → hammer hits gong → contact screw breaks circuit → spring resets.
• Heating Effect: High resistance Nichrome wire converts electric energy to heat.
• Safety Fuse: Low melting point wire protects from overload and short circuits.
