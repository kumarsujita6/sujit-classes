2.1 The True Meaning of "Percent" & The Great Disguise

Imagine you go to a market during the Durga Puja festival and see a big colorful banner: “Mega Clearance Sale — Up to 50% Off!”. Later that evening, your sister proudly tells the family: “I scored 83% in my Grade 8 board exams!”

What do these percentages actually mean? The word percent comes directly from two ancient Latin words: per (meaning "for each" or "out of") and centum (meaning "one hundred"). Therefore, percent literally means "out of one hundred".

Formal Definition: Percentage as a Fraction

A percentage is simply a fraction whose denominator is fixed at 100. It is denoted by the universal symbol ‘%’ (pronounced per cent).

x% = x / 100 = 0.01 × x

When you say 50%, you are secretly saying 50/100 (which simplifies to 1/2). When you say 83%, you are secretly saying 83/100 (or 0.83). The percentage sign ‘%’ is just a festive mask or disguise worn by a fraction with denominator 100!

Why Do We Need Percentages? Why Not Just Keep Using Fractions?

Let us look at a real puzzle from a biscuit-making bakery:

  • Variety 1: Sugar makes up 9 / 34 of the total weight.
  • Variety 2: Sugar makes up 13 / 45 of the total weight.

Which variety is sweeter? At a glance, nobody can easily tell! The denominators 34 and 45 are completely different. To compare them as fractions, you would have to find the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of 34 and 45 (which is 1530!), multiply large numbers, and spend five minutes doing tedious long division.

Now, let us put them in their percentage disguise:

  • Variety 1: (9 ÷ 34) × 100 = 26.47%
  • Variety 2: (13 ÷ 45) × 100 = 28.88%

Immediately, within one second, any school child can see that Variety 2 is sweeter because 28.88 is greater than 26.47! Percentages create a level playing field by scaling every fraction to a common baseline of 100.

Why Choose 100? The Proportional Metric Scale

Could we choose a denominator of 10, 50, 1000, or 43 instead? In theory, yes! But 100 is magical for four specific reasons:

  1. Decimal Harmony: Our entire numbering system is Base 10. The number 100 is 10², which fits decimals like a glove: 31% = 31/100 = 0.31. You just slide the decimal point two places!
  2. The "Sweet Spot" of Granularity: "Per 10" (per decem) is too small to show fine differences (9/34 would be 2.6 out of 10). "Per 1,000" (per mille, ‰) is used in ocean salinity and birth rates, but numbers become unnecessarily large for shopping. 100 is large enough to give fine detail, yet small enough to visualize mentally!

2.2 The FDP Rosetta Stone: Converting Fractions, Decimals, and Percentages

Fractions, decimals, and percentages are three different dialects of the very same mathematical language. A master student must be able to switch between them instantly.

Three Equivalent Methods to Turn a Fraction into a Percentage

Suppose Surya wants to save 3 / 5 of his prize money to buy canvas boards. What percentage is he saving?

  1. Method 1: Equivalent Fraction with Denominator 100:
    We find what number multiplies 5 to make 100. Since 100 ÷ 5 = 20, we multiply both numerator and denominator by 20:
    (3 × 20) / (5 × 20) = 60 / 100 = 60%.
  2. Method 2: Unitary Method (Step-by-Step Scaling):
    Out of 5 parts, Surya saves 3 parts.
    Out of 1 part, he saves 3 / 5 parts.
    Out of 100 parts, he saves (3 / 5) × 100 = 3 × 20 = 60%.
  3. Method 3: Direct 100% Multiplication:
    Since 100% equals 100/100 = 1, multiplying any number by 100% does not change its value at all!
    (3 / 5) × 100% = (300 / 5)% = 60%.
Handling Repeating / Non-Terminating Decimals

Some fractions do not divide cleanly into terminating decimals because their denominators contain prime factors other than 2 or 5:

  • 1 / 3 = 0.3333... = 33.33% = 33 ⅓%
  • 2 / 3 = 0.6666... = 66.67% = 66 ⅔%
  • 1 / 6 = 0.1666... = 16.67% = 16 ⅔%
  • 5 / 11 = 0.4545... = 45.45% = 45 ⁵/₁₁%

In Grade 8 exams, you can write the answer either rounded to two decimal places (e.g., 33.33%) or as a mixed fraction percentage (33 ⅓%).

2.3 Finding Percentage of a Quantity & Free-Hand Mental Toolkit

When someone asks you: “What is 25% of 120 grams?”, how should you think?

Mathematically, the word “of” in mathematics always means multiplication (×).

y% of Q = (y / 100) × Q = 0.01 × y × Q

Therefore, 25% of 120 g = (25/100) × 120 = (1/4) × 120 = 30 grams.

The Free-Hand Mental Math Toolkit (No Pen & Paper Needed!)

To be lightning fast in exams and daily life, master these mental benchmark anchors:

10% Anchor (Slide 1 place left):
To find 10% of any number, just divide by 10 (shift decimal point 1 place left).
Example: 10% of 280 = 28. 10% of 35 = 3.5.
1% Anchor (Slide 2 places left):
To find 1% of any number, just divide by 100 (shift decimal point 2 places left).
Example: 1% of 280 = 2.8. 1% of 35 = 0.35.
5% Benchmark (Half of 10%):
Find 10% first, then take half!
Example: For 80: 10% is 8, so 5% is 4.
20% Benchmark (Double of 10%):
Find 10% first, then double it!
Example: For 80: 10% is 8, so 20% is 8 × 2 = 16.
25% Benchmark (Quarter / Half of Half):
Divide the number by 4 (or halve it, then halve again).
Example: 25% of 80 = 80 ÷ 4 = 20.
15% Benchmark (10% + 5% Combo):
Take 10% and add 5% to it!
Example: For 80: 10% is 8, 5% is 4. So 15% is 8 + 4 = 12!

2.4 The Commutative Property: x% of y = y% of x

Here is one of the most astonishing and elegant secrets in all of arithmetic:

Algebraic Proof of Commutativity

Let x and y be any two numbers. Then:

x% of y = (x / 100) × y = (x × y) / 100

Because standard multiplication of numbers is commutative (x × y = y × x), we can rewrite the numerator:

(x × y) / 100 = (y × x) / 100 = (y / 100) × x = y% of x.

Conclusion: Calculating x% of y gives the exact same result as calculating y% of x!

Why is this useful? Because sometimes one calculation looks terrifying, but its reversed version is child's play!

  • Question: What is 16% of 50?
  • The Hard Way: 16 × 50 ÷ 100 = 800 ÷ 100 = 8 (requires scratch paper).
  • The Genius Commutative Way: Flip it! 16% of 50 = 50% of 16. And 50% of 16 is just half of 16, which is 8! Done mentally in under 1 second!
  • Another Example: What is 4% of 75? Flip it to 75% of 4! Three-quarters of 4 is simply 3!

2.5 Multipliers & Percentages Greater Than 100%

Can a percentage ever exceed 100%? Yes, absolutely!

When you compare a part of a single pizza to the whole pizza, the fraction cannot exceed 1 (you cannot eat 5 slices out of 4). But when you compare growth, production targets, or harvests over time, quantities can multiply many times over!

  • 100% of target: Multiplier is 1.0 (Target achieved exactly).
  • 120% of target: Multiplier is 1.2 (Exceeded target by 20%).
  • 150% of target: Multiplier is 1.5 (Target achieved plus half as much again).
  • 200% of target: Multiplier is 2.0 (Achieved exactly twice or double the target).
  • 250% of harvest: Multiplier is 2.5 (A farmer harvesting 260 kg last year who harvests 650 kg this year has achieved (650 / 260) × 100 = 250% of last year's yield).

2.6 Percentage Change (Increase and Decrease) & KYC

In economics, science, and everyday life, we constantly measure how things change. Did petrol prices go up? Did theater footfalls go down after COVID?

The Fundamental Law of Percentage Change

Percentage change always measures the absolute change relative to the original starting value:

Percentage Increase = (Increase in Value / Original Starting Value) × 100%
Percentage Decrease = (Decrease in Value / Original Starting Value) × 100%

Critical Base Alert: Always Divide by the ORIGINAL Value!

Students frequently make the fatal mistake of dividing by the new final value instead of the original value!

  • Example 1: Tomato price rises from ₹30 to ₹42. The change is ₹12. The percentage increase is (12 / 30) × 100 = 40% (NOT 12/42!).
  • Example 2: Theater footfall drops from 160 to 100. The drop is 60 people. The percentage decrease is (60 / 160) × 100 = 37.5% (NOT 60/100!).

2.7 Commercial Arithmetic: Cost Price, Profit, Loss, Discount & GST

Commerce is the heartbeat of society. Let us understand the journey of a product from the factory to your hands:

Key Commercial Vocabulary & Equations

  • Cost Price (CP): The total expenditure incurred to acquire or manufacture the article.
  • Overhead Expenses (OH): Additional costs incurred after purchasing, such as freight transportation, labor charges, repairs, or packaging. Effective CP = Purchase Price + Overheads.
  • Selling Price (SP): The price at which the article is finally sold to the customer.
  • Profit (Gain): Occurs when SP > CP. Profit = SP - CP.
  • Loss: Occurs when SP < CP. Loss = CP - SP.
  • Profit % or Loss %: ALWAYS calculated with respect to Cost Price (CP):
    Profit% = (Profit / CP) × 100%   |   Loss% = (Loss / CP) × 100%
  • Marked Price (MP) / List Price: The printed price tag on an article before any discount is applied.
  • Discount: The price reduction offered on the Marked Price: Discount = MP - SP.
  • Discount %: ALWAYS calculated with respect to Marked Price (MP):
    Discount% = (Discount / MP) × 100%   ⇒   SP = MP × (1 - d/100)
  • Goods and Services Tax (GST): An indirect government tax charged on the actual selling price of goods and services:
    GST Amount = (GST% / 100) × SP   ⇒   Final Bill = SP × (1 + GST%/100)

2.8 Capital Growth: The Mechanics of Simple vs Compound Interest

When you deposit money in a bank, the bank uses your money to issue loans to businesses and homeowners. In return for using your money, the bank pays you extra money called Interest.

  • Principal (P): The initial amount of money deposited or borrowed.
  • Rate of Interest (R): The percentage paid per year (per annum, abbreviated as p.a.).
  • Time Period (T or t): The duration for which the money remains invested.
Option 1: Simple Interest (Linear Non-Compounding Growth)

In Simple Interest (SI), the interest earned each year is paid out or kept separate. The principal amount remains fixed at P for the entire duration!

SI = (P × R × T) / 100   |   Total Amount A = P + SI = P × (1 + (R × T) / 100)

Example: If you deposit ₹6,000 at 10% p.a. for 3 years: Each year you earn 10% of ₹6,000 = ₹600. Over 3 years, total SI = ₹600 × 3 = ₹1,800. Total amount received = ₹6,000 + ₹1,800 = ₹7,800.

Option 2: Compound Interest (The "Interest on Interest" Engine)

In Compound Interest (CI), the interest earned at the end of each year is NOT paid out—it is added back to the principal! Therefore, in the next year, you earn interest on both your original principal AND on the previous interest.

Annual Multiplier m = (1 + R / 100)
Amount after t years: A = P × (1 + R / 100)ᵗ   |   CI = A - P

Watch the magic on ₹6,000 at 10% p.a.:

  • Year 1: Interest = 10% of ₹6,000 = ₹600. New Principal = ₹6,000 + ₹600 = ₹6,600 (= ₹6,000 × 1.10).
  • Year 2: Interest = 10% of ₹6,600 = ₹660! (Notice the extra ₹60!). New Principal = ₹6,600 + ₹660 = ₹7,260 (= ₹6,000 × 1.10²).
  • Year 3: Interest = 10% of ₹7,260 = ₹726! (Notice the extra ₹126!). Final Amount = ₹7,260 + ₹726 = ₹7,986 (= ₹6,000 × 1.10³).

Total Compound Interest = ₹7,986 - ₹6,000 = ₹1,986. Compared to Simple Interest (₹1,800), compounding yielded an extra ₹186 for free because of interest earning interest!

2.9 Depreciation & Exponential Decay

Just as investments can grow by compounding, physical assets like vehicles, smartphones, machines, and televisions lose value over time due to wear, tear, and technological obsolescence. This reduction in value is called Depreciation.

The Depreciation Formula

If an article with initial purchase value P depreciates at an annual rate of R% per year, its depreciated book value A after n years is given by:

A = P × (1 - R / 100)ⁿ

Example: A TV bought for ₹21,000 depreciates by 5% in its first year. Its value after 1 year is ₹21,000 × (1 - 0.05) = ₹21,000 × 0.95 = ₹19,950.

2.10 Deceptive Percentages & The Non-Additivity Fallacy

Percentages can play mind tricks on unsuspecting shoppers and business owners. The NCERT textbook highlights two monumental psychological traps:

Trap 1: The Successive Discount Illusion (Bakery Cakely vs Cakify)

Bakery Cakely advertises: “30% + 20% Discount on All Cakes!”
Bakery Cakify advertises: “Flat 50% Discount on All Cakes!”

Many students naively assume 30% + 20% = 50%, so both shops must charge the same! This is completely false!

  • Suppose a cake is marked at ₹200.
  • At Cakify (Flat 50%): Discount = 50% of ₹200 = ₹100. Price you pay = ₹100.
  • At Cakely (30% + 20%): First discount = 30% of ₹200 = ₹60, bringing price to ₹140. Now the second 20% discount applies only on the remaining ₹140: 20% of ₹140 = ₹28! Final price you pay = ₹140 - ₹28 = ₹112!

Cakify is cheaper! A successive discount of 30% and 20% is equivalent to a single net discount of 1 - (0.70 × 0.80) = 1 - 0.56 = 44%, NOT 50%!

Trap 2: Surbhi's Mishap (The Markup & Discount Asymmetry)

Surbhi bought cookware from a wholesaler for cost price x. She marked it up with a 50% profit margin, setting the price to 1.5x. Later, to clear unsold stock without loss, she offered a 50% discount, believing she would break even.

What actually happened?
A 50% discount on 1.5x is 0.50 × 1.5x = 0.75x!
She sold goods that cost her x for only 0.75x, suffering a massive 25% net loss!

The Mathematical Truth: To reverse a 50% markup and return to original cost price x, the required discount is: (1.5x - x) / 1.5x = 0.5x / 1.5x = 1/3 = 33.33%!

Part 6.1: Five Common Exam Hall Blunders & Fixes

Blunder 1: Dividing by the New Value Instead of the Original Base

When tomato prices rise from ₹30 to ₹42, students often write: (12 / 42) × 100 = 28.57%.

WRONG APPROACH Dividing the price change by the new final price (₹42). This measures change against the future, which is mathematically invalid in percentage change.
CORRECT METHOD Always divide by the ORIGINAL starting price (₹30):
Percentage Increase = (12 / 30) × 100% = 40%.
Blunder 2: The Successive Discount Addition Fallacy

Assuming that two successive discounts of 30% and 20% add up to a flat 50% discount.

WRONG APPROACH Adding percentages directly: 30% + 20% = 50%. Successive discounts operate on different baselines and cannot be summed arithmetically!
CORRECT METHOD Multiply the remaining price fractions:
Net Multiplier = 0.70 × 0.80 = 0.56.
Net discount = (1 - 0.56) × 100% = 44% (NOT 50%!).

Blunder 3: The Surbhi Reversal Delusion (Equal Markup and Discount)

Believing that marking up by X% and then discounting by X% brings you back to the exact starting cost price.

WRONG APPROACH Thinking Cost + 50% - 50% = Cost. This ignores the fact that 50% of the larger marked-up amount is much greater than 50% of the original cost!
CORRECT METHOD A 50% markup gives 1.50x. A 50% discount gives 1.50x × 0.50 = 0.75x, creating a 25% net loss!
To break even, the discount needed is: 0.5x / 1.5x = 33.33%.
Blunder 4: Calculating Profit or Loss on Selling Price Instead of Cost Price

When an article bought for ₹300 is sold for ₹400 (Profit = ₹100), writing Profit% = (100 / 400) × 100 = 25%.

WRONG APPROACH Dividing profit by the Selling Price (₹400). This calculates the retailer's margin on sales, NOT the mathematical profit percentage prescribed in CBSE/NCERT syllabus!
CORRECT METHOD Always divide by Cost Price (₹300):
Profit% = (100 / 300) × 100% = 33.33%.
Blunder 5: Adding Non-Homogeneous Percentage Literacy Rates Across Genders

In the NSS Survey graph (NCERT Page 30), adding Male literacy (37%) and Female literacy (26%) to claim that 63% of young people use computers.

WRONG APPROACH Adding percentage rates from two distinct subgroups: 37% + 26% = 63%. Percentages are rates, not raw counts!
CORRECT METHOD Take the weighted average across both populations:
Overall Rate ≈ (37% + 26%) / 2 = 31.5% (assuming roughly equal gender numbers).

Part 6.2: 15 Rapid Revision Memory Anchors

1

"Per Centum" Literal Meaning: Percent literally means parts out of 100. The sign ‘%’ represents a fraction with denominator 100: p% = p / 100.

2

Three Conversion Routes: Convert fraction to percent by (a) finding equivalent denominator 100, (b) unitary scaling, or (c) multiplying by 100%.

3

Decimals to Percentages: Shift the decimal point exactly two places to the right: 0.375 = 37.5%. For percent to decimal, shift two places left.

4

Commutative Magic: x% of y = y% of x. Always flip cumbersome percentage calculations to their easy counterparts (e.g., 14% of 50 = 50% of 14 = 7!).

5

The Mental Anchors: 10% = divide by 10; 1% = divide by 100; 5% = half of 10%; 20% = double of 10%; 25% = divide by 4; 15% = 10% + 5%.

6

Multipliers > 1.0: Percentages above 100% indicate expansion: 120% = 1.20×; 150% = 1.50×; 200% = double (2.0×); 250% = 2.50×.

7

Percentage Change Base: Always divide absolute change by the original starting quantity: (Change / Original) × 100%.

8

Effective Cost Price: Overhead expenses (transportation, labor, packaging, repairs) must ALWAYS be added to the purchase price before computing profit or loss.

9

Profit/Loss Baseline: Profit and Loss percentages are strictly calculated on Cost Price (CP): Profit% = (Profit / CP) × 100%.

10

Discount Baseline: Trade discount is strictly calculated on Marked Price (MP): Discount% = (Discount / MP) × 100%; SP = MP × (1 - d/100).

11

GST Billing: Goods and Services Tax is added to the net selling price after all discounts are subtracted: Bill = SP × (1 + GST/100).

12

Successive Discount Rule: Two discounts d_1% and d_2% give net discount: [1 - (1 - d₁/100)(1 - d₂/100)] × 100%. They NEVER add directly.

13

Simple Interest (SI): Linear growth where principal remains constant: SI = (P × R × T) / 100; Amount = P + SI.

14

Compound Interest (CI): Geometric growth where each year's interest is added to the principal: A = P × (1 + R/100)ᵗ. Compounding always surpasses SI over time.

15

Depreciation Law: Physical assets (cars, electronics) lose value via exponential decay: A = P × (1 - R/100)ⁿ. Multiplier is strictly less than 1.0.
