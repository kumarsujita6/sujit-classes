The Five Pedagogical Pillars of Chapter 7

Mastering proportional reasoning requires understanding five interrelated core pillars that distinguish genuine multiplicative thinking from simple mechanical arithmetic:

Pillar 1: Multiplicative Invariance

When dimensions change proportionally, they change by the same multiplying factor. Adding or subtracting equal amounts destroys the ratio and distorts the shape.

Pillar 2: Ratio as a Pure Number

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind. Once written in standard units, the units cancel out: a ratio is completely dimensionless and reduced to lowest terms via HCF.

Pillar 3: The Cross-Product Principle

Four numbers a, b, c, d are in proportion (a : b :: c : d) if and only if the product of extremes equals the product of means: ad = bc.

Pillar 4: Āryabhaṭa’s Trairāśika

Given three terms—the base measure (pramāṇa), the result (phala), and the inquiry (ichchhā)—the unknown yield (ichchhāphala) is calculated in one clean step: (phala × ichchhā) / pramāṇa.

Pillar 5: Partitioning the Whole (m : n)

Dividing a whole quantity x into parts proportional to m : n requires dividing x into (m + n) equal units, then assigning m and n units respectively.

7.1 Observing Similarity in Change: Multiplicative vs Additive Scaling

In everyday life, we frequently resize digital photos on smartphones and computers. When an image is resized correctly, the subject looks natural—whether made larger or smaller. But when resized incorrectly, the subject looks unnaturally stretched, squashed, or fatter.

The Tiger Image Experiment (Textbook Case Study)

Consider five digital images of a tiger analyzed in the NCERT textbook:

  • Image A (Original Reference): Width = 60 mm, Height = 40 mm. (Width-to-height ratio = 60 : 40 = 3 : 2).
  • Image C (Half Scale): Width = 30 mm, Height = 20 mm. Both dimensions multiplied by 1/2. Ratio = 30 : 20 = 3 : 2. Image looks perfectly similar!
  • Image D (1.5× Scale): Width = 90 mm, Height = 60 mm. Both dimensions multiplied by 3/2. Ratio = 90 : 60 = 3 : 2. Image looks perfectly similar!
  • Image B (Additive Mistake): Width = 40 mm, Height = 20 mm. Both dimensions were reduced by subtracting 20 mm (60 − 20 = 40; 40 − 20 = 20). Here ratio = 40 : 20 = 2 : 1 ≠ 3 : 2. The tiger appears unnaturally elongated and skinny!
  • Image E (Square Crop Distortion): Width = 60 mm, Height = 60 mm. Width changed by factor 1, height multiplied by 1.5. Ratio = 60 : 60 = 1 : 1 ≠ 3 : 2. The tiger looks squashed and compressed!

7.2 Ratios and Their Mathematical Anatomy

In mathematics, a ratio is an expression that compares two quantities of the same kind by division. When we write a : b (read as "a is to b"), we state that:

"For every a units of the first quantity, there are exactly b units of the second quantity."

A ratio has three fundamental structural properties that every student must remember:

  1. The Terms: In the ratio a : b, a is the antecedent (first term) and b is the consequent (second term).
  2. Order Matters Strictly: The ratio a : b is not the same as b : a unless a = b. For instance, in Image A, the ratio of width to height is 60 : 40 = 3 : 2, whereas the ratio of height to width is 40 : 60 = 2 : 3.
  3. Dimensionless Property: Both quantities must be expressed in the exact same units before forming the ratio. Once expressed in identical units, the units cancel out completely: a ratio is a pure, unitless number.

7.3 Ratios in Their Simplest Form

Just like fractions, ratios can be reduced to their lowest terms. A ratio a : b is in its simplest form (or lowest terms) when a and b are co-prime positive integers—meaning their Highest Common Factor (HCF) is 1.

The HCF Reduction Algorithm

To reduce any ratio a : b to simplest form:

Simplest Ratio = (a ÷ HCF(a, b)) : (b ÷ HCF(a, b))

Example: Image A has width 60 mm and height 40 mm.
HCF(60, 40) = 20.
Dividing both terms by 20 gives: (60 ÷ 20) : (40 ÷ 20) = 3 : 2.

7.4 Proportionality & Āryabhaṭa’s Rule of Three (Trairāśika)

When two ratios are identical in their simplest forms, we say that the two ratios are in proportion, or that they are proportional. We use the four-dot symbol :: to represent proportionality:

a : b :: c : d   ⟺   a / b = c / d

In the proportion a : b :: c : d:

  • a and d are located at the outer ends and are called the Extremes (ପ୍ରାନ୍ତପଦ).
  • b and c are located in the middle and are called the Means (ମଧ୍ୟପଦ).
Formal Proof of Cross-Multiplication (Textbook Derivation, p. 168)

Let a : b and c : d be two proportional ratios. For these ratios to be equivalent, c must equal a scaled by some non-zero factor f, and d must equal b scaled by the exact same factor f:

c = f · a    ...(Equation 1)
d = f · b    ...(Equation 2)

Solving each equation for the common factor f:

f = c / a   and   f = d / b

Equating both expressions for f:

c / a = d / b

Multiplying both sides of the equation by the non-zero product ab:

ab × (c / a) = ab × (d / b)
bc = ad   ⟹   ad = bc

Conclusion: The product of extremes equals the product of means (ad = bc). Hence, the unknown fourth quantity is directly given by: d = (bc) / a.

English: "Multiply the phala by the ichchhā, and divide the product by the pramāṇa; the quotient is called ichchhāphala." ichchhāphala = (phala × ichchhā) / pramāṇa Modern Algebraic Equivalence: a / b = c / d   ⟹   a · d = b · c • Product of Extremes = Product of Means • Solve for unknown d: d = (b · c) / a Verified: pramāṇa × yield = fruit × inquiry!

7.5 Sharing, but Not Equally! (Dividing a Whole in Ratio m : n)

In business partnerships, construction mixtures, legal settlements, and culinary recipes, quantities are rarely split into equal halves (1 : 1). Instead, a total whole x must be partitioned between two or more recipients in a specified ratio m : n.

The Unit-Block Partitioning Method

To divide any total quantity x in the ratio m : n:

  1. Step 1: Calculate Total Ratio Units: Total equal parts = m + n.
  2. Step 2: Calculate the Value of 1 Unit Block: Unit Block Size = x / (m + n).
  3. Step 3: Assign Proportional Shares:
    First Share = m × [x / (m + n)] = [m / (m + n)] × x
    Second Share = n × [x / (m + n)] = [n / (m + n)] × x
  4. Step 4: Check Sum: First Share + Second Share = x (The whole must be conserved).

7.6 Mixture Alterations & Successive Concentrations

A frequent real-world challenge involves altering an existing mixture by adding more of one ingredient. For example, adding sand to cement, water to acid, or yellow paint to green paint.

The Invariance Principle in Mixtures

When you add only one ingredient to a mixture:

  • The quantity of the unchanged ingredient remains exactly constant.
  • The total quantity and the quantity of the added ingredient both increase.
  • The ratio of ingredients changes completely, meaning the new ratio must be solved by equating the constant ingredient to its new fractional share.

7.7 Unit Conversions & Dimensional Consistency

A ratio comparison is meaningless unless both terms are measured in identical physical units. Comparing 150 minutes with 4 hours as 150 : 4 will yield an entirely incorrect, distorted answer because 1 hour contains 60 minutes!

Dimensional Homogeneity Principle

Before equating ratios or executing cross-multiplication, convert all terms representing the same physical dimension into a single common unit:

Incorrect Setup: 150 min : 90 km :: 4 hours : x km  ✘
Correct Setup: 150 min : 90 km :: 240 min : x km  ✔ (Since 4 hours = 4 × 60 = 240 min)

7.8 Recreational Mathematical Logic: The Binairo (Takuzu) Grid Puzzle

At the conclusion of Chapter 7 (p. 178), NCERT introduces Binairo (also globally known as Takuzu)—a captivating logic grid puzzle played with two symbols: horizontal dashes (―) and vertical lines (|).

The Three Invariant Rules of Binairo
  1. Equal Partition Rule (1 : 1 Ratio): Each row and each column must contain an exactly equal number of horizontal lines and vertical lines. (In a 4×4 grid, exactly 2 horizontal and 2 vertical; in a 6×6 grid, exactly 3 horizontal and 3 vertical).
  2. No Three-in-a-Row Rule: You cannot have more than two identical symbols consecutively adjacent in any row or column. (Patterns like ― ― ― or | | | are strictly forbidden).
  3. Unique Line Rule: Every completed row must be completely unique, and every completed column must be completely unique. No two rows or two columns can be identical.

NCERT Example 1 (p. 162): Checking Proportionality of 3 : 4 and 72 : 96

Problem Statement: Are the ratios 3 : 4 and 72 : 96 proportional?

Step 1: Given Data First ratio = 3 : 4
Second ratio = 72 : 96
Step 2: Mathematical Principle Two ratios are proportional if and only if they reduce to the exact same simplest form, or if their cross-products are equal (ad = bc).
Step 3: Setup & Simplification Let us find the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of 72 and 96:
72 = 24 × 3
96 = 24 × 4
HCF(72, 96) = 24.
Step 4: Calculation & Cross-Product Verification Dividing numerator and denominator by 24:
72 / 96 = (72 ÷ 24) / (96 ÷ 24) = 3 / 4.
Cross-product check:
3 × 96 = 288
4 × 72 = 288
Since 3 × 96 = 4 × 72 = 288, the cross-products are identical.
Step 5: Final Conclusion Yes, the ratios 3 : 4 and 72 : 96 are proportional. We can write: 3 : 4 :: 72 : 96.
NCERT Example 2 (p. 162): Kesang's Lemonade Sugar Scaling

Problem Statement: Kesang wanted to make lemonade for a celebration. She found that for 6 glasses of lemonade, she needed 10 spoons of sugar. If she needs to make 18 glasses of lemonade with the same sweetness, how many spoons of sugar should she use?

Step 1: Given Data Initial lemonade = 6 glasses
Sugar used = 10 spoons
Target lemonade = 18 glasses
Required: Spoons of sugar (x) for 18 glasses while maintaining identical sweetness.
Step 2: Mathematical Principle For identical sweetness, the concentration (ratio of glasses to sugar spoons) must remain constant (Direct Proportionality: a : b :: c : d).
Step 3: Proportional Setup 6 : 10 :: 18 : x  ⟺  6 / 10 = 18 / x
Step 4: Calculation via Scaling Factor & Cross-Multiplication Method A (Multiplicative Factor):
The number of glasses increases by a factor of: 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
Therefore, the spoons of sugar must also multiply by 3:
x = 10 × 3 = 30 spoons.

Method B (Cross-Multiplication):
6 · x = 10 · 18
6x = 180
x = 180 ÷ 6 = 30 spoons.
Step 5: Final Conclusion Kesang should use 30 spoons of sugar to make 18 glasses of lemonade of the same sweetness.
NCERT Example 3 (p. 163): Nitin and Hari Compound Wall Construction

Problem Statement: Nitin and Hari were constructing a compound wall around their house. Nitin was building the longer side, 60 ft in length, and Hari was building the shorter side, 40 ft in length. Nitin used 3 bags of cement, while Hari used 2 bags of cement. Nitin thought Hari’s wall would be stronger because Hari used more cement per foot. Is Nitin right?

Step 1: Given Data Nitin's wall length = 60 ft; Cement used by Nitin = 3 bags.
Hari's wall length = 40 ft; Cement used by Hari = 2 bags.
Step 2: Mathematical Principle The strength of the wall depends on the ratio of wall length to cement bags used (or cement per foot). We must compare the simplest forms of both ratios: (Length : Cement Bags).
Step 3: Ratio Formulations Nitin's ratio = 60 ft : 3 bags
Hari's ratio = 40 ft : 2 bags
Step 4: Simplification Nitin's ratio in simplest form = (60 ÷ 3) : (3 ÷ 3) = 20 : 1 (i.e., 20 feet per bag of cement).
Hari's ratio in simplest form = (40 ÷ 2) : (2 ÷ 2) = 20 : 1 (i.e., 20 feet per bag of cement).
Both ratios are completely equal: 60 : 3 :: 40 : 2.
Step 5: Final Conclusion No, Nitin is not right. Both walls use exactly 1 bag of cement for every 20 feet of wall. Since the ratios are strictly proportional, both walls have the exact same strength. Nitin should not worry!
NCERT Example 4 (p. 163): Teacher-to-Student Ratio Inquiry

Problem Statement: In my school, there are 5 teachers and 170 students. The ratio of teachers to students in my school is 5 : 170. Count the number of teachers and students in your school. What is the ratio in your school? Is it proportional to the one in my school?

Step 1: Given Data Reference school teachers = 5; Reference school students = 170.
Exemplar local school data (for demonstration): Teachers = 10; Students = 340.
Step 2: Mathematical Principle A teacher-student ratio is obtained by comparing teacher count to student count. Proportionality requires both simplest forms to match.
Step 3: Simplification of Reference Ratio Reference ratio = 5 : 170.
HCF(5, 170) = 5.
Simplest form = (5 ÷ 5) : (170 ÷ 5) = 1 : 34 (meaning 1 teacher for every 34 students).
Step 4: Comparison with Local School For a school with 10 teachers and 340 students:
Ratio = 10 : 340 = (10 ÷ 10) : (340 ÷ 10) = 1 : 34.
Cross-product check: 5 × 340 = 1700; 170 × 10 = 1700.
Step 5: Final Conclusion The reference ratio is 1 : 34. If your school has 10 teachers and 340 students, it is proportional (5 : 170 :: 10 : 340). If your school has 8 teachers and 200 students (1 : 25), it is not proportional because 1 : 25 ≠ 1 : 34.
NCERT Example 5 (p. 163): Blackboard Dimensions & Proportional Drawing

Problem Statement: Measure the width and height (to the nearest cm) of the blackboard in your classroom. What is the ratio of width to height? Can you draw a rectangle in your notebook whose width and height are proportional to the blackboard?

Step 1: Given / Measured Data Standard school blackboard dimensions: Width = 240 cm, Height = 120 cm.
Step 2: Mathematical Principle Any geometric drawing is similar (preserves aspect ratio) if its dimensions are scaled by a uniform multiplicative factor f.
Step 3: Determining Simplest Ratio Ratio of width to height = 240 : 120.
HCF(240, 120) = 120.
Simplest form = (240 ÷ 120) : (120 ÷ 120) = 2 : 1.
Step 4: Notebook Drawing Construction To draw a proportional rectangle in a student notebook:
Choose a scaling factor f = 1/20:
Notebook width = 240 × (1/20) = 12 cm.
Notebook height = 120 × (1/20) = 6 cm.

Law: If the linear dimensions scale by factor k (here 3/5), the areas scale by k² (here (3/5)² = 9/25). [1 Mark]

Case Study 1: Smart City Solar Photovoltaic Farm

Under the National Solar Mission, an Odisha green energy park installs high-efficiency monocrystalline solar panels. Baseline engineering tests prove that an array of 16 solar panels occupying 40 m² of land generates 6.4 kW of peak electric power under standard sunlight.

Question 1.1: Calculate the land area required to install 240 solar panels.
Proportion Setup Panels : Area (m²) :: Panels : Area (m²)
16 : 40 :: 240 : A
Calculation 16 · A = 40 · 240
A = (40 × 240) / 16 = 40 × 15 = 600 m².

Proportion Setup Panels : Power (kW) :: Panels : Power (kW)
16 : 6.4 :: 240 : P
Calculation Scaling factor = 240 ÷ 16 = 15.
P = 6.4 kW × 15 = 96 kW.

Calculation Power scaling = 480 ÷ 6.4 = 75.
Total panels required = 16 × 75 = 1,200 panels.

To prepare Olympic athletes for intense marathon running, a sports physiologist prepares an isotonic electrolyte drink. The formula requires mixing glucose, sodium salts, and pure spring water in the mass ratio 12 : 1 : 187.

Calculation Sodium salt is 1 part in 200 total parts (Ratio 1 : 200).

PYQ 1 (CBSE 2023): Linear Proportional Equation: (3x + 5) : (5x + 3) = 2 : 3

Question: If (3x + 5) : (5x + 3) = 2 : 3, find the value of x.

Cross-Multiplication Setup (3x + 5) / (5x + 3) = 2 / 3
3 · (3x + 5) = 2 · (5x + 3)
Algebraic Expansion 9x + 15 = 10x + 6
15 − 6 = 10x − 9x
x = 9.
Verification Numerator = 3(9) + 5 = 27 + 5 = 32.
Denominator = 5(9) + 3 = 45 + 3 = 48.
Ratio = 32 / 48 = (32 ÷ 16) / (48 ÷ 16) = 2 / 3. Verified!
PYQ 2 (CBSE 2022): Vehicle Petrol Fuel Consumption Scaling

Question: A car consumes 8 litres of petrol to cover 120 km. How much petrol is required to cover a distance of 375 km?

Direct Proportionality Setup Distance (km) : Petrol (L) :: Distance (km) : Petrol (L)
120 : 8 :: 375 : p
Calculation 120 · p = 8 · 375
p = (8 × 375) / 120 = 3,000 / 120 = 25 litres.
Final Answer The car requires exactly 25 litres of petrol.
PYQ 3 (CBSE 2021): Two Numbers in Ratio 7 : 11 with Constant Addition

Question: Two numbers are in the ratio 7 : 11. If 7 is added to each of the numbers, the ratio becomes 2 : 3. Find the original numbers.

Algebraic Representation Let the common ratio multiplier be k.
First number = 7k; Second number = 11k.
Equation Setup (7k + 7) / (11k + 7) = 2 / 3
Cross-Multiplication 3 · (7k + 7) = 2 · (11k + 7)
21k + 21 = 22k + 14
21 − 14 = 22k − 21k ⟹ k = 7.
• First number = 7 × 7 = 49.
• Second number = 11 × 7 = 77.
Verification Original ratio = 49 : 77 = 7 : 11.
After adding 7: (49 + 7) : (77 + 7) = 56 : 84 = (56 ÷ 28) : (84 ÷ 28) = 2 : 3. Verified!

PYQ 5 (State Board / NCERT Exemplar): Cloth Length Purchase Scaling

Question: If 25 metres of fine cotton cloth costs ₹1,575, how many metres of the same cloth can be purchased for ₹2,016?

PYQ 6 (Most Probable Exam Problem): Water Tank Filling Time Conversion

Question: A continuous-flow municipal pipe fills a 720-litre reservoir in 2 hours 24 minutes. How long will the same pipe take to fill a 1,200-litre reservoir at the same flow rate?

Unit Conversion Convert 2 hours 24 minutes into minutes:
Time = (2 × 60) + 24 = 120 + 24 = 144 minutes.
Proportion Setup Volume (L) : Time (min) :: Volume (L) : Time (min)
720 : 144 :: 1,200 : T
Calculation 720 · T = 144 · 1,200
T = (144 × 1,200) / 720
Notice 720 ÷ 144 = 5.
T = 1,200 ÷ 5 = 240 minutes.
Convert minutes back to hours: 240 ÷ 60 = 4 hours.

The 5 Deadliest Student Blunders in Proportional Reasoning

Trap 1: The Additive Fallacy (Assuming Addition Preserves Ratios)

Why Students Fall Into It: Students intuitively believe that adding or subtracting the exact same number from both sides maintains fairness or similarity. In arithmetic, adding 5 to both sides preserves an equation (x = y ⟹ x+5 = y+5), so students wrongly assume the same holds for ratios.

The Fatal Mistake:
"A rectangle is 4 cm × 6 cm (ratio 2 : 3). If I add 2 cm to both sides, the new rectangle (6 cm × 8 cm) is proportional to the original because both increased by 2."
The Topper's Safe Path:
Original ratio = 4 : 6 = 2 : 3 (0.667).
New ratio = 6 : 8 = 3 : 4 (0.750).
Since 2/3 ≠ 3/4, the shape is distorted. Only multiplication/division by a uniform scaling factor preserves proportionality!
Trap 2: The Unit Mismatch Disaster (Comparing Unlike Quantities)

Why Students Fall Into It: Students copy numbers straight out of the question prompt without reading the accompanying physical units.

The Fatal Mistake:
Writing the ratio of 250 mL to 2 Litres as 250 : 2 = 125 : 1. This absurdly claims that 250 mL is 125 times larger than a 2-litre bottle!
The Topper's Safe Path:
Always harmonize units first!
2 L = 2 × 1,000 mL = 2,000 mL.
Ratio = 250 : 2000 = (250 ÷ 250) : (2000 ÷ 250) = 1 : 8.
Trap 3: The Order Inversion Blunder (Swapping Antecedent & Consequent)

Why Students Fall Into It: In casual English, people say "the ratio of boys and girls" or "girls and boys" interchangeably. In mathematics, order is strictly sacred!

The Fatal Mistake:
A class has 15 girls and 20 boys. The question asks for "the ratio of boys to girls", but the student writes 15 : 20 = 3 : 4.
The Topper's Safe Path:
The quantity mentioned first ("boys") must be the antecedent (first term):
Boys : Girls = 20 : 15 = 4 : 3.
Trap 4: The Inverse Proportion Blindspot (Blind Rule of Three Application)

Why Students Fall Into It: Having learned Āryabhaṭa’s Rule of Three, students mechanically apply cross-multiplication to every word problem involving four numbers without checking whether quantities increase or decrease together.

The Fatal Mistake:
"If 4 workers build a wall in 6 days, then 8 workers will take: 4 : 6 :: 8 : x ⟹ x = (6 × 8) / 4 = 12 days!" (Double the workers take double the time!).
The Topper's Safe Path:
More workers mean fewer days! This is inverse proportion:
Workers × Days = Constant (4 × 6 = 24 worker-days).
For 8 workers: Time = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 days.
Trap 5: The Incomplete Whole Denominator in Partitioning (m : n)

Why Students Fall Into It: When asked to divide a quantity into the ratio 3 : 5, students mistakenly divide by 3 or by 5 instead of the sum (3 + 5 = 8).

The Fatal Mistake:
To divide ₹800 in ratio 3 : 5, calculating: First part = 800 × (3/5) = ₹480; Second part = 800 × (5/3)... Total exceeds ₹800 completely!
The Topper's Safe Path:
Total units = 3 + 5 = 8.
First share = (3 / 8) × 800 = ₹300.
Second share = (5 / 8) × 800 = ₹500.
Check: 300 + 500 = ₹800.

15 High-Yield Rapid Revision Pearls

1
Multiplicative Nature: Proportionality is strictly a multiplicative relationship (y = kx). Resizing preserves appearance if and only if all linear dimensions change by the exact same multiplier.
2
Ratio Anatomy: In the ratio a : b, a is the antecedent (first term) and b is the consequent (second term). Both must be non-zero real numbers.
3
Dimensionless Property: A ratio has no physical units. Units must be identical before forming the ratio, causing them to cancel out completely (e.g., 500 g : 2 kg = 500 g : 2000 g = 1 : 4).
4
Simplest Form: A ratio a : b is in its simplest form when HCF(a, b) = 1. Always divide both terms by their Highest Common Factor.
5
Proportion Definition: The equality of two ratios is a proportion, denoted by a : b :: c : d (read "a is to b as c is to d"), which algebraically means a / b = c / d.
6
Cross-Product Rule: In any proportion a : b :: c : d, the product of the extremes equals the product of the means: ad = bc.
7
Ancient Trairāśika: Formulated by Āryabhaṭa in 499 CE, the Rule of Three states: ichchhāphala = (phala × ichchhā) / pramāṇa.
8
Partitioning a Whole (m : n): To split quantity x into parts proportional to m : n, the shares are [m / (m + n)] · x and [n / (m + n)] · x.
9
Mixture Invariance: When one ingredient is added to an existing mixture, the quantity of the unadded ingredient remains strictly constant. Use this constant term to equate the new ratio.
10
Area Scaling Law: When linear dimensions of similar 2D figures scale by factor k, their perimeters scale by k, but their surface areas scale by k².
11
Agricultural Conversion: 1 acre = 43,560 sq. ft, and 1 hectare = 10,000 sq. m ≈ 2.471 acres.
12
Volumetric & Mass Equivalents: 1 L = 1,000 mL = 1,000 cm³. For pure water, 1 L has a mass of exactly 1 kg.
13
Temperature Is Not Direct: Conversion between Celsius and Fahrenheit is an affine linear transformation (°F = (9/5)°C + 32), not a direct proportion, because 0 °C corresponds to 32 °F.
14
Population Density: Measured as Total Population ÷ Total Land Area. A smaller city with fewer people can have a higher density if its area is sufficiently compact.
15
Binairo Grid Logic: In Takuzu / Binairo puzzles, each row and column contains an equal number of horizontal and vertical symbols (1 : 1 balance), with no more than two identical symbols adjacent.
