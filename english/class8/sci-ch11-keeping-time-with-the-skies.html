11.0 Probe & Ponder: Mysteries of the Sky

Look up into the sky. During the day, the golden Sun bathes the Earth in warm light. At night, a soft silvery Moon glows among countless twinkling stars. Since ancient times, humans across the world have looked up at these celestial bodies with wonder and curiosity. Long before watches, smartphones, and printed wall calendars were invented, the sky was our ancestors' only clock and calendar!

Probe & Ponder — Thinking Beyond Everyday Sight

The NCERT textbook invites us to ponder four intriguing everyday questions:

  1. Have you ever seen the Moon during the day? Why is it visible when the Sun is up?
    Answer: Yes! Many people mistakenly think the Moon only exists at night. In reality, the Moon is often above the horizon during broad daylight. Because the Moon is relatively close to Earth and reflects bright sunlight from its rocky surface, its brightness is high enough to be seen clearly against the daytime blue sky—especially during the first quarter and waxing gibbous phases in the afternoon, or waning crescent phases in the morning!
  2. Imagine you lived on the Moon instead of Earth. What would you mean by a day, a month, or a year?
    Answer:
    • Lunar Day: The time it takes for the Sun to rise, set, and rise again in the lunar sky. Because the Moon takes about 29.5 Earth days to rotate once relative to the Sun, one "day" on the Moon lasts approximately 29.5 Earth days (about 708 hours: roughly 14.75 days of continuous blazing daylight followed by 14.75 days of freezing night!).
    • Lunar Month: On Earth, a month is based on the Moon revolving around us. But on the Moon, looking back at Earth, you would see the Earth hanging in almost the exact same spot in the lunar sky! The Earth would go through "phases" (Full Earth, Crescent Earth) over the exact same period of 29.5 Earth days.
    • Lunar Year: The Moon travels with the Earth around the Sun. Thus, one lunar year is exactly the same as one Earth year—about 365.25 Earth days!
  3. What would happen if Earth had two moons instead of one? How would that change our night sky?
    Answer: If Earth had two moons (for example, Moon 1 orbiting at 200,000 km and Moon 2 at 400,000 km), our night sky would be spectacular! They would orbit at different speeds, creating complex overlapping phases, dual eclipses, and intricate calendar systems. Furthermore, their combined gravitational pull would produce dramatic, complicated ocean tides with multiple high and low tides each day.
  4. If we didn't have clocks or calendars, how else could we measure time?
    Answer: We would do exactly what our ancestors did for thousands of years: use natural, repeating periodic events! The rotation of Earth (rising and setting of the Sun and changing shadow lengths) measures the day; the repeating cycle of Moon phases measures the month; and the repeating cycle of seasons and shifting sunrise positions on the horizon measures the year.

Meera's Observation at Makar Sankranti

On Makar Sankranti, Meera visited Ahmedabad in Gujarat to witness the famous Patang Mahotsav (International Kite Festival). As thousands of colourful kites soared into the clear blue sky, Meera happened to look up and was surprised to spot the Moon shining distinctly in the middle of the day!

Meera had always thought that the Moon was strictly a nighttime object. She also noticed that it was not a complete circle, but a curved shape with one side bright and the other invisible. She remembered from her earlier science lessons that:

  • The Moon is a solid spherical rock and does not produce its own light (it is a non-luminous body).
  • The Moon shines exclusively by reflecting sunlight that strikes its surface.

Then why wasn't the entire circular disc of the Moon visible? For a fleeting moment, Meera wondered if it was caused by a lunar eclipse. But she immediately reasoned that eclipses are extremely rare, brief events lasting only a few hours. In contrast, the Moon changes its shape slowly and steadily every single night! What causes these predictable, graceful changes in the Moon's appearance?

11.1 How Does the Moon's Appearance Change and Why?

To understand the Moon's shifting appearance, we must observe it patiently over the course of an entire month. This is the goal of Activity 11.1 in the NCERT textbook.

Activity 11.1: Documenting the Moon Over a Month

Procedure & Scientific Methodology:

  1. Start on the morning immediately following a Full Moon day (Purnima), when spotting the Moon at sunrise in the western sky is easiest.
  2. Every day at sunrise, observe the western horizon. Draw a small circle in your observation notebook and shade the unlit portion with a pencil, leaving the illuminated portion white.
  3. Note whether the bright portion is increasing or decreasing compared to the previous day.
  4. Observe whether the Moon appears closer to or farther from the Sun in the sky compared to the day before.
  5. After about 14 to 15 days, the Moon will reach the New Moon (Amavasya) phase and will disappear entirely from view at sunrise.
  6. For the next 15 days, switch your observation time to sunset, looking towards the western and southern sky to observe the waxing cycle back to Full Moon.

11.1.1 Phases of the Moon (Shukla Paksha & Krishna Paksha)

When you analyze the data recorded in Activity 11.1, you discover a continuous, cyclical pattern that repeats every month:

Definition: Phases of the Moon

The changing visible shapes of the bright, illuminated portion of the Moon as viewed from the Earth from one day to another are called the Phases of the Moon (ଚନ୍ଦ୍ରର କଳା ବା ଅବସ୍ଥା).

The complete cycle from one Full Moon to the very next Full Moon takes approximately 29.5 days (nearly one month). This cycle is naturally divided into two equal halves of about two weeks (14 to 15 days) each:

  • 1. The Waning Period (Krishna Paksha - କୃଷ୍ଣ ପକ୍ଷ):
    Begins immediately after the Full Moon (Purnima). The bright circular disc gradually shrinks (wanes) day by day. In about 7 days (one week), it shrinks to a neat half circle (Third/Last Quarter). Over the next 7 days, it narrows into a slender crescent, until after about 14 to 15 days, it becomes completely invisible. This day of total invisibility is called the New Moon day (Amavasya - ଅମାବାସ୍ୟା).
  • 2. The Waxing Period (Shukla Paksha - ଶୁକ୍ଳ ପକ୍ଷ):
    Begins immediately after the New Moon. Day by day, a delicate sliver of light appears and grows (waxes). In about 7 days, it grows into a half circle (First Quarter). Over the next 7 days, it swells into an oval gibbous shape, until it once again blazes as a fully illuminated circle. This is the Full Moon day (Purnima - ପୂର୍ଣ୍ଣିମା).

Figure 1.2: Complete Geometry of the 8 Lunar Phases. Notice that in space, exactly half of the Moon is always sunlit (yellow). What changes from day to day is merely the fraction of that sunlit hemisphere visible from the surface of Earth!

11.1.2 Locating the Moon in the Sky & The 50-Minute Daily Shift

When you observe the Moon on successive mornings or evenings at the exact same hour, you will notice that it does not stay in the same place. It slowly changes its position relative to the Sun:

  • On Full Moon day (Purnima): The Moon is positioned almost 180° opposite the Sun. When the Sun sets in the West, the Full Moon rises in the East! When the Sun rises in the East, the Full Moon sets in the West. It stays in the sky all night long!
  • During Waning phase: On successive mornings at sunrise, the Moon is seen higher and higher above the western horizon, shifting progressively towards the East, closer to the Sun. At Third Quarter (Half Moon), the Moon is directly overhead at sunrise! A few days later, the waning crescent is seen low in the eastern sky just before sunrise.
  • During Waxing phase: The crescent Moon is seen low in the western sky just after sunset. Day by day, it appears higher in the sky at sunset. At First Quarter, the half Moon is directly overhead at sunset!
Memory Hack: When and Where to Spot the Moon

• Waxing Moon (Shukla Paksha): Easiest to spot at sunset looking towards the West or South.
• Waning Moon (Krishna Paksha): Easiest to spot at sunrise looking towards the East or South.
• Full Moon: Overhead at midnight; visible from sunset to sunrise.
• New Moon: In the sky during midday alongside the Sun, but completely invisible because its dark side faces Earth!

A Step Further: Why Does the Moon Rise ~50 Minutes Later Each Day?

Many people believe that the Moon always rises as soon as the Sun sets. This is true only on a Full Moon night! On all other days, the moonrise time changes predictably: the Moon rises about 50 minutes later every single day.

The Exact Mathematical & Geometric Reason:

  1. Earth completes one full spin (360°) on its axis in 24 hours (1,440 minutes). This means Earth rotates at:
    360° ÷ 24 hours = 15° per hour (or 1° every 4 minutes).
  2. While the Earth is spinning, the Moon is not standing still! It is actively revolving around Earth in the same counter-clockwise direction, completing one orbit in about 27.3 days.
  3. In one day (24 hours), the Moon moves forward in its orbit by:
    360° ÷ 27.3 days ≈ 13.2° per day.
  4. Therefore, after Earth completes one 360° spin (24 hours), the observer has returned to the same orientation in space, but the Moon has already travelled 13.2° ahead in its orbit!
  5. To face the Moon again and see it rise above the horizon, Earth must rotate an extra 13.2°:
    Extra Time = 13.2° × 4 minutes/degree ≈ 52.8 minutes (~50 minutes)!

This 50-minute daily delay explains why moonrise shifts progressively from evening to midnight, to early dawn, and into the middle of the afternoon!

11.1.3 Making Sense of Our Observations: Ball-and-Torch Model

Why does the lit portion change shape so gracefully? To prove that the Moon itself never changes its physical shape, NCERT presents Activity 11.2 using a simple ball and torch:

Activity 11.2: Modeling Lunar Phases with a Ball and Lamp

Setup & Roles:

  • A small soft ball mounted on a stick represents the Moon.
  • A stationary lamp or torch shone from 3 meters away in a dark room represents Sunlight.
  • Your own head represents the Earth, with your eyes representing an observer on the Earth's surface!

Step-by-Step Observations as You Spin Anti-Clockwise:

  1. Position E (Ball held towards the lamp): The illuminated side of the ball faces away from you; you only see the dark, unlit hemisphere. This is New Moon (Amavasya).
  2. Turn 45° to the left (Position F): A thin curved crescent of light appears on the right edge of the ball. This is Waxing Crescent.
  3. Turn 90° (Position G): Exactly half of the visible hemisphere facing your eyes is bright, separated by a crisp line from the dark half. This is First Quarter.
  4. Turn 135° (Position H): More than half of the visible side is bright (gibbous shape). This is Waxing Gibbous.
  5. Turn 180° (Position A, back to lamp): Your eyes look directly at the fully lit face of the ball (held slightly above your head so your shadow doesn't fall on it). This is Full Moon (Purnima)!
  6. Continuing the turn brings you through Waning Gibbous → Third Quarter → Waning Crescent, and back to New Moon!

Synchronous Rotation & Tidal Locking: The Moon's Hidden Face

One of the most fascinating facts in astronomy is that we on Earth only ever see ONE SIDE of the Moon (the near side)! The opposite side (the far side) was never seen by human eyes until spacecraft flew behind the Moon in 1959.

Why is this so? Does the Moon rotate on its axis? YES, IT DOES!

Scientific Mechanism: Synchronous Rotation (Tidal Locking)

The Moon takes exactly the same amount of time to spin once on its axis as it takes to complete one revolution around the Earth (about 27.3 days).

Because its rotation period strictly equals its orbital period, it keeps the exact same hemisphere permanently turned towards Earth!

Classroom Analogy: Walk in a circle around your friend while keeping your eyes locked onto their face. When you complete one circle, have you turned around? Yes! You faced north, west, south, and east during the walk, meaning you completed a full 360° turn. But your friend never saw your back! That is exactly what the Moon does with Earth.

Exam Trap Buster: Phases Are NOT Caused by the Earth's Shadow!

In school examinations across India, the single most common mistake made by students is writing that "phases of the Moon are caused by the shadow of the Earth falling on the Moon."

CRITICAL CONCEPT: Phases vs. Lunar Eclipses

THIS IS COMPLETELY FALSE! The Earth's shadow plays zero role in creating regular lunar phases:

  • Phases: Caused solely by our changing angle of view of the sunlit hemisphere of the Moon as it orbits Earth. The dark portion of a crescent or quarter moon is simply the Moon's own nighttime hemisphere facing away from the Sun!
  • Lunar Eclipse: Occurs ONLY when the Earth passes directly between the Sun and Moon, casting Earth's shadow onto the Moon. Eclipses can happen only on a Full Moon night, are rare, and last only 1 to 2 hours!
Why Don't We Have Eclipses Every Month? (The 5° Orbit Tilt)

If the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth every New Moon, and Earth passes between the Sun and Moon every Full Moon, why don't eclipses occur every single month?

The Reason: The plane of the Moon's orbit around the Earth is tilted by about 5° relative to the plane of the Earth's orbit around the Sun (the ecliptic plane).

Because of this 5° tilt:

  • On most New Moon days, the Moon passes slightly above or below the Sun in the sky, so its shadow misses Earth (no solar eclipse).
  • On most Full Moon days, the Moon passes slightly above or below Earth's shadow, remaining fully bathed in direct sunlight (no lunar eclipse).
  • Eclipses can occur only when a New Moon or Full Moon happens at or near the two points where the two orbital planes cross each other (called nodes).

11.2 How Did Calendars Come into Existence?

Every clock or calendar that humanity has ever constructed relies on a single fundamental scientific principle: periodic natural events. A periodic event is any physical phenomenon that repeats itself at regular, predictable intervals of time.

When ancient humans watched the sky, they discovered three great natural astronomical cycles:

  • 1. The Day: Caused by the Earth spinning once on its axis (24 hours). The Sun rises in the East, reaches its peak height at noon, sets in the West, and reappears the next morning.
  • 2. The Month: Caused by the Moon revolving once around the Earth (~29.5 days). Marked by the steady, beautiful progression of lunar phases from New Moon to Full Moon and back.
  • 3. The Year: Caused by the Earth completing one full orbit around the Sun (~365.25 days). Marked by the cyclical rhythm of nature: scorching summers, rejuvenating monsoons, and crisp winters.
Activity 11.3: Let Us Measure a Solar Day with a Gnomon!

Apparatus & Setup:

Find a level, unobstructed patch of ground bathed in direct sunlight. Firmly fix a straight, 1-meter long wooden stick or metal rod (called a gnomon - ଛାୟା ଶଙ୍କୁ) perfectly vertically into the ground.

Procedure & Observations:

  1. Begin observations at 11:00 a.m. Every minute (or every 5 minutes), mark a tiny dot on the ground at the sharp tip of the stick's shadow.
  2. Continue marking dots until around 1:10 p.m. Notice that the shadow tip traces a smooth curve on the ground!
  3. Measure the distance from the base of the stick to each marked dot. You will find that the shadow starts long at 11:00 a.m., gradually shrinks to its minimum length around midday, and then begins to lengthen again!
  4. The exact instant when the shadow is at its absolute shortest is called Local Solar Noon. At this exact moment, the Sun has reached its highest altitude in the sky and is crossing the local meridian (directly North-South line).
  5. Repeat this experiment the following day. Measure the exact time elapsed between the shortest shadow of Day 1 and the shortest shadow of Day 2.

Scientific Conclusion: The time interval between two consecutive solar noons is called a Solar Day. Averaged across the year, its duration is precisely 24 hours (1,440 minutes), termed the Mean Solar Day (ମାଧ୍ୟ ସୌର ଦିବସ).

Figure 1.5: Principle of the Solar Gnomon. When the Sun climbs to its highest elevation in the sky, it casts the shortest shadow of the day. The interval between two consecutive shortest shadows defines our 24-hour day.

11.2.1 Lunar Calendars & The 11-Day Seasonal Deficit

In early human civilizations, tracking the phases of the Moon was far easier than measuring the subtle orbital movements of the Sun. Anyone looking up at night could instantly identify whether it was New Moon, crescent, half, or full!

Our ancestors noticed that in the time it took for the seasons to cycle once, the Moon completed roughly 12 full cycles of phases. This led to the birth of Lunar Calendars (ଚାନ୍ଦ୍ର ପଞ୍ଜିକା):

  • 1 Lunar Month = ~29.5 days
  • 1 Lunar Year = 12 lunar months × 29.5 days = 354 days
The Problem with Pure Lunar Calendars

A true solar year (the cycle of seasons) takes approximately 365.25 days.

Notice the big mathematical difference:
Annual Deficit = 365.25 - 354 ≈ 11.25 days every year!

Because a pure lunar calendar is 11 days shorter than the solar year, its months rapidly lose sync with the natural seasons:

  • After 3 years, the calendar is out of sync by about 33 days (over an entire month!).
  • After 16 years, seasons completely reverse (a winter month shifts into scorching midsummer!).
  • After about 33 solar years, a month completes one full circuit through all four seasons!

Real-World Example: The Islamic Hijri calendar is a pure lunar calendar. This is why the holy month of Ramadan and the celebration of Eid-ul-Fitr move earlier by about 11 days every year in the Gregorian calendar, falling in summer during some decades and in winter during others!

11.2.2 Solar Calendars & The Science of Leap Years

While tracking the Moon was simple, agriculture demanded absolute synchronization with the seasons. Farmers needed to know exactly when monsoon rains would arrive to sow seeds, and when winter frost would set in to harvest crops. If a calendar shifted seasons by 11 days each year, agricultural planning would descend into chaos!

This vital economic and survival need led to the invention of Solar Calendars (ସୌର ପଞ୍ଜିକା). The modern international calendar used worldwide today—the Gregorian Calendar—is a solar calendar.

In a solar calendar, the year is tied strictly to the revolution of Earth around the Sun. The months are assigned arbitrary lengths of 30 or 31 days (and 28 days for February) so that all 12 months add up to 365 days.

The Exact Science of the Leap Year

Earth does not take exactly 365 days to orbit the Sun. It takes approximately 365 days, 5 hours, 48 minutes, and 46 seconds (roughly 365.2422 days, or about 365¼ days).

That extra quarter-day (about 6 hours) accumulates every single year:
4 years × ~6 hours = ~24 hours = 1 Extra Full Day!

To keep the calendar locked to the seasons, solar calendars add an extra day—February 29—every four years. This 366-day year is called a Leap Year (ଅଧିବର୍ଷ).

The Complete 3-Step Gregorian Leap Year Rule:

  1. Divisible by 4 Rule: If a year is evenly divisible by 4, it is a leap year (e.g., 2020, 2024, 2028).
  2. Century Exception (Divisible by 100): Because the extra time is slightly less than a full 6 hours (it is 5h 48m 46s), adding a day every 4 years adds about 11 minutes too much each year! Over 100 years, this over-correction accumulates to nearly a whole day. To fix this, century years divisible by 100 are NOT leap years (e.g., 1700, 1800, and 1900 had only 365 days!).
  3. 400-Year Correction: Skipping every century year lags the calendar slightly behind again. Therefore, century years that are divisible by 400 ARE leap years (e.g., 1600 and 2000 were leap years; 2400 will be a leap year).
A Step Further: Tropical Year vs. Sidereal Year

NCERT highlights two precise ways modern astronomers measure a solar year:

  • 1. Tropical Year (କ୍ରାନ୍ତିକ ବର୍ଷ - 365.2422 days):
    The time interval between two successive Spring (Vernal) Equinoxes. It measures the exact cycle of natural seasons on Earth. The Gregorian calendar is strictly based on the tropical year.
  • 2. Sidereal Year (ନାକ୍ଷତ୍ରିକ ବର୍ଷ - 365.2564 days):
    The time duration required for the Sun to return to the exact same position relative to the fixed stars (the stellar constellations rising at sunset).
  • The 20-Minute Precession Difference: The sidereal year is longer than the tropical year by about 20 minutes and 24 seconds! This minute difference is caused by the slow, graceful wobble of Earth's rotational axis—called axial precession (similar to how a spinning toy top slowly wobbles in a circle). One complete wobble takes approximately 25,800 years!

11.2.3 Luni-Solar Calendars & The Intercalary Month (Adhika Maasa)

What if a culture wants the best of both worlds—to use the natural, easy-to-spot phases of the Moon for counting daily dates (tithis) and celebrating religious festivals, yet remain firmly anchored to agricultural seasons?

This ingenious synthesis is called a Luni-Solar Calendar (ଚାନ୍ଦ୍ର-ସୌର ପଞ୍ଜିକା). It has been used across India for millennia!

How Luni-Solar Calendars Work: The Adhika Maasa (ଅଧିକ ମାସ)

As we learned, 12 lunar months equal 354 days, creating an annual deficit of 11 days compared to the 365-day solar year:

  • After Year 1: Calendar falls behind by 11 days.
  • After Year 2: Calendar falls behind by 22 days.
  • After Year 2.7 (about 32 to 33 months): The accumulated shortfall reaches ~30 days—which equals one full lunar month!

At this point, traditional Indian astronomers insert an extra 13th month into the year! This intercalary month is called Adhika Maasa (ଅଧିକ ମାସ), Malamaasa (ମଳମାସ), or Purushottama Maasa.

By inserting this extra month approximately every 2 to 3 years (specifically, 7 times in 19 years), the lunar months jump forward by 30 days and land back in perfect harmony with the solar seasons!

Amant vs. Purnimant Systems: Two Ways to Count a Month

Across different regions of India, traditional communities follow one of two conventions for starting and ending a lunar month:

Our Scientific Heritage: Ancient Indian Astronomy

Thousands of years before the invention of telescopes and modern clocks, ancient Indian skywatchers carefully monitored the heavens. Even without modern equipment, their meticulous, daily records allowed them to calculate the length of the solar year with astonishing precision!

Uttarayan and Dakshinayan: The Shifting Rising Point of the Sun

If you stand in the exact same spot outside your home every morning, you will notice that the Sun does not always rise at the exact same point on the eastern horizon:

  • On the Equinoxes (around March 21 and September 23), the Sun rises due East and sets due West. Day and night are exactly equal (12 hours each) everywhere on Earth.
  • From December 21 to June 21, the sunrise point shifts progressively towards the North of East. This apparent northward movement of the Sun is called Uttarayan (ଉତ୍ତରାୟଣ). On the Summer Solstice (around June 21), the Sun rises at its northernmost point, bringing the longest day and shortest night of the year in India.
  • From June 21 to December 21, the sunrise point retreats southward, shifting towards the South of East. This apparent southward movement of the Sun is called Dakshinayan (ଦକ୍ଷିଣାୟନ). On the Winter Solstice (around December 21), the Sun rises at its southernmost point, giving us the shortest day and longest night.
Classical Sanskrit Records: Taittirīya Saṁhitā & Sūrya Siddhānta

The ancient Vedic text Taittirīya Saṁhitā (verse 6.5.3) clearly documents this six-month solstitial cycle:

तस्मादादित्यः षण्मासा दक्षिणेनैति षडुत्तरेण ।

Translation: "Thus the Sun moves southwards for six months (Dakshinayan) and northwards for six months (Uttarayan)."

Later, the celebrated classical astronomical treatise Sūrya Siddhānta (verse 9) linked these movements directly to constellations (Rāśis):

भानोर्मकरसंक्रान्तेः षण्मासा उत्तरायणम् ।
कर्क्यादेस्तु तथैव स्यात् षण्मासा दक्षिणायनम् ॥

Translation: "From the moment the Sun enters the constellation of Capricorn (Makara), six months make up its northward progress (Uttarayan); likewise from its entrance into Cancer (Karka), six months are its southward progress (Dakshinayan)."

Why Makar Sankranti Shifted: In ancient times (~1,500 years ago), the Sun entered Capricorn on the exact day of the Winter Solstice (December 21). Because of Earth's axial precession, the sidereal calendar shifts relative to the tropical solstices by 1 day every 71 years! This is why Makar Sankranti is celebrated today on January 14 or 15, about 24 days after the true winter solstice!

11.2.4 The Indian National Calendar (Saka Calendar)

When India achieved independence in 1947, over 30 different regional calendars were being used across the country! This created immense administrative confusion for national holidays, financial accounts, and official gazettes.

In November 1952, the Government of India established the Calendar Reform Committee (CRC) under the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR). The committee was chaired by the internationally renowned Indian astrophysicist Prof. Meghnad Saha.

Key Features of the Indian National Calendar

Formally adopted on 22 March 1957 CE (corresponding to 1 Chaitra 1879 Saka Era):

  • Solar Calendar: It is a purely solar calendar tied directly to the tropical year, consisting of 365 days in a standard year and 366 days in a leap year.
  • Starting Point: The year begins on 1 Chaitra, which corresponds to March 22 in a standard Gregorian year, and March 21 in a Gregorian leap year (the day after the vernal equinox).
  • Month Lengths:
    • Month 1 (Chaitra): 30 days in a normal year; 31 days in a leap year.
    • Months 2 to 6 (Vaisakha, Jyeshtha, Ashadha, Shravana, Bhadrapada): Exactly 31 days each (coinciding with the longer summer season in the northern hemisphere!).
    • Months 7 to 12 (Ashwina, Kartika, Margashirsha, Pausha, Magha, Phalguna): Exactly 30 days each.
  • Leap Year Synchronization: The Saka calendar adds a leap day to Chaitra in the exact same years as the Gregorian calendar (whenever the Gregorian year is a leap year). To find the Saka year, subtract 78 from the Gregorian year (e.g., 2025 - 78 = 1947 Saka).
Be a Scientist: Meghnad Saha (1893–1956)

Born in Seoratali (now Bangladesh), Meghnad Saha was one of modern India's greatest astrophysicists. In 1920, he formulated the world-famous Saha Ionization Equation, which relates the ionization state of chemical elements in a star to its temperature and pressure. This equation allowed astronomers for the first time to determine the temperatures and chemical compositions of distant stars simply by analyzing their light spectra!

Saha was also a dedicated institution-builder. He founded the Saha Institute of Nuclear Physics in Kolkata and served as Member of Parliament. As chairperson of the Calendar Reform Committee, his scientific rigor gave India an accurate, uniform national solar calendar.

11.3 Are Festivals Related to Astronomical Phenomena?

Have you ever wondered why your school winter vacation or Independence Day is always on the exact same date every year, whereas Diwali, Holi, Dussehra, and Eid-ul-Fitr fall on completely different dates each year?

Why Indian Festival Dates Shift in the Gregorian Calendar

The explanation lies in the calendar system upon which the festival is calculated:

  1. 1. Festivals Based on Pure Lunar Calendars (e.g., Eid-ul-Fitr):
    Celebrated upon sighting the delicate new crescent Moon at the end of the Islamic month of Ramadan. Because a lunar year has only 354 days, Eid occurs approximately 11 days earlier in the Gregorian calendar each successive year, cycling through every season over 33 years!
  2. 2. Festivals Based on Luni-Solar Calendars (e.g., Diwali, Holi, Dussehra, Buddha Purnima):
    These festivals are tied strictly to lunar phases within designated traditional months:
    • Diwali: Celebrated on the Amavasya (New Moon) of the month of Kartika.
    • Holi: Celebrated on the Purnima (Full Moon) of the month of Phalguna.
    • Buddha Purnima: Celebrated on the Purnima of the month of Vaisakha.
    • Dussehra (Vijaya Dashami): Celebrated on the 10th day of the waxing moon (Shukla Dashami) of Ashwina.
    • Raksha Bandhan: Celebrated on the Purnima of Shravana.
    In consecutive years, these festivals shift earlier by 11 days. But whenever the shift reaches ~30 days, the calendar inserts an Adhika Maasa, causing the festival date to leap forward by a month! This keeps the festival firmly anchored inside its natural season (e.g., Diwali is always in autumn, Holi is always in spring!).
  3. 3. Festivals Based on the Solar Sidereal Calendar (e.g., Makar Sankranti, Pongal, Bihu, Pana Sankranti):
    These celebrate the Sun entering specific constellations (Sankranti). Because they are solar, they fall on almost the exact same Gregorian date every single year!
    • Makar Sankranti / Pongal / Magh Bihu: January 14 or 15 every year.
    • Maha Bishuba Pana Sankranti (Odia New Year) / Poila Boishakh / Vaisakhi / Puthandu: April 14 or 15 every year.
Positional Astronomy Centre & The Rashtriya Panchang

In India, festival dates are traditionally calculated based on the exact lunar phase (tithi) present at sunrise. Because India spans nearly 30° of longitude, the Sun rises roughly two hours earlier in Arunachal Pradesh and Odisha than in Gujarat!

On rare occasions, a tithi may change during those two hours, causing two states to calculate different festival days! To establish uniform national holidays, the Positional Astronomy Centre (PAC) of the India Meteorological Department (IMD) in Kolkata calculates celestial coordinates for a central Indian location (82.5° E longitude, near Mirzapur/Prayagraj) and officially publishes the annual Rashtriya Panchang.

Cultural Reverberations: Moon in Indian Music, Dance & Art

The beauty of the Moon has deeply enriched India's artistic heritage for centuries:

  • Classical Ragas: Melodies inspired by moonlight include Raga Chandrakauns, Chandranandan, and Shubhapantuvarali (which translates to "auspicious moon").
  • Classical Dance Mudras: In Bharatanatyam, Odissi, and Kathak, dancers use precise hand gestures such as Chandrakala (digit of the Moon) and Ardhachandran (half moon) to depict celestial serenity.
  • Indigenous Tribal Art: In Odisha's tribal Saura paintings (Idital), Warli wall murals in Maharashtra, Madhubani folk art in Bihar, and Gond paintings in Madhya Pradesh, the Sun and Moon are revered as eternal witnesses to human life.

11.4 Why Do We Launch Artificial Satellites?

Just as the Moon is Earth's only natural satellite, humans have built and launched thousands of Artificial Satellites (କୃତ୍ରିମ ଉପଗ୍ରହ) into space. An artificial satellite is an engineered machine placed into orbit around the Earth using multi-stage rockets.

Crucial Roles of Artificial Satellites in Modern Life
  1. Telecommunication & Television: Relaying live sports, mobile telephone signals, and high-speed satellite internet across continents.
  2. Weather Monitoring & Cyclone Early Warning: Tracking massive tropical cyclones building over the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea, saving hundreds of thousands of lives along coastal Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, and West Bengal!
  3. Navigation & Mapping: Supplying real-time coordinates for smartphones and vehicle maps via GPS and India's own sovereign satellite constellation, NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation).
  4. Remote Sensing & Earth Observation: ISRO's Cartosat and Resourcesat series capture hyper-detailed imagery of terrain, forest cover, soil moisture, and water bodies, accessible to citizens through ISRO's Bhuvan portal.
  5. Deep Space Science: AstroSat (India's dedicated multi-wavelength space observatory studying neutron stars and black holes), Aditya-L1 (stationed at the Sun-Earth L1 Lagrange point to study solar flares), Chandrayaan 1, 2, 3 (discovering water molecules and soft-landing on the Moon's South Pole), and Mangalyaan (Mars Orbiter Mission).
  6. Student & Youth Satellites: ISRO empowers university and school students to build and launch cubesats, including Jugnu (IIT Kanpur), InspireSat-1, and AzaadiSat (built by 750 rural schoolgirls across India!).
Activity 11.4: Spotting an Artificial Satellite in the Twilight Sky

When & Where to Look:

The best time is twilight—roughly 30 to 60 minutes after sunset or before sunrise. At this time, the ground where you stand is dark, but satellites orbiting hundreds of kilometers high are still fully illuminated by sunlight!

How to Distinguish a Satellite from an Aeroplane or Star:

  • Stars: Twinkle and stay fixed in constellations.
  • Aeroplanes: Have flashing, blinking red and green strobe lights and make audible engine noise.
  • Artificial Satellites: Appear as a steady, silent, non-blinking point of white light gliding smoothly and rapidly across the sky, taking just 2 to 5 minutes to travel from one horizon to the other!
A Step Further: The Menace of Space Debris (Space Junk)

Over six decades of space exploration, thousands of spent rocket upper stages, dead satellites, and fragments from past collisions have accumulated in orbit. This is called Space Debris (ମହାକାଶ ଆବର୍ଜନା).

Because objects in Low Earth Orbit travel at hyper-velocity (around 7.8 km/s or 28,000 km/h), even a tiny 1-cm paint fleck carries the kinetic punch of an exploding grenade! It can shatter functioning weather satellites or endanger astronauts aboard space stations. To protect our spacecraft, ISRO established the NETRA (Network for space object Tracking and Analysis) project, and international space agencies now require satellites to perform de-orbiting burns at the end of their mission to safely burn up in the upper atmosphere.

Be a Scientist: Dr. Vikram Ambalal Sarabhai (1919–1971)

Widely revered as the Father of the Indian Space Programme, Dr. Vikram Sarabhai was a visionary physicist who foresaw that space technology should not be a luxury for rich nations, but a powerful catalyst to solve real-world problems in agriculture, education, and disaster management in developing India.

In 1962, he convinced Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru to establish the Indian National Committee for Space Research (INCOSPAR), which evolved into the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) in 1969. He set up India's first rocket launching station at Thumba near Thiruvananthapuram. Today, ISRO's premier launch vehicle development centre—the Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre (VSSC)—stands proudly in his honour.

A Step Further: Ocean Tides & The Moon's 50-Minute Rhythm

If you visit the seaside beaches of Puri, Chandrabhaga, or Gopalpur in Odisha, you will observe the rhythmic rise and fall of ocean water levels—known as Tides (ଜୁଆର-ଭଟ୍ଟା):

  • High Tide (ଜୁଆର): Sea water surges inland, submerging sandy shorelines.
  • Low Tide (ଭଟ୍ଟା): Sea water recedes hundreds of meters back into the ocean.
  • The Astounding 50-Minute Connection: If a high tide occurs at 10:00 a.m. on Monday, the corresponding tide on Tuesday will arrive at 10:50 a.m. (50 minutes later)! Why? Because ocean tides are driven primarily by the gravitational pull of the Moon. As Earth rotates, the tidal bulge stays aligned with the Moon—and as we proved earlier, the Moon advances in its orbit, rising 50 minutes later each day! The ocean's heartbeat directly mirrors the Moon's orbital rhythm!

Question 8 Mathematical Proof: 37 Full Moons in 3 Solar Years (Pigeonhole Principle)
Step 1: Question Breakdown

A total of 37 full Moons happen during 3 years in a solar calendar. Show that at least two of the 37 full moons must happen during the same month of the solar calendar.

Step 2: Mathematical Theorem

Dirichlet's Pigeonhole Principle (କପୋତ କୋଠରୀ ନିୟମ): If n + 1 or more items are distributed among n pigeonholes (containers), then at least one pigeonhole must contain two or more items.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Proof
  1. Let us determine the total number of calendar months in 3 solar years:
    Total Months (Pigeonholes) = 3 years × 12 months/year = 36 months.
  2. We are given that a total of 37 full Moons (items/pigeons) occur during this exact 3-year period.
  3. Assign each full Moon to the specific calendar month in which it occurs.
  4. Assume for contradiction that no month contains more than one full Moon (i.e., every month contains at most 1 full Moon).
  5. Then the maximum possible number of full Moons that could occur in 36 months would be:
    36 months × 1 full Moon/month = 36 full Moons.
  6. However, we have 37 full Moons, and 37 > 36!
  7. By Dirichlet's Pigeonhole Principle, since the number of full Moons (37) strictly exceeds the number of calendar months (36), at least one month MUST contain at least two full Moons!
Step 4: Final Precise Conclusion

Hence proved mathematically. In any 3-year span containing 37 full moons across 36 calendar months, at least one calendar month must have two full moons (popularly known as a Blue Moon).

LAQ 1 (5M) Comprehensive Treatise on the Phases of the Moon

Question: Explain in detail the phenomenon of the phases of the Moon. Discuss: (a) Why phases occur, (b) The eight distinct phases in sequence, (c) Why we only ever see one face of the Moon, and (d) Why phases are NOT caused by the Earth's shadow.

Model Answer (CBSE Topper 5-Step Structure):

(a) Why Phases Occur (1 Mark):
The Moon is an opaque, spherical, non-luminous body that shines strictly by reflecting sunlight. At all times, the Sun illuminates exactly 50% (one hemisphere) of the Moon. As the Moon revolves around Earth once every ~29.5 days, the angle between the Sun, Earth, and Moon changes continuously. Observers on Earth see varying fractions of this illuminated hemisphere, creating the apparent changing shapes called phases of the Moon.

(b) The 8 Distinct Phases in Sequence (1.5 Marks):

  1. New Moon (Amavasya): Moon is between Earth and Sun; unlit face faces Earth (0% visible).
  2. Waxing Crescent: A thin sliver of light appears on the right edge (<50% lit).
  3. First Quarter: Half of the illuminated hemisphere is seen on the right (50% lit).
  4. Waxing Gibbous: The bright portion bulges into an egg-like shape (>50% lit).
  5. Full Moon (Purnima): Earth is between Sun and Moon; entire illuminated face is visible (100% lit).
  6. Waning Gibbous: Bright portion begins shrinking from the right (>50% lit on left).
  7. Third / Last Quarter: Half of the illuminated hemisphere is seen on the left (50% lit).
  8. Waning Crescent: A thin sliver of light remains on the left edge (<50% lit), leading back to New Moon.

(c) Why We Only Ever See One Face of the Moon (1.5 Marks):
This occurs due to Synchronous Rotation (Tidal Locking). The gravitational tidal forces exerted by Earth on the Moon over billions of years have slowed the Moon's rotation until its period of axial rotation strictly equals its period of orbital revolution around Earth (~27.3 days). Because it spins once on its axis in the exact time it circles Earth once, the same hemisphere permanently faces Earth.

(d) Why Phases Are NOT Caused by Earth's Shadow (1 Mark):
Earth's shadow plays zero role in regular moon phases! The dark portion of a crescent or quarter moon is simply the Moon's own nighttime hemisphere facing away from the Sun. Earth's shadow falls on the Moon only during a lunar eclipse, which can occur strictly on a Full Moon night, is rare, and lasts only a few hours because the Moon's orbit is tilted by 5° to the ecliptic.

LAQ 2 (5M) Comparative Analysis of Calendar Systems & Leap Mechanisms

Question: Compare Solar, Lunar, and Luni-Solar calendars. Explain: (a) The astronomical basis of each, (b) The leap year rule of the Gregorian solar calendar with century exceptions, (c) The cause and remedy of the 11-day deficit in luni-solar calendars, and (d) Give two Indian festival examples for each calendar type.

Model Answer:

(a) Astronomical Basis of the Three Systems (1.5 Marks):

  • Solar Calendar: Governed strictly by the tropical year (Earth's revolution around the Sun = 365.2422 days). Months are civil divisions (30/31 days) designed to sum to 365 days.
  • Lunar Calendar: Governed strictly by the synodic lunar cycle (Moon's revolution around Earth = 29.53 days). 12 lunar months equal 354 days.
  • Luni-Solar Calendar: Hybrid system. Days and months follow lunar phases (~29.5 days), but periodic intercalary months are added to synchronize the year with solar seasons.

(b) Gregorian Leap Year Mechanism & Century Rules (1.5 Marks):
A tropical year is ~365.2422 days (~365¼ days). The extra ~6 hours accumulate to 1 full day every 4 years.
1. Divisible by 4 Rule: Years divisible by 4 add February 29 (366 days).
2. Century Exception (Divisible by 100): Adding 1 day every 4 years adds 11 minutes too much per year. To compensate, century years divisible by 100 are NOT leap years (e.g., 1700, 1800, 1900 had 365 days).
3. 400-Year Correction: Skipping every century year over-corrects. Thus, century years divisible by 400 ARE leap years (e.g., 1600, 2000 had 366 days).

(c) The 11-Day Deficit & Adhika Maasa (1.0 Mark):
A lunar year (354 days) is 11.25 days shorter than a solar year (365.25 days). In ~2.7 years, this shortfall equals ~30 days (1 full lunar month). Luni-solar calendars insert an extra 13th month—Adhika Maasa (ମଳମାସ)—every 32 to 33 months, resetting the lunar months back into seasonal alignment.

(d) Indian Festival Examples (1.0 Mark):

  • Solar Calendar Festivals: Makar Sankranti (Jan 14/15), Maha Bishuba Pana Sankranti (April 14/15) — fixed Gregorian dates.
  • Luni-Solar Festivals: Diwali (Kartika Amavasya), Holi (Phalguna Purnima) — dates shift within a 3-week seasonal window.
  • Pure Lunar Festivals: Eid-ul-Fitr (1st crescent of Shawwal), Muharram — dates shift 11 days earlier every year across all seasons.

LAQ 3 (5M) The Indian National Calendar (Saka Calendar) & Prof. Meghnad Saha

Question: Write a comprehensive account of the Indian National Calendar. (a) Historical Background & Leadership (1.0 Mark):
At Independence, over 30 distinct regional calendars were in use across India, leading to widespread confusion in scheduling national holidays, civil administration, and meteorological records. In November 1952, the Government of India under CSIR established the Calendar Reform Committee (CRC) chaired by the renowned astrophysicist Prof. Meghnad Saha. The committee designed a scientific, uniform national calendar adopted on 22 March 1957 CE (1 Chaitra 1879 Saka).

(b) Starting Epoch & Vernal Equinox Alignment (1.0 Mark):
The Indian National Calendar uses the historical Saka Era (epoch year 0 = 78 CE; Saka year = Gregorian year - 78). It is a pure solar calendar locked to the tropical year. The civil year begins on 1 Chaitra, which coincides with March 22 in normal Gregorian years and March 21 in Gregorian leap years (the day following the Spring Equinox).

(c) Month Structure & Distribution of Days (1.5 Marks):
The calendar has 12 months with traditional Indian names, scientifically structured into two halves:
• Month 1 (Chaitra): 30 days in normal years; 31 days in leap years.
• Months 2 to 6 (Vaisakha, Jyeshtha, Ashadha, Shravana, Bhadrapada): Exactly 31 days each. (This accounts for Earth moving slightly slower along its elliptical orbit during northern hemisphere summer!).
• Months 7 to 12 (Ashwina, Kartika, Margashirsha, Pausha, Magha, Phalguna): Exactly 30 days each.
Total = 30 + (5 × 31) + (6 × 30) = 365 days (366 in leap year).

The Saka calendar adds its leap day to Chaitra in the exact same years that the Gregorian calendar has a leap year (divisible by 4, skipping century years unless divisible by 400). When 78 is added to the Saka year, if the resulting Gregorian year is a leap year, Chaitra has 31 days and the new year begins on March 21. It is officially published alongside the Gregorian calendar in the Gazette of India, All India Radio, and Doordarshan broadcasts.

PYQ 1 (2M) Shortest Shadow at Solar Noon & Sundial Construction

Question: Why does an upright vertical stick cast the shortest shadow at solar noon? How did ancient astronomers use this principle to construct sundials?

Answer:

  • Cause of Shortest Shadow: The length of a shadow is inversely proportional to the Sun's elevation angle. At Local Solar Noon, the Sun reaches its highest point in the sky as it crosses the local celestial meridian. Because the sun rays strike the vertical stick at the steepest (most direct) angle, the shadow cast on the horizontal ground is at its absolute minimum length. [1 Mark]
  • Sundial Construction: Ancient astronomers calibrated a flat dial plate with hour lines radiating from the base of the gnomon. As the Earth rotated from West to East, the shadow cast by the Sun rotated like a clock hand from West to North to East, allowing people to read the exact solar time of day. [1 Mark]
PYQ 2 (3M) Long-Term Impact of Lunar Orbital Recession (3.8 cm/year)

Question: Laser measurements show that the Moon is moving away from Earth at a rate of 3.8 cm per year. Explain the long-term scientific consequences of this recession on: (a) Total Solar Eclipses, (b) Duration of the month, and (c) Ocean tides.

Answer:

  1. (a) Disappearance of Total Solar Eclipses: As the Moon's distance increases, its apparent angular size in the sky will shrink. In roughly 600 million years, the Moon will appear too small to completely cover the Sun's disc, ending all Total Solar Eclipses forever (leaving only Annular "Ring of Fire" eclipses!). [1 Mark]
  2. (b) Lengthening of the Month: By Kepler's Third Law (T² ∝ r³), as the orbital radius increases, the Moon revolves slower, making the lunar month longer than 29.5 days. [1 Mark]
  3. (c) Weaker Ocean Tides: Gravitational tidal force decreases with the cube of distance (F ∝ 1/r³). As the Moon recedes, its tidal pull on Earth's oceans will weaken significantly, diminishing the height difference between high and low tides. [1 Mark]
PYQ 3 (2M) Why Solar Festivals Shift by 1 Day Every 71 Years

Question: Why do solar festivals like Makar Sankranti shift forward in the Gregorian calendar by 1 day every 71 years?

Answer:

  • Makar Sankranti is based on the Sidereal Solar Calendar (the Sun entering the constellation of Capricorn relative to fixed stars). In contrast, the Gregorian calendar is based on the Tropical Year (the cycle of equinoxes and solstices). [1 Mark]
  • Due to axial precession (the slow 25,800-year wobble of Earth's rotational axis), the sidereal year is longer than the tropical year by ~20.4 minutes. This minute gap accumulates to 1 full day every 24 hours ÷ 20.4 min ≈ 71 years. Consequently, Makar Sankranti, which was celebrated on December 21 in ancient times, has shifted to January 14/15 today! [1 Mark]

5 Fatal Student Blunders (Exam Hall Trap Alerts)

TRAP 1: "The Earth's shadow causes the Moon's phases."

The Fatal Mistake: Writing that when the Moon is in crescent or quarter phase, the dark missing part is caused by the shadow of the Earth falling on the Moon.

The Scientific Reality: Earth's shadow plays zero role in regular moon phases! The dark portion of a crescent or quarter moon is simply the Moon's own nighttime hemisphere facing away from the Sun. Earth's shadow falls on the Moon only during a lunar eclipse, which is a rare event occurring strictly on a Full Moon night!

TRAP 2: "The Moon is strictly a nighttime celestial object."

The Fatal Mistake: Assuming that the Moon only appears after sunset and sets at dawn.

The Scientific Reality: The Moon is above the local horizon during daylight hours for roughly 50% of its orbital life! It is clearly visible during the day (especially Waxing Gibbous in the afternoon and Waning Crescent in the morning) whenever its angular separation from the Sun is large enough to contrast against the daylight blue sky.

TRAP 3: "Because we always see the same face, the Moon does not rotate on its axis."

The Fatal Mistake: Confusing tidal locking with a lack of rotation.

The Scientific Reality: The Moon DOES rotate on its axis! In fact, its rotational period strictly equals its orbital revolution period (~27.3 days). If the Moon did not rotate at all, an observer on Earth would see every side of the Moon (front, back, left, right) over the course of one month! It is precisely because it rotates synchronously that the same hemisphere permanently faces Earth.

TRAP 4: "Every year divisible by 4 is a leap year (e.g., 1900)."

The Fatal Mistake: Applying only the basic 4-year rule and claiming 1900, 2100, or 2200 are leap years.

The Scientific Reality: In the Gregorian calendar, century years (divisible by 100) are leap years ONLY if they are also evenly divisible by 400! Therefore, 1900 was NOT a leap year (1900 ÷ 400 = 4.75), whereas 2000 WAS a leap year (2000 ÷ 400 = 5.0). 2100 will NOT be a leap year!

TRAP 5: "The Sun always rises exactly due East every single day."

The Fatal Mistake: Stating that the Sun rises due East 365 days a year.

The Scientific Reality: The Sun rises due East on only two days each year: the Spring Equinox (March 21) and Autumn Equinox (September 23)! During Uttarayan (Dec 21 to June 21), sunrise drifts north of East; during Dakshinayan (June 21 to Dec 21), sunrise drifts south of East.

15 Rapid Revision Points (Golden Takeaways)

  1. Non-Luminous Moon: The Moon does not emit light of its own; it shines exclusively by reflecting sunlight striking its rocky surface.
  2. 50% Illumination Rule: Exactly half of the Moon's sphere is always illuminated by the Sun at all times; phases are purely our changing viewpoint of this illuminated half from Earth.
  3. Synodic Lunar Month: The full cycle of lunar phases from New Moon to New Moon (or Full Moon to Full Moon) takes approximately 29.5 days.
  4. Shukla Paksha (Waxing Phase): The 2-week period following New Moon (Amavasya) during which the illuminated portion visible from Earth increases up to Full Moon (Purnima).
  5. Krishna Paksha (Waning Phase): The 2-week period following Full Moon (Purnima) during which the illuminated portion decreases down to New Moon (Amavasya).
  6. The 50-Minute Daily Shift: Because the Moon advances 13.2° forward along its orbit every 24 hours, the Earth must rotate an extra ~50 minutes for an observer to face it again, causing the Moon to rise ~50 minutes later every day.
  7. Ocean Tides Synchrony: High tides at coastal beaches arrive approximately 50 minutes later each day, directly matching the Moon's daily rising delay due to gravitational tidal bulges.
  8. Synchronous Rotation: The Moon's period of axial rotation strictly equals its period of orbital revolution around Earth (~27.3 days), keeping the same hemisphere permanently turned toward Earth.
  9. The 5° Orbit Tilt: The Moon's orbital plane is inclined by ~5° relative to the ecliptic plane, preventing solar and lunar eclipses from occurring every month.
  10. The Mean Solar Day (24 Hours): Defined by the time interval between consecutive local solar noons (when a vertical gnomon casts the shortest shadow).
  11. The 11-Day Lunar Deficit: A lunar year (12 × 29.5 = 354 days) falls 11 days short of the solar tropical year (365.25 days).
  12. The Adhika Maasa Solution: Luni-solar calendars add an intercalary 13th month (Adhika Maasa / Malamaasa) every 32 to 33 months (2.7 years) to re-synchronize lunar months with solar seasons.
  13. The Indian National Calendar: A solar calendar based on the Saka Era (epoch 78 CE) designed by the Calendar Reform Committee under Prof. Meghnad Saha; year begins on 1 Chaitra = March 22 (March 21 in leap years).
  14. Uttarayan & Dakshinayan: Uttarayan is the 6-month northward migration of sunrise (Dec 21 to June 21); Dakshinayan is the 6-month southward retreat (June 21 to Dec 21), documented in the Taittirīya Saṁhitā.
  15. Artificial Satellites & Space Technology: Human-made spacecraft launched into LEO and GEO for cyclone tracking, communications, and NavIC navigation, pioneered by Dr. Vikram Sarabhai and ISRO.
