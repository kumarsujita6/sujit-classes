2. Deep-Dive Core Concepts & Dissection Theorems

2.1 Rectangles, Pathways & The Perimeter-Area Fallacy

The area of a plane figure is measured by the number of unit squares required to cover its interior without gaps or overlaps. For a rectangle of length l and width w, Area = l × w.

The Perimeter-Area Fallacy: Why Perimeter Never Measures Area

A widespread misconception is that a longer boundary (perimeter) implies a larger enclosed space (area). Mathematics proves this completely false:

  • Same Perimeter, Radically Different Areas: Consider two rectangles with perimeter = 20 m:
    • Square (5 m × 5 m): Perimeter = 20 m, Area = 25 m².
    • Thin strip (9 m × 1 m): Perimeter = 20 m, Area = 9 m².
    The square encloses nearly THREE TIMES the area of the strip with the exact same boundary length!
  • Larger Perimeter with a Smaller Area!
    • Compact square (3 m × 3 m): Perimeter = 12 m, Area = 9 m².
    • Ribbon sliver (10 m × 0.1 m): Perimeter = 2(10 + 0.1) = 20.2 m, Area = 1 m².
    The ribbon has a far larger perimeter (20.2 m vs 12 m), yet encloses only 1/9th of the area!
Pathway Formulas around Rectangles

For a rectangular garden of length l and breadth b surrounded by a path of uniform width w:

1
External Border Path: Outer dimensions are (l + 2w) and (b + 2w).
Area of Path = Outer Area - Inner Area = (l + 2w)(b + 2w) - lb = 2w(l + b + 2w).
2
Central Crossroads (intersecting at right angles):
Area = Path along length + Path along breadth - Overlapping Central Square = wl + wb - w² = w(l + b - w).

2.2 Triangles: Dissection, Medians & Parallel Invariance

Every triangle can be enclosed within an outer bounding rectangle of the same base b and height h. Drawing the altitude splits the triangle into two right triangles, each occupying exactly half of its surrounding sub-rectangle. Hence:

Area of a Triangle = ½ × Base × Height = ½ × b × h
Fundamental Theorems on Triangles
  • Parallels Invariance: All triangles drawn on the same base between two parallel lines have identical areas, because their base b and perpendicular height h are identical!
  • Minimum Perimeter Theorem: Among all triangles on base BC between lines l₁ ∥ l₂, the Isosceles Triangle (AB = AC) has the strictly minimum perimeter. (By Heron's optical reflection principle, the straight-line bounce path minimizes AB + AC).
  • Median Area Bisector: In any triangle, a median connects a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Because both sub-triangles share the same altitude and have equal half-bases, a median divides a triangle into two triangles of equal area!

2.3 Parallelograms: Shear Transformations

If you drop a perpendicular altitude from vertex A to base DC forming right triangle ΔAXD, cut it out and paste it onto the opposite side along BC, it fits perfectly to form a rectangle of length DC and height AX.

Area of a Parallelogram = Base × Height = b × h

2.4 Trapeziums & Rhombus: Three Rigorous Proofs

Rhombus Area Formula

A rhombus has perpendicular diagonals d1 and d2 that bisect each other, partitioning the rhombus into 4 congruent right triangles:

Area of a Rhombus = ½ × Product of Diagonals = ½ × d1 × d2
Trapezium Area: 3 Mathematical Approaches

Let a trapezium have parallel sides a and b, and perpendicular height h:

1
Approach 1 (Rectangle + 2 Triangles):
Split base b into x + a + y = b. Area = Rectangle (a·h) + ½xh + ½yh = h[a + ½(x + y)] = h[a + ½(b - a)] = ½(a + b)h.
2
Approach 2 (Parallelogram + 1 Triangle):
Draw a line parallel to one leg, forming a parallelogram of base a and a triangle of base (b - a).
Area = Parallelogram (a·h) + Triangle [½(b - a)h] = ½h[2a + b - a] = ½(a + b)h.
3
Approach 3 (The Double-Copy Rotation):
Take two identical copies of the trapezium. Rotate the second copy upside down (by 180°) and attach it along their slanted edges. They form a single large parallelogram of base (a + b) and height h!
Area of two trapeziums = (a + b) × h  ⇒  Area of one trapezium = ½(a + b)h.

2.5 General Polygons & The Surveyor's Field-Book Method

To measure large, irregularly shaped land plots (such as agricultural fields, municipal parks, or construction sites), civil engineers and land surveyors use the Field-Book Triangulation Method:

  • A straight central traverse baseline (e.g. from station A to station D) is surveyed with a chain or electronic total station.
  • At critical stations along the baseline, perpendicular offset lines are laid out to the outer boundary vertices (B, C, E, F).
  • This partitions the entire irregular polygon into a collection of right-angled triangles and trapeziums whose areas are easily computed and summed.
Area of General Quadrilateral with diagonal d and perpendicular offsets h₁, h₂ = ½ × d × (h₁ + h₂)

3. In-Text Activities & Explorations (Step-by-Step)

Master geometric dissections and real-world unit conversions with these guided step-by-step investigations directly from the NCERT text.

Exploration 1: The Median Area Bisection Proof

Textbook Question: In a triangle ΔABC, line segment AD connects vertex A to the midpoint D of base BC. Prove that ΔABD and ΔACD have identical areas, even though they are not congruent.

View Geometric Proof
1
Identify the Bases: Since D is the midpoint of BC, the base BD of ΔABD equals the base CD of ΔACD: BD = CD = ½ BC.
2
Identify the Heights: Both triangles share the exact same vertex A. Dropping a perpendicular altitude from A to the line containing base BC gives the identical altitude h for both triangles.
3
Compute Areas:
• Area(ΔABD) = ½ × BD × h = ½ × (½BC) × h.
• Area(ΔACD) = ½ × CD × h = ½ × (½BC) × h.
Area(ΔABD) = Area(ΔACD). (Q.E.D.)
Exploration 2: Ancient Indian Śulba-Sūtra Dissection of an Isosceles Trapezium

Historical Context: In the Baudhayana Sulba-Sutra (c. 800 BCE), Vedic sacrificial fire altars (Mahāvedi) were shaped as isosceles trapeziums. How did Vedic geometers convert a trapezium into a rectangle of equal area?

View Vedic Dissection Method
1
Let ABCD be an isosceles trapezium with parallel bases AB = a and CD = b (with b > a), and slanted legs AD = BC.
2
Drop vertical altitudes from top vertices A and B down to CD, meeting at points P and Q.
3
By symmetry, the two outer triangular wings ΔAPD and ΔBQC are congruent right triangles, each with base (b - a)/2.
4
Cut off right triangle ΔAPD, invert it, and attach it to the opposite side. The resulting figure is a perfect rectangle with length equal to the midline ½(a + b) and height h!
Area = ½(a + b) × h.
Exploration 3: Real-World Length and Area Unit Conversions

Perform the textbook practice conversions between imperial and metric systems:
(i) Express 5 in and 7.4 in in centimeters.
(ii) Express 5.08 cm and 11.43 cm in inches.
(iii) Convert 161.29 cm² into square inches (in²).

View Complete Calculations
i
Inches to Centimeters (Multiply by 2.54):
• 5 in = 5 × 2.54 = 12.7 cm.
• 7.4 in = 7.4 × 2.54 = 18.796 cm.
ii
Centimeters to Inches (Divide by 2.54):
• 5.08 cm = 5.08 ÷ 2.54 = 2.0 inches.
• 11.43 cm = 11.43 ÷ 2.54 = 4.5 inches.
iii
Square Centimeters to Square Inches:
Since 1 in = 2.54 cm  ⇒  1 in² = (2.54 cm)² = 6.4516 cm².
Area in in² = 161.29 ÷ 6.4516 = 25 in²!

Figure it Out • Set 1 (Partitioned Rectangles & Park Paths)

Question 1: Finding Missing Sidelengths in Partitioned Rectangles

(i) A grid of 4 sub-rectangles has areas 14 in², 35 in², 28 in², and 21 in². Given some side markings (7 in, 4 in, 3 in, 2 in), identify the missing sidelengths.
(ii) A garden of total area 50 m² is partitioned into sub-rectangles with areas 29 m², 11 m², etc. Find the missing dimensions.

View Place-Value & Common Factor Solutions
i
Part (i) Analysis:
• Sub-rectangle 14 in² has a given vertical height of 2 in  ⇒  Width = 14 ÷ 2 = 7 in.
• Sub-rectangle 35 in² shares the width of 7 in with the 14 in² block  ⇒  Height = 35 ÷ 7 = 5 in.
• Sub-rectangle 28 in² has height 4 in  ⇒  Width = 28 ÷ 4 = 7 in.
• Sub-rectangle 21 in² has width 7 in  ⇒  Height = 21 ÷ 7 = 3 in.
ii
Part (ii) Total Area Balance:
Total Area = 50 m². Known sub-areas: 29 m² and 11 m².
Remaining sub-area = 50 - (29 + 11) = 50 - 40 = 10 m².
Using the given edge 4 m, missing side length = 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 m.
Question 2: Surrounding Path around Rectangular Park EFGH

A shaded path of uniform width is laid around a rectangular park EFGH. What measurements do you need, and what is the algebraic formula for the area of the path?

View Formula Derivation & Example Calculation
1
Measurements Needed:
1. Length of inner park: L
2. Breadth of inner park: B
3. Uniform width of surrounding path: w
2
General Formula:
Outer Length = L + 2w,   Outer Breadth = B + 2w.
Area of Path = (L + 2w)(B + 2w) - LB = LB + 2wL + 2wB + 4w² - LB
Area of Path = 2w(L + B + 2w).
3
Numerical Example: Let L = 40 m, B = 25 m, and w = 2.5 m:
Area = 2(2.5)[40 + 25 + 2(2.5)] = 5 × [65 + 5] = 5 × 70 = 350 m².

Figure it Out • Set 3 (Composite Polygons & Shaded Regions)

Question 1: Quadrilateral via Diagonal Offsets

Find the area of quadrilateral ABCD given that diagonal AC = 22 cm, and perpendicular offsets are BM = 3 cm and DN = 3 cm.

View Solution
Area(ABCD) = Area(ΔABC) + Area(ΔADC) = ½ × AC × BM + ½ × AC × DN
= ½ × AC × (BM + DN) = ½ × 22 × (3 + 3) = 11 × 6 = 66 cm².
Question 2: Shaded Triangle in Rectangle ABCD

Rectangle ABCD has dimensions length AB = 18 cm and breadth BC = 10 cm. Point E lies on AB such that AE = 10 cm (so EB = 8 cm). Point F lies on BC such that BF = 6 cm (so FC = 4 cm). Find the area of shaded ΔDEF.

View Subtraction Method
1
Total Rectangle Area: Area(ABCD) = 18 × 10 = 180 cm².
2
Area of Unshaded Corner Triangles:
• Right ΔDAE = ½ × AD × AE = ½ × 10 × 10 = 50 cm².
• Right ΔEBF = ½ × EB × BF = ½ × 8 × 6 = 24 cm².
• Right ΔFCD = ½ × FC × CD = ½ × 4 × 18 = 36 cm².
3
Subtract Unshaded Area:
Total unshaded area = 50 + 24 + 36 = 110 cm².
Area of Shaded ΔDEF = 180 - 110 = 70 cm².
Questions 3, 4 & 5: Hexagon Measurements & Half-Area Quadrilaterals

Q3: What measurements do you need to find the area of a regular hexagon?
Q4: What fraction of a rectangle's area is occupied by a triangle sharing the same base and height?
Q5: Give a geometric method to obtain a quadrilateral whose area is exactly half of a given quadrilateral.

View Conceptual Answers
  • Q3 Hexagon: You only need the sidelength s (or the distance between opposite parallel sides). A regular hexagon comprises 6 congruent equilateral triangles of side s: Area = 6 × (√3/4)s² = (3√3/2)s².
  • Q4 Triangle in Rectangle: Area(Δ) = ½ × b × h, while Area(Rectangle) = b × h. Therefore, the triangle occupies exactly ½ (50%) of the total rectangle's area.
  • Q5 Varignon's Theorem: Connect the midpoints of the 4 consecutive sides of any convex quadrilateral. The resulting figure is always a parallelogram (Varignon's Parallelogram), and its area is strictly equal to ½ the area of the original quadrilateral!

Figure it Out • Set 4 (Parallelograms & Dual Altitudes)

Question 1: Parallelograms on Same Base Between Parallels

Observe a series of parallelograms drawn on the same base between two parallel lines:
(i) What can we say about their areas?
(ii) What can we say about their perimeters? Which figure has minimum and maximum perimeter?

View Analytical Solution
i
Areas: All parallelograms have identical areas! The formula is Area = Base × Height. Since both the base and the perpendicular distance between the parallel lines are constant, every parallelogram encloses the exact same area.
ii
Perimeters:
• Minimum Perimeter: The Rectangle (where the slant sides are perpendicular to the base, making slant length equal to the shortest distance h).
• Maximum Perimeter: As the parallelogram shears and leans further, the slanted sides stretch indefinitely. The perimeter grows without bound (theoretically infinite as the angle approaches 0°).
Questions 2 & 3: Numerical Areas & Dual Altitudes

Q2: Find the areas of parallelograms with given base and height:
(i) b = 7 cm, h = 4 cm   (ii) b = 5 cm, h = 3 cm   (iii) b = 5 cm, h = 4.8 cm   (iv) b = 2 cm, h = 4.4 cm
Q3: Parallelogram PQRS has base SR = 12 cm with altitude QM = 7.6 cm. Find the second altitude QN corresponding to base PS = 8 cm.

View Calculations
2
Q2 Areas (Base × Height):
• (i) 7 × 4 = 28 cm².
• (ii) 5 × 3 = 15 cm².
• (iii) 5 × 4.8 = 24 cm².
• (iv) 2 × 4.4 = 8.8 cm².
3
Q3 Dual Altitude Calculation:
Compute total area using first base SR: Area = SR × QM = 12 cm × 7.6 cm = 91.2 cm².
Now equate to second base PS: Area = PS × QN  ⇒  8 × QN = 91.2.

Exercises 7 & 8: Regular Hexagon Decomposition & Trapezium Triangle Proof

Q7: A regular hexagon is divided into a trapezium, an equilateral triangle, and a rhombus. Find the ratio of their areas.
Q8: ZYXW is a trapezium with ZY ∥ WX. A is the midpoint of leg XY. Line ZA is extended to meet the extension of WX at point B. Prove that Area(Trapezium ZYXW) = Area(ΔZWB).

View Rigorous Geometric Proofs
7
Hexagon Partition Ratio:
A regular hexagon decomposes symmetrically into 6 congruent equilateral triangles of unit area T (Total Area = 6T):
• The equilateral triangle consists of 1 unit triangle: Area = 1T.
• The rhombus consists of 2 unit triangles: Area = 2T.
• The trapezium consists of 3 unit triangles: Area = 3T.
Ratio of Areas (Trapezium : Equilateral Triangle : Rhombus) = 3 : 1 : 2.
8
Trapezium-Triangle Equivalence Proof:
Compare ΔZYA and ΔBXA:
• YA = XA (Given that A is the midpoint of XY).
• ∠ZAY = ∠BAX (Vertically opposite angles).
• ∠ZY A = ∠BX A (Alternate interior angles since ZY ∥ WX).
By ASA Congruence Criterion, ΔZYA ≅ ΔBXA  ⇒  Area(ΔZYA) = Area(ΔBXA).
Now observe the total area:
Area(Trapezium ZYXW) = Area(Quadrilateral ZAXW) + Area(ΔZYA)
= Area(Quadrilateral ZAXW) + Area(ΔBXA) = Area(ΔZWB)! (Q.E.D.)

6. Quick Revision Notes, Exam Traps & Common Pitfalls

Golden Formula Cheat Sheet
  • Rectangle: Area = l × w  |  Perimeter = 2(l + w).
  • Triangle: Area = ½ × b × h  |  Median bisects area into equal halves.
  • Parallelogram: Area = b × h.
  • Rhombus: Area = ½ × d₁ × d₂.
  • Trapezium: Area = ½ × (a + b) × h.
  • General Quadrilateral: Area = ½ × d × (h₁ + h₂).
  • Regular Hexagon: Area = 6 × (√3/4)s² = (3√3/2)s².
  • Unit Conversions: 1 in² = 6.4516 cm² • 1 ft² = 144 in² • 1 Acre = 43,560 ft² • 1 Hectare = 10,000 m².
4 High-Risk Exam Traps & How to Avoid Them
  • Trap 1: Confusing Slanted Leg with Perpendicular Height: In parallelograms and trapeziums, students often mistakenly multiply the base by the slanted side length. Always use the perpendicular distance h between parallel sides!
  • Trap 2: Forgetting the ½ Factor in Trapezium and Rhombus: Remember that trapeziums and rhombuses are halved versions of their corresponding bounding parallelograms/rectangles. Never omit the factor of ½!
  • Trap 3: Linear vs Area Unit Conversion Blunder: If 1 ft = 12 inches, 1 ft² is NOT 12 in²! Area scales quadratically: 1 ft² = 12 × 12 = 144 in². Similarly, 1 m² = 100 × 100 = 10,000 cm²!
  • Trap 4: Overlapping Crossroads Center: When finding the area of intersecting crossroads through a field, remember to subtract the central square (w²) once, because it is counted twice in the two intersecting paths!
