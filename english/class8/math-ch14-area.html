2.1 The Concept of Area & The Perimeter Paradox

What is area? In simple words, area is the amount of flat, two-dimensional surface enclosed inside a closed boundary. When you draw a rangoli design during Diwali, paint a classroom wall, or spread a carpet across the floor, the amount of colour powder, paint, or carpet you need depends on the area of the surface, not on the length of its boundary.

Why Do We Measure Area in "Unit Squares"?

Why do we count squares rather than circles, triangles, or hexagons to measure area?

  • Perfect Tiling (No Gaps, No Overlaps): Unit squares fit side by side perfectly on a flat plane without leaving any empty gaps and without overlapping each other. If you tried tiling a floor with circles, you would always leave annoying triangular gaps between them!
  • Simplicity of Multiplication: A rectangle with length 7 cm and width 4 cm can be packed with 4 neat rows of 7 unit squares each. The total count is simply:
    ext{Total Unit Squares} = 7 imes 4 = 28 ext{ unit squares} = 28 ext{ cm}²

The Great Perimeter vs. Area Paradox: Why Perimeter Cannot Measure Area!

Many students make the mistake of thinking: "If a figure has a bigger perimeter, it must have a bigger area!" This is completely wrong!

The Golden Geometric Rule: Perimeter ≠ Area

Perimeter measures the 1-dimensional boundary length (measured in cm, m, ft).
Area measures the 2-dimensional enclosed space (measured in cm², m², ft²).
Two shapes can have the exact same perimeter but totally different areas. Even more surprising: a region with a larger perimeter can have a much smaller area than a region with a smaller perimeter!

Let us prove this with two striking examples:

  • Case 1: Same Perimeter, Completely Different Areas:
    Consider a square of side 4 cm:
    • Perimeter = 4 × 4 = 16 cm. Area = 4 × 4 = 16 cm².
    Now consider a long, skinny rectangle of length 7 cm and width 1 cm:
    • Perimeter = 2 × (7 + 1) = 16 cm. Area = 7 × 1 = 7 cm².
    Both shapes require exactly 16 cm of wire to frame, but the square encloses more than double the area of the skinny rectangle!
  • Case 2: Larger Perimeter, but Smaller Area! (Textbook Page 150):
    Consider Region 1 (a thin strip of paper): Length = 10 cm, Width = 0.5 cm.
    • Perimeter of Region 1 = 2 × (10 + 0.5) = 21 cm.
    • Area of Region 1 = 10 × 0.5 = 5 cm².
    Now consider Region 2 (a compact square): Side = 3 cm.
    • Perimeter of Region 2 = 4 × 3 = 12 cm.
    • Area of Region 2 = 3 × 3 = 9 cm².
    Here, Perimeter of Region 1 (21 cm) > Perimeter of Region 2 (12 cm), but Area of Region 1 (5 cm²) < Area of Region 2 (9 cm²)!

Dividing a Square into 4 Equal-Area Parts: Infinitely Many Ways!

On Page 149, NCERT presents a creative challenge: How many different ways can you divide a square into 4 parts of equal area?

Most students immediately think of two ways:

  1. Draw two perpendicular lines connecting the opposite midpoints (4 smaller squares).
  2. Draw the two diagonals (4 isosceles right-angled triangles).
  3. Draw 3 equally spaced parallel lines (4 long identical strips).

However, the mathematical reality is that there are infinitely many ways! If you draw any arbitrary dividing curve from the center to one edge, and compress the area along one edge while expanding it along another edge by the exact same magnitude, the total area of all 4 parts remains perfectly identical!

2.2 Rectangular Pathways, Borders & Unfolding Tubes

In real-world construction, civil engineers and garden designers frequently build footpaths around parks, tiled borders around swimming pools, or central crossroads through agricultural fields. Calculating their areas requires mastering composite rectangular subtraction and addition.

Type A: The Outer Path Around a Rectangular Park (Textbook Page 151, Q2)

Suppose an inner rectangular park EFGH has length L and width W. A shaded footpath of uniform width w is constructed around it, forming an outer rectangle ABCD.

  • Outer Length = L + 2w (since the path adds width w on both the left and right).
  • Outer Width = W + 2w (since the path adds width w on both top and bottom).
  • ext{Area of the Outer Rectangle } ABCD = (L + 2w)(W + 2w).
  • ext{Area of the Inner Park } EFGH = L imes W.
  • Area of the Path:
    ext{Area of Path} = ext{Area}(ABCD) - ext{Area}(EFGH) = (L + 2w)(W + 2w) - LW
    ⇒ ext{Area of Path} = LW + 2wL + 2wW + 4w² - LW = 2w(L + W + 2w)
The Invariant Position Theorem (Page 151, Q2(iii))

Does the area of the path change if the outer rectangle is shifted while keeping the inner park EFGH entirely inside it?
Answer: NO, the area does NOT change! Why? Because the area of the path is purely the difference of two fixed areas: ext{Area}(ABCD) - ext{Area}(EFGH). As long as the dimensions of both rectangles remain unchanged and one rectangle is completely inside the other, their individual areas do not change, so their difference remains strictly constant!

Type B: Central Cross-Paths (Textbook Page 151, Q3)

When two perpendicular paths run through the center of a rectangular plot of dimensions L imes W, one path runs horizontally along length L and one runs vertically along width W, each having uniform width w.

  • Area of Horizontal Path = L imes w.
  • Area of Vertical Path = W imes w.
  • The Central Overlap: The intersection of the two paths is a square of dimensions w imes w. If we simply add the areas of both paths, this central square is counted twice!
  • Exact Formula:
    ext{Area of Cross-Paths} = (L imes w) + (W imes w) - w² = w(L + W - w)

Type C: The Unfolding Spiral Tube & Centerline Principle (Textbook Page 152, Q4)

Consider a bent spiral pipe or tube of uniform width w = 5 cm. Finding the area of each bend can seem complicated, but mathematics gives us a genius shortcut: The Centerline Principle!

If you take a bent tube of uniform width and straighten it out, its area is simply that of a straight rectangle:

ext{Area of Tube} = ext{Uniform Width } (w) imes ext{Length of Centerline } (L_{ ext{center}})

Alternatively, one can dissect the spiral into non-overlapping horizontal and vertical rectangular blocks and sum them up. Both methods yield identical results!

2.3 Triangles & The Shearing Invariant

Why is the area of every triangle rac{1}{2} imes ext{base} imes ext{height}? Let us examine the geometric proof across all three types of triangles:

  1. Right-Angled Triangle: A rectangle of length b and width h has area b imes h. Its diagonal cuts it into two congruent right-angled triangles. By RHS congruence, the two triangles have equal area, so each triangle has area rac{1}{2}bh.
  2. Acute-Angled Triangle: Drop an altitude h from the top vertex A perpendicular to base BC. This divides the triangle into two right-angled triangles. Enclose each in a rectangle of height h. Each right triangle is half of its respective rectangle, so:
    ext{Area}( riangle ABC) = rac{1}{2}h(BD) + rac{1}{2}h(DC) = rac{1}{2}h(BD + DC) = rac{1}{2} imes ext{base} imes ext{height}
  3. Obtuse-Angled Triangle (Exterior Altitude): When the altitude falls outside the triangle on the extended base line (Textbook Page 154):
    ext{Area}( riangle ABC) = ext{Area}( riangle ADC) - ext{Area}( riangle ADB) = rac{1}{2}h imes DC - rac{1}{2}h imes DB = rac{1}{2}h(DC - DB) = rac{1}{2} imes ext{base} imes ext{height}
    Thus, the formula rac{1}{2}bh holds rigorously for every single triangle in the universe!

Core Triangle Properties Derived in Chapter 14:

  • Property 1: The Median Area Bisection Theorem:
    In any triangle, the median (the line segment connecting a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side) divides the triangle into two smaller triangles of strictly equal area!
    Proof: Both smaller triangles have bases of equal length (since the midpoint divides the base into two equal halves) and share the exact same vertex, meaning they have the exact same perpendicular height h. Since ext{Area} = rac{1}{2} imes ext{base} imes h, their areas must be equal!
  • Property 2: The 4 Diagonals of a Rectangle:
    When you draw the two diagonals of a rectangle, they intersect at the center O and split the rectangle into 4 triangles.
    Are all 4 triangles equal in area? YES! Even though opposite triangles are congruent while adjacent ones are not, the diagonals of a rectangle bisect each other into equal half-segments (OA = OB = OC = OD). By applying the Median Area Bisection Theorem to each half of the rectangle, every single one of the 4 triangles has area exactly equal to rac{1}{4} imes ext{Area of Rectangle}!
  • Property 3: Dual Altitude Calculation (Textbook Page 155):
    Because the area of a triangle is independent of which side you pick as the base:
    ext{Area}( riangle ABC) = rac{1}{2} imes BC imes AX = rac{1}{2} imes AC imes BY
    If you know three of these measurements, you can instantly find the unknown altitude BY!

2.4 Parallelograms & Dissection

A parallelogram is a four-sided figure where opposite sides are parallel and equal in length. How do we find its area?

The Dissection Proof: Converting a Parallelogram into a Rectangle (Pages 161–162)

Take a parallelogram ABCD with base DC and side AD. Drop a perpendicular line from top vertex A to base DC, meeting it at point X. Length AX = h is the perpendicular height.

  1. Slice along line AX. You get a right-angled triangle riangle AXD and a trapezium ABCX.
  2. Pick up riangle AXD, slide it to the right, and attach it to side BC. The triangle fits precisely into position as riangle BYC, forming a neat rectangle ABYX!
  3. RHS Congruence Justification:
    • AX = BY (perpendicular distance between parallel lines AB and DC).
    • ngle AXD = ngle BYC = 90^° (both are right angles).
    • AD = BC (opposite sides of the parallelogram are equal).
    By the RHS (Right angle - Hypotenuse - Side) Congruence Criterion, riangle AXD ≅ riangle BYC. Thus, the sliced piece fits with mathematical perfection!
  4. Calculating Area:
    ext{Area of Parallelogram } ABCD = ext{Area of Rectangle } ABYX = ext{Length } (XY) imes ext{Height } (AX)
    Since DX = CY, we have XY = XC + CY = XC + DX = DC = ext{base}. Therefore:
    ext{Area of a Parallelogram} = ext{base} imes ext{height}

Rectangle vs. Parallelogram with Same Side Lengths (Page 163, Q4)

Suppose you take four rigid wooden sticks: two of length 5 cm and two of length 4 cm.
• When arranged into a rectangle, the angle is 90°, so the height is exactly 4 cm. Area = 5 imes 4 = 20 ext{ cm²}.
• When pushed into a slanted parallelogram, the side remains 4 cm, but the perpendicular height h tilts and shrinks (h < 4 cm). Area = 5 imes h < 20 ext{ cm}².
Conclusion: Among all parallelograms with the same side lengths, the rectangle encloses the MAXIMUM area!

2.5 Rhombus & Diagonal Product Theorem

A rhombus is an equilateral parallelogram — all four of its sides have equal length. Because it is a parallelogram, its area can always be found using ext{base} imes ext{height}. However, a rhombus possesses two magical symmetry properties:

  • Its diagonals bisect each other at right angles (90^°).
  • Its diagonals divide the rhombus into 4 congruent right-angled triangles!

Proof 1: Dissection into a Rectangle (The Śulba-Sūtras Altar Method)

In the ancient Indian Śulba-Sūtras, geometric altars (Vedis) often required converting diamond/rhombus shapes into rectangular bricks of identical area:

  1. Let the lengths of the two diagonals be d_1 = AC and d_2 = BD. They intersect perpendicularly at center O.
  2. The diagonal BD splits the rhombus into two isosceles triangles: riangle ABD and riangle CBD.
  3. Each triangle has base d_2 and altitude rac{d_1}{2}. Slicing the two outer corners and assembling them forms a neat rectangle WXYZ.
  4. The length of rectangle WXYZ is d_1, and its width is rac{d_2}{2}.
    ext{Area of Rhombus} = ext{Area of Rectangle } WXYZ = d_1 imes rac{d_2}{2} = rac{1}{2} imes d_1 imes d_2

Proof 2: Sum of Two Triangles (Algebraic Proof)

Diagonal AC cuts rhombus ABCD into two triangles: riangle ABC and riangle ADC. Since diagonal BD is perpendicular to AC, the altitudes from vertices B and D to base AC are OB and OD:

ext{Area}(ABCD) = ext{Area}( riangle ABC) + ext{Area}( riangle ADC) = rac{1}{2}(AC)(OB) + rac{1}{2}(AC)(OD)
⇒ ext{Area}(ABCD) = rac{1}{2}(AC)(OB + OD) = rac{1}{2} imes AC imes BD = rac{1}{2} imes d_1 imes d_2

2.6 Trapezium & The Three Classical Proofs

A trapezium (also called a trapezoid) is a quadrilateral in which at least one pair of opposite sides are parallel. Let the lengths of the two parallel sides be a and b, and let the perpendicular distance between them be height h.

NCERT Grade 8 presents three distinct, brilliant pedagogical approaches to prove that:

ext{Area of a Trapezium} = rac{1}{2} imes ext{height} imes ( ext{sum of parallel sides}) = rac{1}{2}h(a + b)

Approach 1: Rectangle and Two Triangles (Textbook Page 166)

Drop two perpendiculars WM and XN from the top parallel side WX = a to the bottom parallel side ZY = b.

  • This partitions the trapezium into a central rectangle WXNM and two right-angled flank triangles riangle WMZ and riangle XNY.
  • Let MZ = x and NY = y. Then the bottom parallel side is b = x + a + y ⇒ x + y = b - a.
  • Summing all three pieces:
    ext{Area} = ext{Area}( riangle WMZ) + ext{Area}(WXNM) + ext{Area}( riangle XNY)
    = rac{1}{2}hx + ha + rac{1}{2}hy = rac{1}{2}h(x + y + 2a)
    = rac{1}{2}h((b - a) + 2a) = rac{1}{2}h(a + b)

Approach 2: Parallelogram and One Triangle (Textbook Page 167)

From top vertex B, draw a line segment BG parallel to side AD, meeting the base at point G.

  • Now, figure ABGD is a parallelogram with base a and height h. Its area is a imes h.
  • Figure riangle BGC is a triangle with base GC = b - a and height h. Its area is rac{1}{2}(b - a)h.
  • Total Area:
    ext{Area} = ext{Area}(ABGD) + ext{Area}( riangle BGC) = ah + rac{1}{2}(b - a)h = h(a + rac{b - a}{2} ight) = rac{1}{2}h(a + b)

Approach 3: Two Identical Copies Assembled into a Large Parallelogram (Page 168)

This is the most elegant proof in all of middle school geometry!

  1. Cut out two identical copies of the trapezium.
  2. Rotate the second copy upside down by 180^° and glue it along the slanted edge.
  3. Because adjacent interior angles along the parallel lines sum to 180^° (x + y = 180^°), the joined edges form perfectly straight lines!
  4. The resulting combined shape is a single giant parallelogram whose base is (a + b) and whose height is h!
  5. ext{Area of the Giant Parallelogram} = ext{Base} imes ext{Height} = (a + b)h.
  6. Since the giant parallelogram was made from two identical trapeziums:
    ext{Area of ONE Trapezium} = rac{1}{2} imes ext{Area of Parallelogram} = rac{1}{2}h(a + b)

2.7 General Polygons, Regular Hexagon & Surveyor's Field-Book Triangulation

The General Quadrilateral Diagonal Offset Formula

Any irregular four-sided figure ABCD can be split into two triangles by drawing diagonal AC = d.
From vertices B and D, drop perpendicular offsets h_1 and h_2 to diagonal AC.

ext{Area}(ABCD) = ext{Area}( riangle ABC) + ext{Area}( riangle ADC) = rac{1}{2}d × h_1 + rac{1}{2}d × h_2 = rac{1}{2}d(h_1 + h_2)

The Regular Hexagon Partition Theorem (Textbook Page 170, Q7)

A regular hexagon can be partitioned into 6 identical equilateral triangles meeting at the center point O. Let the area of one small equilateral triangle be T.

  • A trapezium formed by connecting 4 adjacent vertices contains exactly 3 small equilateral triangles. ⇒ Area = 3T.
  • An equilateral triangle formed by connecting the center to two vertices is simply 1 small triangle. ⇒ Area = 1T.
  • A rhombus formed by two adjacent small equilateral triangles has area = 2T.
  • Ratio of Areas:
    ext{Area}( ext{Trapezium}) : ext{Area}( ext{Equilateral Triangle}) : ext{Area}( ext{Rhombus}) = 3T : 1T : 2T = 3 : 1 : 2

The Surveyor's Field-Book Method (Triangulation)

How do land revenue officers, Patwaris, and civil engineers measure irregular, curvy agricultural land? They use a Field Book!

  1. A long, straight central baseline AB is laid across the field using a surveyor's measuring chain.
  2. From various boundary corners of the field, perpendicular lines (called offsets) are measured to the baseline.
  3. The offsets slice the entire irregular field into a neat collection of right-angled triangles and trapeziums!
  4. The area of each piece is calculated individually using rac{1}{2} imes ext{base} imes ext{height} or rac{1}{2}h(a + b), and the sum of all pieces gives the exact total land area.

2.8 Real-Life Area Measurements & Unit Conversion Protocols

In everyday life, areas are expressed in various metric, imperial, and traditional Indian units. Students often make fatal calculation errors by forgetting that when linear units are converted, the conversion factor must be SQUARED!

The Metric Conversion Matrix

  • 1 ext{ m} = 100 ext{ cm} ⇒ 1 ext{ m}² = 100 imes 100 = 10,000 ext{ cm²}.
  • 1 ext{ km} = 1000 ext{ m} ⇒ 1 ext{ km}² = 1000 imes 1000 = 1,000,000 ext{ m²}.

The 5 Most Dangerous Student Blunders in Area

Trap 1: Multiplying the Slanted Side instead of the Perpendicular Height!

The Blunder: When given a parallelogram with base 8 cm and slanted side 6 cm, students write ext{Area} = 8 imes 6 = 48 ext{ cm}²!
Why It Fails: Area is base times perpendicular height (b imes h). The slanted side is the hypotenuse of the right corner triangle, so the true height is strictly less than 6 cm.
The Golden Rule: Always identify the perpendicular symbol (⊥)! If no height is given, use Pythagoras or trigonometry to find h before multiplying.

Trap 2: The Double-Counted Overlap in Cross-Paths!

The Blunder: In central cross-paths, students calculate ext{Area} = (L imes w) + (W imes w) and forget to subtract the central square!
Why It Fails: The intersection of the horizontal and vertical paths is a square of area w². If you don't subtract it, that central square is counted twice, yielding an answer that is too large.
The Golden Rule: Memorize the cross-path formula: ext{Area = w(L + W - w)}.

Trap 3: Forgetting to Square Linear Units during Area Conversion!

The Blunder: Writing 1 ext{ m}² = 100 ext{ cm}² or 1 ext{ ft}² = 12 ext{ in}²!
Why It Fails: Area is two-dimensional! 1 ext{ m}² = (100 ext{ cm}) imes (100 ext{ cm}) = 10,000 ext{ cm²}. Similarly, 1 ext{ ft}² = 12 imes 12 = 144 ext{ in²}.
The Golden Rule: Always square the linear conversion factor: (k)²!

Trap 4: Forgetting the ½ Factor in Rhombus and Trapezium Formulas!

The Blunder: Writing ext{Area of Rhombus} = d_1 imes d_2 or ext{Area of Trapezium} = h(a + b).
Why It Fails: d_1 imes d_2 is the area of the enclosing bounding rectangle, which contains twice the area of the rhombus. Similarly, h(a+b) is the area of two trapeziums combined into a parallelogram.
The Golden Rule: Both formulas must have the factor rac{1{2}}!

Trap 5: Adding Path Width Only Once to the Outer Border!

The Blunder: When a path of width w runs around a park of length L, students write L_{ ext{outer}} = L + w.
Why It Fails: The path wraps around both sides (left and right, top and bottom).
The Golden Rule: The outer dimension is always L + 2w and W + 2w!
