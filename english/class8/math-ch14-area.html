1. Conservation of Area

Cutting a 2D shape into pieces and rearranging them without gaps or overlaps preserves the total surface area completely. This dissection principle connects rectangles to parallelograms, rhombuses, and trapeziums.

2. The Perimeter Paradox

Perimeter (1D boundary length) and Area (2D enclosed space) are fundamentally independent. Two shapes can have identical perimeters but vastly different areas, and a shape with a larger perimeter can enclose a smaller area!

3. The Shearing Invariant

Sliding the top vertex of a triangle or the top side of a parallelogram along a line parallel to its base keeps the perpendicular height constant, leaving the total enclosed area strictly unchanged (Cavalieri's 2D principle).

4. Indian Geometric Heritage

The ancient Vedic Śulba-Sūtras (c. 800 BCE) perfected geometric equi-area transformations. Priests constructed ritual altars by converting squares into circles, triangles into rectangles, and trapeziums without altering area.

2.1 The Concept of Area & The Unit Square Tiling Principle

Imagine you are decorating your front porch during Diwali with a beautiful rangoli. You have two flat rectangular boards to fill evenly with colored rangoli powder:

  • Board A: Dimensions 7 cm by 4 cm.
  • Board B: Dimensions 8 cm by 3 cm.

Which board needs more colored powder? To answer this with mathematical certainty, we do not guess or measure the outer border. Instead, we ask: How many non-overlapping unit squares of size 1 cm × 1 cm can fit exactly inside each board?

Formal Definition of Area

Area is the quantitative measure of the two-dimensional region enclosed within a closed geometric boundary. It is expressed as the total number of non-overlapping unit squares (each having sides of length 1 unit) required to completely cover the planar surface without gaps or overlaps.

When we place 1 cm unit squares inside Board A, we form 4 horizontal rows, each containing 7 unit squares.
Total Unit Squares in Board A = 7 × 4 = 28 unit squares = 28 cm².
For Board B, we form 3 rows of 8 unit squares:
Total Unit Squares in Board B = 8 × 3 = 24 unit squares = 24 cm².
Since 28 > 24, Board A encloses 4 more square centimeters of surface and therefore requires more rangoli powder!

Universal Formula for Any Rectangular Surface Area of Rectangle = Length × Width = L × W Area of Square (where L = W = s): Area = Side × Side = s²

2.2 The Great Perimeter Paradox: Why Perimeter Cannot Measure Area

A frequent misconception among beginning students is believing that perimeter and area are directly proportional—that a shape with a longer perimeter must necessarily contain more area, or that two shapes with the same perimeter must have the same area.
This belief is mathematically false!

The Core Distinction

Perimeter is a one-dimensional (1D) measurement of the total linear distance around the outer boundary, measured in linear units (cm, m, ft).
Area is a two-dimensional (2D) measurement of the enclosed planar space, measured in square units (cm², m², ft²).

Demonstration 1: Identical Perimeter, Radically Different Areas

Consider four different rectangles, each made by bending a wire of fixed length 24 cm (so Perimeter = 24 cm, Semi-perimeter L + W = 12 cm):

  • Rectangle 1 (Very skinny): Length = 11 cm, Width = 1 cm ⇒ Area = 11 × 1 = 11 cm².
  • Rectangle 2 (Moderately skinny): Length = 9 cm, Width = 3 cm ⇒ Area = 9 × 3 = 27 cm².
  • Rectangle 3 (Balanced): Length = 8 cm, Width = 4 cm ⇒ Area = 8 × 4 = 32 cm².
  • Rectangle 4 (Equilateral / Square): Length = 6 cm, Width = 6 cm ⇒ Area = 6 × 6 = 36 cm².

Notice that all four rectangles have the exact same perimeter of 24 cm, yet the square encloses more than three times the area of the skinniest rectangle!

The Isoperimetric Theorem for Quadrilaterals

Among all rectangles having a fixed, constant perimeter, the square encloses the MAXIMUM area. The closer the length and width are to each other, the larger the enclosed area becomes!

Demonstration 2: Larger Perimeter, Smaller Area!

We can even construct two regions where Region 1 has a strictly larger perimeter than Region 2, but a strictly smaller area:

  • Region 1 (Skinny Strip): A rectangle of Length 10 cm and Width 1 cm.
    Perimeter = 2(10 + 1) = 22 cm.
    Area = 10 × 1 = 10 cm².
  • Region 2 (Square): A square of Side 4 cm.
    Perimeter = 4 × 4 = 16 cm.
    Area = 4 × 4 = 16 cm².

Comparing them:
Perimeter of Region 1 (22 cm) > Perimeter of Region 2 (16 cm)
Area of Region 1 (10 cm²) < Area of Region 2 (16 cm²)
This completely shatters any assumption that perimeter can serve as a proxy for area!

2.3 Rectangular Pathways and Spiral Tube Unfolding

Real-world landscape architecture frequently requires calculating the area of walking tracks, tiled borders, and cross-pathways running inside or outside parks.

Case A: Uniform Pathway Laid OUTSIDE a Rectangular Park

Let a rectangular park EFGH have inner length L and inner width W. A pathway of uniform width w is constructed around its outer perimeter to form outer rectangle ABCD.

  • The outer length is extended by width w on both left and right: L_outer = L + 2w.
  • The outer width is extended by width w on both top and bottom: W_outer = W + 2w.
  • Area of Outer Rectangle ABCD = (L + 2w)(W + 2w) = LW + 2wL + 2wW + 4w².
  • Area of Inner Park EFGH = LW.
Pathway Around Outside of Park Area of Outer Path = (L + 2w)(W + 2w) - LW = 2w(L + W + 2w)

Case B: Uniform Pathway Laid INSIDE a Rectangular Park

If the outer plot has dimensions L × W, and a path of width w is built inside along the boundary:

  • The inner unpaved garden has length L - 2w and width W - 2w.
  • Area of Path = LW - (L - 2w)(W - 2w) = 2w(L + W - 2w).

Case C: Two Central Cross-Paths (Horizontal & Vertical)

When two paths of uniform width w run through the center of a plot of dimensions L × W, intersecting perpendicularly:

  • Area of horizontal path = L × w.
  • Area of vertical path = W × w.
  • The central cross intersection is a square of size w × w that gets counted twice if we simply sum them!
Central Cross-Paths Formula Area of Cross-Paths = (L × w) + (W × w) - w² = w(L + W - w) Remaining Plot Area = (L - w)(W - w)

Case D: Spiral Tube Unfolding Technique

Consider a long, winding tube with uniform cross-sectional width w = 5 units that bends back and forth like a labyrinth. How do we compute its area without tedious subdivisions?
The Unfolding Theorem: A bent tube of uniform width w can be straightened out along its centerline into an equivalent single straight rectangle!
Area of Spiral Tube = Uniform Width (w) × Total Length of Centerline (L_center).

2.4 The Universal Triangle Area Law & The Median Bisection Theorem

How do we find the area of a three-sided polygon (triangle)?

1. The Right-Angled Triangle

In a right-angled triangle with perpendicular legs b and h, a second identical congruent triangle can be rotated and joined along the hypotenuse to form a complete rectangle of dimensions b × h.
Therefore, each triangle contains exactly half the unit squares of the rectangle:
Area = ½ × base × height.

2. Acute Triangles (Altitude Falls Inside Base)

In an acute triangle ΔABC, drop altitude AX of length h perpendicular to base BC. Construct a bounding rectangle BCDE of length b and height h.
The altitude AX splits the triangle into two right triangles ΔABX and ΔACX:
• Area(ΔABX) = ½ × Area(Rectangle BXAE)
• Area(ΔACX) = ½ × Area(Rectangle XCDA)
Summing them:
Area(ΔABC) = ½ [Area(BXAE) + Area(XCDA)] = ½ × Area(BCDE) = ½ × b × h.

3. Obtuse Triangles (Altitude Falls Outside Base)

What happens if the triangle is obtuse, such that the altitude from vertex A falls outside the segment BC on its extension?
Let D be the foot of the perpendicular from A to the extended line BC. Now we observe two right triangles sharing altitude AD = h:

  • Big right triangle ΔADC with base DC.
  • Small external right triangle ΔADB with base DB.

The area of our target triangle ΔABC is the difference:
Area(ΔABC) = Area(ΔADC) - Area(ΔADB)
= ½ × h × DC - ½ × h × DB
= ½ × h × (DC - DB)
Since DC - DB = BC (the base of ΔABC):
= ½ × base × height!

The Universal Triangle Area Law

The formula Area = ½ × base × height holds universally for every triangle in existence—right, acute, or obtuse! Any side can be chosen as the base, provided the corresponding perpendicular altitude to that base line is used.

The Median Bisection Theorem

Median Equal-Area Theorem

Theorem: In any triangle, the median (the straight line joining any vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side) divides the triangle into two smaller triangles of strictly equal area!
Proof: Let AM be the median to side BC in ΔABC, so BM = MC. Drop a perpendicular altitude AH from vertex A to line BC.
• Area(ΔABM) = ½ × BM × AH
• Area(ΔACM) = ½ × MC × AH
Since BM = MC and both share the exact same perpendicular altitude AH:
Area(ΔABM) = Area(ΔACM) = ½ × Area(ΔABC).

2.5 Triangles Between Parallel Lines & Heron's Reflection Principle

Consider a fixed horizontal line segment BC of length b, and a parallel line l ∥ BC at a fixed perpendicular distance h.
Let point A be any point sliding freely along line l.

Triangles on Common Base Between Parallel Lines & Heron's Shortest Path Proof
SVG Geometry Proof

Figure 2.1: Triangles ΔABC, ΔXBC, and ΔYBC share identical area (½ × b × h). The isosceles triangle (at the perpendicular bisector) minimizes the perimeter via Heron's mirror reflection principle.

1. The Area Invariant (Shearing Principle)

No matter where point A slides along line l:
• The base is always the fixed segment BC of length b.
• The perpendicular distance from A to line BC is always the constant vertical height h.
Therefore, Area(ΔABC) = ½ × b × h is strictly constant for all positions of A!

2. The Perimeter Minimum (Heron's Reflection Principle)

While all triangles have identical area, what about their perimeter?
Perimeter(ΔABC) = BC + AB + AC.
Since BC is fixed, minimizing the perimeter is equivalent to finding the point A on line l that minimizes the sum of lengths AB + AC.

Heron of Alexandria's Mirror Reflection Proof
  1. Imagine line l acts as a flat plane mirror.
  2. Reflect point C across mirror line l to obtain its mirror image point C'. The perpendicular distance from C to l equals the distance from C' to l.
  3. By mirror symmetry (or RHS triangle congruence), for any point A on line l, the distance AC = AC'.
  4. Therefore, the total path length AB + AC = AB + AC'!
  5. To make the path from B to C' as short as possible, we know that the shortest distance between two points in a plane is the straight line segment BC'!
  6. The intersection of straight line segment BC' with line l defines the optimal point A.
  7. By vertical angle symmetry and congruent right triangles, this optimal point A lies exactly on the perpendicular bisector of BC!
  8. Hence, AB = AC, making the optimal triangle an isosceles triangle.

2.6 Parallelograms: Dissection and Base-Height Duality

A parallelogram is a quadrilateral in which both pairs of opposite sides are parallel. How do we prove its area formula without formulas?

Parallelogram Dissection into a Rectangle of Equal Area
Dissection Proof

Figure 2.2: Slicing right triangle ΔAXD from the left corner and translating it to the right creates a perfect rectangle ABYX with identical base and height.

Formal Dissection Proof
  1. Let ABCD be a parallelogram with base DC of length b.
  2. Drop perpendicular altitude AX from vertex A to DC. Let AX = h.
  3. Slice along AX, dividing the parallelogram into right triangle ΔAXD and right-trapezium ABCX.
  4. Extend line DC to the right, and drop perpendicular BY from vertex B to line DC.
  5. Observe that ΔAXD and ΔBYC are congruent:
    • AX = BY (distance between parallel lines AB and DC).
    • ∠AXD = ∠BYC = 90°.
    • AD = BC (opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal).
    By RHS congruence, ΔAXD ≅ ΔBYC!
  6. Translate ΔAXD to the right side where it fits perfectly onto ΔBYC.
  7. The resulting shape is a rectangle ABYX with width AX = h and length XY = XC + CY = XC + DX = DC = b!
  8. Area of Parallelogram ABCD = Area of Rectangle ABYX = b × h.
Universal Parallelogram Area Formula Area = Base × Corresponding Height = b × h Duality Property: Base₁ × Height₁ = Base₂ × Height₂ = Total Area

2.7 Rhombus: Diagonals and Altar Math

A rhombus is an equilateral parallelogram—all four sides have equal length a. Its two diagonals d₁ and d₂ have remarkable geometric properties:

  • They bisect each other at right angles (perpendicular bisectors).
  • They divide the rhombus into 4 congruent right-angled triangles.
Rhombus Dissection into an Equivalent Rectangle (Śulba-Sūtras Method)
Altar Geometry

Figure 2.3: Splitting a rhombus along diagonal AC into two isosceles triangles and reassembling the four quarter triangles creates a rectangle of dimensions d₁ × (d₂ / 2).

Algebraic Proof by Triangle Summation:

Diagonal BD divides rhombus ABCD into two triangles ΔABD and ΔCBD sharing base BD of length d₂:
• Diagonal AC is perpendicular to BD, so AO is the altitude of ΔABD, and CO is the altitude of ΔCBD.
• Area(ΔABD) = ½ × BD × AO
• Area(ΔCBD) = ½ × BD × CO
Total Area = ½ × BD × (AO + CO) = ½ × BD × AC = ½ × d₁ × d₂!

Rhombus Formulas Area = ½ × d₁ × d₂ Side length from diagonals: a = √[(d₁/2)² + (d₂/2)²] ⇒ Perimeter = 4a

2.8 Trapezium: Three Distinct Geometric Proofs

A trapezium (or trapezoid) is a quadrilateral with at least one pair of parallel opposite sides, called bases a and b, separated by perpendicular distance h.

Three Independent Geometric Proofs for the Area of a Trapezium
Comprehensive Proof Suite

Figure 2.4: Visual representation of the three classic proofs: (1) Central Rectangle + Two Right Triangles, (2) Double-Copy Joined Parallelogram, and (3) Internal Parallelogram + Triangle.

Proof Method 1: Rectangle and Two Right Triangles

Drop perpendicular altitudes from top vertices W and X to bottom base ZY:
• Bottom base b is partitioned into: MZ = x, MN = a (width of central rectangle), and NY = y.
So b = x + a + y ⇒ x + y = b - a.
Total Area = Area(ΔWMZ) + Area(WXNM) + Area(ΔXNY)
= ½ hx + ha + ½ hy = h(½ x + a + ½ y) = ½ h(x + y + 2a).
Substitute x + y = b - a:
= ½ h(b - a + 2a) = ½ h(a + b)!

Proof Method 2: Two Identical Copies Joined into a Parallelogram

Take a second identical cardboard copy of trapezium ABCD. Rotate it 180° upside down and place it adjacent to the first copy along their slanted side BC:
• Since internal consecutive angles along the transversal add to 180° (co-interior angles), the opposite sides become perfectly continuous straight lines.
• The combined figure is a single giant parallelogram!
• Base of giant parallelogram = a + b.
• Perpendicular height = h.
• Area of giant parallelogram = Base × Height = (a + b) × h.
Since this parallelogram consists of two identical trapeziums:
Area of One Trapezium = ½ × (a + b) × h!

Proof Method 3: Internal Parallelogram and Triangle

From vertex B of top base AB (length a), draw a line BG parallel to side AD meeting bottom base CD at G:
• Quadrilateral ABGD is a parallelogram of base a and height h ⇒ Area = a × h.
• Remaining figure ΔBGC is a triangle with base GC = b - a and height h ⇒ Area = ½ (b - a) × h.
Total Area = ah + ½ (b - a)h = h[a + ½ b - ½ a] = ½ h(a + b)!

Universal Trapezium Area Formula Area = ½ × Height × (Sum of Parallel Sides) = ½ h (a + b) Midline formulation: Area = Midline × Height, where Midline = (a + b) / 2

2.9 Polygons, Hexagon Partitions, and Surveyor's Field-Book Triangulation

1. The General Quadrilateral Area Formula

Any irregular quadrilateral ABCD can be split into two triangles by drawing diagonal AC of length d. Dropping perpendicular offsets h₁ from B and h₂ from D:
Area(ABCD) = Area(ΔABC) + Area(ΔADC) = ½ × d × h₁ + ½ × d × h₂ = ½ × d × (h₁ + h₂).

4. Varignon's Parallelogram Theorem

Pierre Varignon's Theorem (1731)

If you join the midpoints of the four consecutive sides of any quadrilateral (convex, concave, or self-intersecting), the resulting quadrilateral is always a parallelogram, and its area is strictly equal to exactly half (50%) the area of the original quadrilateral!

AR Question 1 Perimeter vs Area Equivalence Invariant

Assertion (A): If two geometric shapes have the exact same perimeter, their enclosed areas must also be strictly equal.

Reason (R): Perimeter is a one-dimensional linear measure of the boundary length, whereas area is a two-dimensional measure of the enclosed surface.

Evaluation
Truth Analysis of Assertion and Reason
• Assertion (A) is completely FALSE: As proven by the Perimeter Paradox, a 4 cm × 4 cm square and a 7 cm × 1 cm rectangle both have perimeter 16 cm, yet their areas are 16 cm² and 7 cm² respectively!
• Reason (R) is completely TRUE: Perimeter is 1D ([L]) while area is 2D ([L²]).
Therefore, Option (d) is the correct choice.
Correct Option: (d) (A is false but R is true).
AR Question 2 Median Bisection Theorem of a Triangle

Assertion (A): In any triangle ΔABC, the median AD divides the triangle into two smaller triangles ΔABD and ΔACD of equal area.

Reason (R): Both triangles ΔABD and ΔACD stand on bases of equal length (BD = CD) and share the exact same perpendicular altitude from common vertex A.

Evaluation
Truth Analysis and Causal Link
• Assertion (A) is TRUE: By the Median Bisection Theorem, a median halves a triangle's area.
• Reason (R) is TRUE: Because D is the midpoint, BD = CD. The perpendicular dropped from A to the line containing BD and CD is common to both triangles. Since Area = ½ × base × height, their areas are identically equal.
• Causal Link: Reason (R) directly and correctly explains why Assertion (A) is true.
Correct Option: (a) (Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A).
AR Question 3 Rectangle vs Non-Rectangular Parallelogram of Equal Sides

Assertion (A): A rectangle of sides 8 cm and 6 cm has a strictly greater area than a non-rectangular parallelogram having the same sidelengths 8 cm and 6 cm.

Reason (R): In any right-angled triangle formed by the perpendicular altitude and the slanted side of a parallelogram, the perpendicular leg (height) is always shorter than the hypotenuse (slanted side).

Evaluation
Truth Analysis and Causal Link
• Assertion (A) is TRUE: Area of rectangle = 8 × 6 = 48 cm². For the parallelogram on base 8 cm, the altitude h < 6 cm, so its area is 8h < 48 cm².
• Reason (R) is TRUE: By the Pythagorean theorem, the hypotenuse is the longest side of a right triangle, so h < hypotenuse = 6.
• Causal Link: Reason (R) explains precisely why the parallelogram's height is strictly smaller than the rectangle's width, directly causing the area inequality.
Correct Option: (a) (Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A).
AR Question 4 Diagonals and Area of a Rhombus

Assertion (A): The area of a rhombus is given by the formula Area = ½ × d₁ × d₂.

Reason (R): The diagonals of any arbitrary parallelogram bisect each other at right angles.

Evaluation
Truth Analysis of Assertion and Reason
• Assertion (A) is TRUE: The area of a rhombus is indeed half the product of its diagonals.
• Reason (R) is completely FALSE: The diagonals of an arbitrary parallelogram bisect each other, but they do NOT necessarily intersect at right angles! Perpendicular diagonal bisection is a unique property of rhombuses and squares, not general parallelograms.
Therefore, Assertion (A) is true but Reason (R) is false.
Correct Option: (c) (A is true but R is false).
AR Question 5 Area Scaling Under Linear Dimension Doubling

Assertion (A): If the side of a square is doubled from 5 cm to 10 cm, its area is also doubled from 25 cm² to 50 cm².

Reason (R): When linear dimensions of a 2D geometric shape are scaled by a factor k, its area scales by the square of the factor, k².

Evaluation
Truth Analysis of Assertion and Reason
• Assertion (A) is completely FALSE: Area of a 10 cm square is 10 × 10 = 100 cm², which is 4 times (not 2 times!) the original 25 cm² area.
• Reason (R) is completely TRUE: Area scales quadratically: New Area = k² × Original Area = 2² × 25 = 100 cm².
Therefore, Assertion (A) is false but Reason (R) is true.
Correct Option: (d) (A is false but R is true).

Theorem Definition
"The figure formed by joining the midpoints of the four consecutive sides of any arbitrary quadrilateral is always a parallelogram, and its area is strictly equal to half the area of the original quadrilateral." [1 Mark]
Part (b): Step-by-Step Proof [3 Marks]
Geometric Proof using Diagonal Midpoints
1. Let ABCD be a quadrilateral. Let P, Q, R, S be the midpoints of sides AB, BC, CD, DA respectively.
2. Draw diagonal BD.
• In ΔABD, S is midpoint of AD and P is midpoint of AB. By the Midpoint Theorem:
SP ∥ BD and SP = ½ BD. Also, Area(ΔAPS) = ¼ Area(ΔABD).
• In ΔCBD, R is midpoint of CD and Q is midpoint of BC. By the Midpoint Theorem:
RQ ∥ BD and RQ = ½ BD. Also, Area(ΔCQR) = ¼ Area(ΔCBD).
3. Since SP ∥ BD and RQ ∥ BD, we have SP ∥ RQ and SP = RQ.
A quadrilateral with one pair of opposite sides equal and parallel is a parallelogram. Thus, PQRS is a parallelogram! [1.5 Marks]

4. Summing the corner areas:
Area(ΔAPS) + Area(ΔCQR) = ¼ [Area(ΔABD) + Area(ΔCBD)] = ¼ Area(ABCD).
By drawing the second diagonal AC and repeating for corner triangles ΔBPQ and ΔDRS:
Area(ΔBPQ) + Area(ΔDRS) = ¼ Area(ABCD).
5. Combined Area of all 4 corner triangles:
Area_{ corners} = ¼ + ¼ = ½ Area(ABCD).
6. Area of Varignon Parallelogram PQRS:
Area(PQRS) = Area(ABCD) - ½ Area(ABCD) = ½ Area(ABCD). (Hence Proved!) [1.5 Marks]

Centroid Intersection
Let ΔABC have medians AD, BE, CF intersecting at centroid G. The medians create 6 small triangles around G: ΔGBD, ΔGCD, ΔGCE, ΔGAE, ΔGAF, ΔGBF.
Step 2: Pairwise Median Bisection
Equating Adjacent Pairs
1. In ΔGBC, GD is a median to BC (BD = DC) ⇒ Area(ΔGBD) = Area(ΔGCD) = x.
2. In ΔGCA, GE is a median to AC (CE = EA) ⇒ Area(ΔGCE) = Area(ΔGAE) = y.
3. In ΔGAB, GF is a median to AB (AF = FB) ⇒ Area(ΔGAF) = Area(ΔGBF) = z.

Show x = y = z
In ΔABC, AD is a median ⇒ Area(ΔABD) = Area(ΔACD).
z + z + x = y + y + x ⇒ 2z = 2y ⇒ z = y.
Similarly, BE is a median ⇒ 2x = 2z ⇒ x = z.
Therefore, x = y = z!
Step 4: Conclusion
One-Sixth Invariant
All 6 triangles have identical area equal to exactly ⅙ × Area(ΔABC)! (Hence Proved).
Takeaway: Each of the 6 triangles formed by the medians meeting at the centroid has an area of exactly ⅙ of the total triangle's area.

Section 6: Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

5 Common Student Blunders (Exam Hall Traps)

Trap 1: The Perimeter-Area Equivalence Trap

The Blunder: Assuming that two shapes with the same perimeter must have the same area, or that a shape with a larger perimeter must enclose a larger area.
Why It Fails: Perimeter is 1D boundary length, while area is 2D surface coverage. A 4 cm × 4 cm square has perimeter 16 cm and area 16 cm². A 7 cm × 1 cm rectangle has the same perimeter 16 cm, but only 7 cm² of area!
The Golden Rule: Never judge area by perimeter! The square maximizes area for a given quadrilateral perimeter.

Trap 2: Using the Slant Side Instead of Perpendicular Altitude!

The Blunder: In a parallelogram or trapezium, multiplying the base by the adjacent slanted side (e.g. writing Area = 8 × 6 = 48 when 6 is the slanted side).
Why It Fails: The slanted side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle, which is strictly longer than the true perpendicular altitude (h < 6). Using the slant side vastly overestimates the area!
The Golden Rule: Always drop a perpendicular line at 90° to find the true height h before calculating area.

Trap 3: Forgetting the Factor of ½ in Rhombus and Triangle Formulas!

The Blunder: Writing Area of Rhombus = d₁ × d₂ instead of ½ × d₁ × d₂.
Why It Fails: The product d₁ × d₂ gives the area of the entire bounding rectangle surrounding the rhombus. The rhombus only occupies half of this rectangle!
The Golden Rule: Both triangles and rhombuses require the half factor: ½ bh and ½ d₁ d₂.

Trap 4: Forgetting the Central Overlap in Cross-Path Problems!

The Blunder: Simply adding the horizontal path area and the vertical path area (Lw + Ww).
Why It Fails: The intersection where the two paths cross is a square of dimensions w × w. Adding both paths counts this central square twice!
The Golden Rule: Always subtract the central square: Area of Cross-Paths = w(L + W - w).

Trap 5: Adding Path Width Only Once to the Outer Border!

The Blunder: When a path of width w runs outside a park of length L, students write L outer = L + w.
Why It Fails: The path wraps around both sides (left and right, top and bottom).
The Golden Rule: The outer dimensions are always L + 2w and W + 2w!

  3. Maximum Area for Given Perimeter: Among all rectangles with a fixed perimeter, the square encloses the maximum area.
  4. Universal Triangle Law: Area = ½ × base × height holds identically for acute, right, and obtuse triangles (even when altitude falls outside).

  6. Median Bisection Theorem: A median of a triangle divides it into two triangles of equal area.
  7. Rectangle Diagonals: The two diagonals of a rectangle partition it into 4 triangles of strictly equal area (¼ of the rectangle's area).

  12. Hexagon Partition Ratio: A regular hexagon partitioned into a trapezium, equilateral triangle, and rhombus has area ratio 3 : 1 : 2.
  13. Surveyor's Triangulation: Irregular fields are surveyed by measuring perpendicular offsets from a baseline, partitioning the land into right triangles and trapeziums.
