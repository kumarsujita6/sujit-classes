2.1 The Algebra of Mind-Reading & Secret Date Tricks

Ever wondered how a street magician or a friend can "read your mind" to guess your secret number or birthday? The secret is not telepathy—it is algebraic variable cancellation and place-value encoding.

Anatomy of an Invariant "Think of a Number" Trick

Notice how each arithmetic step operates on the unknown variable x:

The Secret Birthday Trick: 100M + 165 + D

Let Month be M (1 to 12) and Day be D (1 to 31):

  1. Multiply month by 5: 5M
  2. Add 6: 5M + 6
  3. Multiply by 4: 4(5M + 6) = 20M + 24
  4. Add 9: 20M + 24 + 9 = 20M + 33
  5. Multiply by 5: 5(20M + 33) = 100M + 165
  6. Add day D: 100M + 165 + D

The Magician's Key: When the participant reveals their final number N, the magician secretly computes:

N - 165 = 100M + D

Because the day D is at most 31, it occupies strictly the tens and units digits. The hundreds and thousands digits reveal the Month M! For example, if N = 291: 291 - 165 = 126 → Month = 1 (Jan), Day = 26 → 26th January!

2.2 Number Pyramids & The Pascal-Fibonacci Connection

In a Number Pyramid, each block is the sum of the two blocks directly beneath it. Stacking these additions algebraically generates binomial coefficients matching Pascal's Triangle (known in ancient India as Meru Prastāra by Pingala, c. 300 BCE).

Historical Tribute: The Virāṅka-Fibonacci Sequence

Centuries before Leonardo Fibonacci wrote Liber Abaci in 1202 CE, Indian prosodist Acharya Virahāṅka (c. 600 CE), followed by Gopāla (c. 1135 CE) and Hemachandra (c. 1150 CE), discovered the recursive sequence when counting poetic meters composed of short (1 mātrā) and long (2 mātrās) syllables:

1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, 55, ...  [Fn = Fn-1 + Fn-2]

Remarkable Pyramid Property: If the base row of a pyramid is populated with consecutive Virāṅka-Fibonacci numbers, every single entry generated in the upper rows is also an exact Virāṅka-Fibonacci number!

2.3 Calendar Magic: The 4a + 16 Theorem

On any standard 7-day monthly calendar, adjacent horizontal cells differ by +1, while cells in consecutive rows directly underneath differ by +7.

a a + 1
a + 7 a + 8
Total Sum S = a + (a + 1) + (a + 7) + (a + 8) = 4a + 16

If someone tells you their secret 2×2 calendar sum is 40: 4a + 16 = 40 ⇒ 4a = 24 ⇒ a = 6. The four dates are 6, 7, 13, 14!

2.4 The Largest Product Optimization Theorem

Given three distinct single digits p < q < r, how do you arrange them into a 2-digit number and a 1-digit number [ _ _ ] × [ _ ] to obtain the maximum possible product?

Theorem: The Optimal Configuration is qp × r

Proof via Place-Value Expansion:
The three best candidate arrangements (pairing largest available tens digits with their multipliers) are:

  • Option 1: qp × r = (10q + p) × r = 10qr + pr
  • Option 2: rp × q = (10r + p) × q = 10rq + pq

Compare the expanded terms:

10qr = 10rq  (Identical tens contribution!)

Now compare the units contribution: pr vs pq.
Since r > q and p > 0, it follows strictly that pr > pq!
Therefore: qp × r > rp × q. The largest digit must ALWAYS serve as the single-digit multiplier, with the remaining two digits arranged in decreasing order as the multiplicand!

2.5 Timeless Divisibility Proofs: 9, 37, and 1001

Three Beautiful Divisibility Proofs
1
Reversing Two-Digit Numbers (Divisible by 9):
Original = 10a + b. Reversed = 10b + a.
Difference = (10a + b) - (10b + a) = 9a - 9b = 9(a - b). Always an exact multiple of 9!
2
Cyclic Permutations of Three-Digit Numbers (Divisible by 37 & 3):
abc = 100a + 10b + c,   bca = 100b + 10c + a,   cab = 100c + 10a + b.
Sum = 111a + 111b + 111c = 111(a + b + c) = (3 × 37)(a + b + c).
Always divisible by both 37 and 3!
3
The 1001 Magic Factor (7 × 11 × 13):
Repeating any 3-digit number to form a 6-digit number abcabc:
abcabc = abc × 1,000 + abc = abc × (1,000 + 1) = abc × 1,001.
Since 1,001 = 7 × 11 × 13, dividing consecutively by 7, 11, and 13 returns the original number abc every single time!

View Simultaneous Linear Elimination
1
Set up equations:
(1) 2C + S = 19
(2) C + 2S = 23
2
Add both equations:
3C + 3S = 42  ⇒  3(C + S) = 42  ⇒  C + S = 14.
3
Substitute C + S = 14 into Equation (1):
C + (C + S) = 19  ⇒  C + 14 = 19  ⇒  C = 5 (Circle).
4
Find S:
S = 14 - 5 = 9 (Square).
Check: 2(5) + 9 = 19; 5 + 2(9) = 23. Perfect!

Exercises 1 & 2: Number Reversal & Palindrome Divisibility

Q1: Choose a 2-digit number of different digits, reverse the digits, and subtract the smaller from the larger. Use algebra to prove why the result is always divisible by 9.

View Place-Value Proofs
1
Q1 Proof: Let the 2-digit number be 10a + b with a > b.
Reversed number = 10b + a.
Difference = (10a + b) - (10b + a) = 10a - a + b - 10b = 9a - 9b = 9(a - b).
Since (a - b) is an integer, the difference is an exact multiple of 9!
2
Q2 Proof: The 4-digit palindrome is abba = 1000a + 100b + 10b + a.
Combine like terms: (1000a + a) + (100b + 10b) = 1001a + 110b.
Notice that 1001 = 11 × 91, and 110 = 11 × 10:
abba = 11(91a + 10b).
Therefore, every 4-digit palindrome abba is strictly divisible by 11!

Exercise 5: The Three Shrines & The Magical Flower Pond

A devotee has flowers. Before each of 3 shrines lies a magical pond that doubles the flowers dipped into it. The devotee dips his flowers into pond 1, then offers an equal number of flowers y at shrine 1. He dips the remainder in pond 2 and offers y at shrine 2. Finally, he dips the remainder in pond 3 and offers all remaining flowers (which equals y) at shrine 3. How many flowers did he start with, and how many did he offer at each shrine?

View Recursive Algebraic Solution
1
Let initial flowers be x, and flowers offered at each shrine be y.
2
Shrine 1: Flowers double to 2x; offers y → Leaves (2x - y) flowers.
3
Shrine 2: Remainder doubles to 2(2x - y) = 4x - 2y; offers y → Leaves (4x - 3y) flowers.
4
Shrine 3: Remainder doubles to 2(4x - 3y) = 8x - 6y. He places all of them at shrine 3, which equals y:
8x - 6y = y  ⇒  8x = 7y  ⇒  x/y = 7/8.

The devotee started with 7 flowers and offered 8 flowers at each shrine! (Or any multiple: 14 and 16, 21 and 24, etc.).

Exercise 6: Horses and Hens (Heads and Legs Puzzle)

A farm has horses (4 legs) and hens (2 legs). Total heads = 55 and total legs = 150. Find the number of horses and hens. Solve both algebraically and without variables.

View Dual Solutions

Method 1: Algebraic System

1
Let horses = s, hens = h.
(1) s + h = 55  ⇒  2s + 2h = 110
(2) 4s + 2h = 150
2
Subtract (1) from (2):
(4s + 2h) - (2s + 2h) = 150 - 110  ⇒  2s = 40  ⇒  s = 20 horses.
Hens = 55 - 20 = 35 hens.

Method 2: Non-Algebraic Thought Experiment (All-Hen Assumption)

☆
If all 55 creatures were 2-legged hens, there would be only 55 × 2 = 110 legs.
Actual leg count has a surplus of 150 - 110 = 40 extra legs.
Since each horse contributes 2 extra legs beyond a hen, the number of horses is 40 ÷ 2 = 20 horses!
Number of hens = 55 - 20 = 35 hens.

Exercise 9: Dosa Cart Commercial Economics

Fixed cart rent = ₹5,000/day. Variable cost per dosa = ₹10.
(i) If 100 dosas are sold per day, what should the selling price be to earn a profit of ₹2,000?
(ii) If selling price is capped at ₹50 per dosa, how many dosas must be sold to make ₹2,000 profit?

View Financial Modeling
i
Case (i): 100 Dosas Sold
Total Cost = Rent + Variable = 5,000 + (100 × 10) = ₹6,000.
Required Revenue = Total Cost + Profit = 6,000 + 2,000 = ₹8,000.
Selling Price per dosa = 8,000 ÷ 100 = ₹80 per dosa.
ii
Case (ii): Price = ₹50 per Dosa, Target Profit = ₹2,000
Let x be number of dosas sold.
Revenue = 50x.
Cost = 5,000 + 10x.
Profit = Revenue - Cost = 50x - (5,000 + 10x) = 40x - 5,000 = 2,000.
40x = 7,000  ⇒  x = 7,000 ÷ 40 = 175 dosas.
Exercise 10: The Invariant 1/3 Odd Number Sequence

Evaluate the sequence of fractions: 1/3, (1+3)/(5+7), (1+3+5)/(7+9+11)... What do you observe? Explain using algebra.

View Elegant Proof
1
Numerical Evaluations:
• Term 1: 1/3 = 1/3
• Term 2: (1 + 3) ÷ (5 + 7) = 4 ÷ 12 = 1/3
• Term 3: (1 + 3 + 5) ÷ (7 + 9 + 11) = 9 ÷ 27 = 1/3
2
Algebraic Proof for n Terms:
• Numerator = Sum of first n odd numbers = n2.
• Denominator = Sum of next n odd numbers = (Sum of first 2n odd numbers) - (Sum of first n odd numbers) = (2n)2 - n2 = 4n2 - n2 = 3n2.
• Ratio = n2 ÷ (3n2) = 1/3 for all positive integers n!
Exercise 11: Karim and the Genie

A genie doubles Karim's coins whenever he walks around a tree, but charges a fee of 8 coins after each round. Karim does 3 rounds. After the 3rd round, doubling leaves him with exactly 8 coins, which he hands to the genie, leaving him broke!
(i) How many coins did Karim start with?
(ii) For what fee per round would Karim increase his coins?
(iii) How should the genie set the fee to bankrupt Karim?

View Complete Recursive Analysis
i
Initial Coins: Let initial coins be c.
• After Round 1: 2c - 8
• After Round 2: 2(2c - 8) - 8 = 4c - 24
• After Round 3: 2(4c - 24) = 8c - 48.
He has exactly 8 coins: 8c - 48 = 8  ⇒  8c = 56  ⇒  c = 7 coins!
ii
Profitable Fee: After 1 round with fee k, coins = 2c - k. For coins to grow (2c - k > c), we need k < c. The fee must be strictly less than his starting money!
iii
Genie's Bankrupting Strategy: For n rounds to completely wipe out Karim, the genie sets fee k = 2nc ÷ (2n - 1). For 3 rounds and c = 7, k = (8 × 7)/7 = 8 coins.

6. Quick Revision Notes, Exam Traps & Common Pitfalls

Golden Formula Cheat Sheet
  • Date Mystery Code: N - 165 = 100M + D  (Hundreds = Month, Last two digits = Day).
  • 3-Row Pyramid Apex: a + 2b + c  (Weights: 1, 2, 1).
  • 4-Row Pyramid Apex: a + 3b + 3c + d  (Weights: 1, 3, 3, 1).
  • 2×2 Calendar Window Sum: S = 4a + 16  ⇒  a = (S - 16) ÷ 4.
  • Largest Product for p < q < r: Optimal arrangement is strictly qp × r.
  • Cyclic Three-Digit Sum: abc + bca + cab = 111(a + b + c) = 37 × 3 × (a + b + c).
  • Odd Sequence Invariant: Sum(first n odds) ÷ Sum(next n odds) = n2 ÷ 3n2 = 1/3.
4 High-Risk Exam Traps & How to Avoid Them
  • Trap 1: Confusing Reversal Difference Signs: Always ensure a > b when stating (10a + b) - (10b + a) = 9(a - b). If the digits are equal (a = b), the difference is 0 (which is still a multiple of 9: 9 × 0).
  • Trap 2: Ignoring Multiple Inner Paths in Pyramids: Students often simply sum the base elements directly (a + b + c). Remember: Inner base elements have 2 or 3 distinct paths flowing to the top, multiplying their weight!
  • Trap 3: Falling for rp × q in Product Optimization: Although placing the largest digit in the tens position (rp) looks tempting, the single-digit multiplier scales the entire 2-digit number! Choosing the largest digit as the multiplier r always produces a larger product (10qr + pr > 10qr + pq).
  • Trap 4: Forgetting the 1001 Prime Trio: Always memorize the prime factorization 1001 = 7 × 11 × 13. It appears frequently in Olympiads, NTSE, and NCERT exam questions.
