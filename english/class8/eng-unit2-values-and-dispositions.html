2.1 Prose Deep Dive: "A Tale of Valour: Major Somnath Sharma and the Battle of Badgam"

Core Historical & Curricular Background

In October 1947, immediately following India's independence and partition, Pakistan launched Operation Gulmarg. Armed tribal raiders (infiltrators) led and supported by Pakistani regular forces invaded the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir to forcefully annex the Kashmir Valley. On 26 October 1947, Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession, legally making Jammu and Kashmir an integral part of the Dominion of India. The following morning (27 October), Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar.

1. The Airborne Lifeline & Major Sharma's Unshakable Resolve

The land route connecting Pathankot to Jammu and Srinagar was a rough, broken dirt track, rendering road reinforcement impossible in the winter of 1947. The Srinagar Airfield was the solitary gateway through which Indian Army troops and vital rations could reach the besieged valley.

On 31 October 1947, D Company of the 4th Battalion, Kumaon Regiment, was flown into Srinagar under the command of 24-year-old Major Somnath Sharma. Major Sharma had recently fractured his left wrist in a military hockey match, and his hand was encased in a heavy plaster cast. Despite medical counsel urging him to remain at base hospital, Major Sharma stood firm:

“I know my men better than anyone else, and if they are going into action, they are not going in without me.”

This iconic declaration illustrates charismatic and servant leadership. A true military commander does not send his men into danger from behind a desk; he shares their hardships, braves their perils, and anchors their morale.

2. Tactical Deployment at Badgam Ridge (3 November 1947)

By early November, rumours spread that infiltrators were moving toward Badgam, a picturesque village nestled on a high ridge less than 5 kilometres from the Srinagar airfield. Because Badgam overlooked the airfield and controlled the high ground, any enemy holding this ridge could direct heavy mortar and machine-gun fire directly onto incoming transport aircraft, effectively choking the lifeline of Kashmir.

Major Somnath Sharma and D Company (consisting of approximately 90 Indian jawans) reached Badgam on a fighting patrol. At approximately 14:30 hours (2:30 PM), a force of roughly 500 heavily armed Pakistani tribal raiders initiated an assault. Major Sharma quickly realized that the first wave of gunfire from the village was merely a diversionary tactic designed to draw Indian attention, while the main enemy force encircled his company from three directions.

3. Valour Under Crushing Numerical Superiority (7 to 1)

Outnumbered almost seven to one, D Company came under intense mortar and machine-gun fire. Knowing that retreating would expose the undefended airfield and lead to the massacre of Srinagar's civilian population, Major Sharma refused to yield an inch:

  • Guiding Indian Air Force (IAF) Strikes: To assist Indian fighter aircraft flying overhead, Major Sharma fearlessly stepped out of his defensive trenches under heavy fire and laid white cloth ground panels to clearly indicate friendly positions and target enemy mortar pits.
  • Personal Bravery & Selfless Action: With his left arm in plaster, Major Sharma ran between forward weapon posts, distributing ammunition, bandaging wounded soldiers, and personally filling Bren-gun magazines for his gunners to sustain maximum firepower.

4. The Immortal Last Radio Message & Supreme Martyrdom

As the afternoon waned, D Company's ammunition dwindled dangerously. When Brigade Headquarters radioed Major Sharma advising him to pull back and withdraw, he delivered the immortal words that are now etched into Indian military history:

Major Somnath Sharma's Final Transmission to Brigade HQ:

“The enemy is only 50 yards from us. We are heavily outnumbered. We are under devastating fire. I shall not withdraw an inch but will fight to the last man and the last round.”

Moments after transmitting this message, an enemy mortar shell landed directly on an ammunition box adjacent to his position. The explosion martyred Major Somnath Sharma instantly. He was only 24 years old.

5. The Strategic Legacy: Srinagar Saved & First Param Vir Chakra

Major Sharma's heroic sacrifice galvanised his men. Inspired by their fallen commander, the surviving jawans held their ground stubbornly for over six more hours, inflicting more than 300 casualties on the enemy raiders. This prolonged standoff delayed the enemy long enough for the 1st Battalion of the Punjab Regiment to be airlifted into Srinagar airfield. The Indian Army subsequently launched a decisive counter-offensive that drove the invaders out of the valley.

For his conspicuous gallantry, inspiring leadership, and supreme sacrifice, Major Somnath Sharma was posthumously awarded the Param Vir Chakra (PVC) — India's very first recipient of this highest military decoration. His battalion, the 4th Battalion, Kumaon Regiment (4 Kumaon), was awarded the prestigious Battle Honour 'Srinagar'.

The Design & Mythological Symbolism of the Param Vir Chakra (PVC)

The Param Vir Chakra translates to the "Wheel of the Ultimate Brave". It was instituted on 26 January 1950 by the first President of India, Dr. Rajendra Prasad, with retrospective effect from 15 August 1947.

The medal was designed by Mrs. Savitri Khanolkar (born Eve Yvonne Maday de Maros), who drew inspiration from ancient Indian Vedic philosophy — specifically Sage Dadhichi. According to Hindu mythology, Sage Dadhichi willingly sacrificed his earthly body so that the gods could fashion the indestructible weapon Vajra (thunderbolt) from his spinal bones to vanquish the demon Vritrasura. The PVC medal features the State Emblem in the centre, surrounded by four replicas of Indra's Vajra, symbolising the highest selfless sacrifice for the protection of righteousness.

2.2 Poetry Deep Dive: "Somebody's Mother" by Mary Dow Brine

Poetic Architecture & Thematic Focus

"Somebody's Mother" by American poet Mary Dow Brine is a classic narrative poem composed in rhyming couplets with an AABBCC rhyme scheme. It explores the universal themes of empathy, civic sensitivity, and the moral imperative of reverencing the elderly. It illustrates the timeless spiritual truth that small, unprompted acts of kindness hold immense power to elevate human society.

Stanza-by-Stanza Line Explanations

The woman was old and ragged and grey
And bent with the chill of the Winter’s day.
The street was wet with a recent snow
And the woman’s feet were aged and slow.

Analysis: The poem opens with bleak visual and tactile imagery. The adjectives "old", "ragged", and "grey" establish the woman's poverty, frailty, and vulnerability. The winter frost literally and metaphorically bends her frail spine. The street is wet and treacherous from freshly melted snow, making each step perilous for her slow, aged feet.

She stood at the crossing and waited long,
Alone, uncared for, amid the throng
Of human beings who passed her by
Nor heeded the glance of her anxious eyes.

Analysis: A poignant study of urban alienation. Despite being surrounded by a large crowd ("throng"), she is utterly "alone and uncared for". The pedestrians are completely indifferent; not a single passerby notices or heeds the silent distress in her anxious glance.

Down the street, with laughter and shout,
Glad in the freedom of “school let out,”
Came the boys like a flock of sheep,
Hailing the snow piled white and deep.
Past the woman so old and grey
Hastened the children on their way.
Nor offered a helping hand to her —
So meek, so timid, afraid to stir
Lest the carriage wheels or the horses’ feet
Should crowd her down in the slippery street.

Analysis: Dynamic contrast between youthful vivacity and elderly helplessness. The poet employs an effective simile: "Came the boys like a flock of sheep", highlighting their boisterous, collective momentum. However, like the adult crowd, the schoolboys rush past heedlessly. The old woman is paralysed by fear ("afraid to stir"), terrified that slipping on the ice would cause heavy horse-drawn carriages to trample her.

At last came one of the merry troop,
The gayest laddie of all the group;
He paused beside her and whispered low,
“I’ll help you cross, if you wish to go.”
Her aged hand on his strong young arm
She placed, and so, without hurt or harm,
He guided the trembling feet along,
Proud that his own were firm and strong.

Analysis: The turning point of chivalric kindness. The "gayest laddie" (the most cheerful and lively boy) displays remarkable emotional maturity. He does not yell or mock; he pauses and "whispered low" with deep gentleness. The juxtaposition of "her aged hand" on "his strong young arm" and "trembling feet" with "firm and strong" legs illustrates how youthful vitality finds its highest noble purpose in safeguarding the weak. His pride is not vanity; it is the humble joy of being able to serve.

Then back again to his friends he went,
His young heart happy and well content.
“She’s somebody’s mother, boys, you know,
For all she’s aged and poor and slow”,
“And I hope some fellow will lend a hand
To help my mother, you understand”,
“If ever she’s poor and old and grey,
When her own dear boy is far away.”
And “somebody’s mother” bowed low her head
In her home that night, and the prayer she said
Was “God be kind to the noble boy,
Who is somebody’s son, and pride and joy!”

Analysis: The moral climax and reciprocal blessing. When reunited with his companions, the boy explains his reasoning with philosophical clarity. He envisions the day when his own beloved mother might be old, frail, and alone while he is away; he hopes that some unknown youth will step forward to assist her. The poem concludes in the sanctity of the old woman's home. Her midnight prayer invokes divine blessings upon the "noble boy", completing a beautiful circle of selfless love and gratitude.

Ancient Indian Curricular Resonance: The Atharva Veda & Prithvi Sukta

In NCERT Poorvi, the textbook draws a rich cultural bridge between "Somebody's Mother" and ancient Indian heritage. In the Atharva Veda (12.1.12), the sacred Pṛthvī-Sūkta (Hymn to Mother Earth) proclaims:

माता भूमिः पुत्रोऽहं पृथिव्याः (Mātā Bhūmiḥ Putro'haṃ Pṛthivyāḥ)

"Earth is my mother, and I am her child." This Vedic worldview extends motherhood beyond biological parentage to encompass all living nature and society. Reverencing an elderly stranger on a cold street is a direct civic manifestation of this timeless civilisational ethos.

2.3 Non-Fiction Deep Dive: "Verghese Kurien — I Too Had A Dream"

Literary Genre & Structure

This selection is an epistolary non-fiction extract from the autobiography I Too Had A Dream by Dr. Verghese Kurien (as told to Gauri Salvi). Written in 2005 from Anand, Gujarat, the letter is addressed to his young grandson, Siddharth. It serves as an intimate ethical compass, imparting life lessons on leadership, integrity, contentment, and nation-building.

1. The Enduring Value of Letters vs. Instant Digital Messages

Dr. Kurien begins his reflection by contrasting the emotional nature of communication channels. In modern society, people are addicted to instant telephonic exchanges. However, Kurien observes:

  • Telephone Calls: Yield an immediate but fleeting joy that disappears as soon as the line disconnects.
  • Written Letters: Capture deeply considered thoughts, values, and philosophies. A letter becomes an enduring physical heirloom that can be treasured and re-read over generations with abiding pleasure.

2. Turning Down Wealth & Glamour for the Common Good

Born in Kozhikode, Kerala, and educated in mechanical engineering and metallurgy at Loyola College, Guindy Engineering College, and Michigan State University (USA), Dr. Kurien possessed credentials that opened every door of elite corporate success. He highlights three prominent alternative career forks that he deliberately turned down:

  1. Becoming a Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of a giant industrial conglomerate like Tata Steel.
  2. Seeking a permanent commission in the Indian Armed Forces and potentially retiring with general rank.
  3. Emigrating to the United States to build private wealth as a highly prosperous Non-Resident Indian (NRI).

Instead, Dr. Kurien chose to stay in the dusty, underdeveloped town of Anand, Gujarat, to work alongside poor milk farmers. He realized that personal satisfaction does not come from building private wealth, but from empowering marginalized producers who were being ruthlessly exploited by middlemen.

3. The Pillars of Success: Molly Kurien & Tribhuvandas Patel

Dr. Kurien emphasizes that monumental social achievements are never solitary:

  • His Wife, Molly Kurien: In the early days, Anand lacked even the basic civic amenities taken for granted in metropolitan cities. Molly ardently supported his decision, embracing rustic rural life without complaint and providing him with the poise and quiet strength to shoulder heavy responsibilities.
  • His Mentor, Tribhuvandas Patel: Tribhuvandas Patel, the visionary founder of the Kaira District Co-operative Milk Producers' Union, embodied absolute honesty, selfless public service, and moral integrity. Dr. Kurien adopted these values as the operational foundation of AMUL (Anand Milk Union Limited).

4. The Padma Vibhushan Incident: "Earn Your Own Reward"

In 1999, Dr. Kurien was conferred India's second-highest civilian honour, the Padma Vibhushan, by the President of India. During the magnificent ceremony at Rashtrapati Bhavan, young Siddharth innocently slipped the heavy medal around his neck and asked if he could keep it forever.

Dr. Kurien and his wife gave an unforgettable reply:

“This medal is yours as much as it is mine, but you should not be satisfied in merely keeping my award — the challenge before you is to earn your own reward for the work that you do in your lifetime.”

This encapsulates the true spirit of self-reliance. Ancestral honours cannot substitute for individual sweat, purpose, and integrity.

5. Redefining Failure & The Philosophy of Privilege

Dr. Kurien provides a revolutionary redefinition of failure for middle school students:

Dr. Kurien's Axiom on Life and Failure

"Life is a privilege, and to waste it would be wrong."
Dr. Kurien insists that failure is not about not succeeding in a specific venture. Real failure occurs when an individual fails to stand up for their principles, compromises on moral integrity, or refuses to give their absolute best to uplift fellow human beings.

NCERT Grammar Grammar Activities V & Passage Error Rectification (Pages 65–66)
Step 1: Core Concept / Context

Subject-Verb Agreement mandates that a singular subject takes a singular verb (ending in -s or -es in simple present tense), while a plural subject takes a plural base verb. Intervening phrases and collective nouns test syntactic discernment.

Step 2: Direct Textbook Evidence

Textbook pages 65–66 provide targeted sentences with compound subjects, 'either...or', collective nouns, and an erroneous military passage.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Exercise Solutions

Fill in the Blanks (Page 65):

  • (i) Either the children or their guardian is (is/are) responsible for the registration.
    [Rule: Proximity rule with 'or'. 'Guardian' is singular, so singular verb 'is' applies.]
  • (ii) Neither the commander nor his soldiers have (has/have) surrendered their post.
    [Rule: 'Soldiers' is plural and closest to the verb, so plural verb 'have' applies.]
  • (iii) The bravery of these young soldiers inspires (inspire/inspires) the nation.
    [Rule: Intervening prepositional phrase 'of these young soldiers' ignored; head noun 'bravery' is singular = inspires.]
  • (iv) A flock of migratory birds was (was/were) seen over the valley.
    [Rule: Collective noun 'flock' is singular = was.]

Passage Error Rectification (Page 66):

Original Error Passage:
“Each soldier salute with pride while the commander and his assistants inspects the parade. The sound of marching feet echo through the air as a group of cadets prepare for their drill. Meanwhile, a convoy of military vehicles pass by, drawing cheers from the gathered crowd.”

Analysis & Corrections:

  1. salute → salutes: Subject is 'Each soldier'. Indefinite distributive pronoun 'Each' always requires a singular verb.
  2. inspects → inspect: Subject is compound: 'the commander AND his assistants' (joined by 'and' = plural subject), requiring plural verb inspect.
  3. echo → echoes: The true head subject is singular 'The sound', not plural 'feet' inside the prepositional phrase. Singular noun takes echoes.
  4. prepare → prepares: The subject is collective noun 'a group' (viewed as a single unit), requiring singular verb prepares.
  5. pass → passes: The subject is collective noun 'a convoy' (singular), requiring singular verb passes.

Corrected Passage:
“Each soldier salutes with pride while the commander and his assistants inspect the parade. The sound of marching feet echoes through the air as a group of cadets prepares for their drill. Meanwhile, a convoy of military vehicles passes by, drawing cheers from the gathered crowd.”

Step 4: Key Vocabulary Nuance

Distributive Pronoun: Pronouns such as each, either, neither refer to persons or things one at a time, and thus are grammatically singular.

Step 5: Exam Tip / Topper Summary

Underline the genuine subject of the clause first before looking at the verb. Cross out prepositional phrases starting with of, with, in, along with.

Case Study 1 Case Study 1: Tactical Defense of Srinagar Airfield & Crisis Decision-Making
Case Background: On 3 November 1947, Major Somnath Sharma occupied Badgam ridge with 90 soldiers of D Company, 4 Kumaon. At 14:30 hours, roughly 500 Pakistani raiders attacked under heavy mortar cover. Recognizing that the village firing was a diversion, Major Sharma called for air support and personally laid white panels in open fire to guide IAF strikes. Surrounded and running out of ammunition, Brigade HQ ordered withdrawal. Major Sharma refused: “I shall not withdraw an inch but will fight to the last man and the last round.” A mortar blast killed him, but his men fought for 6 more hours, inflicting 300 casualties and delaying raiders until Punjab Regiment reinforcements landed.

Sub-Questions & Step-by-Step Solutions:

  1. Identify two tactical decisions that demonstrate Major Sharma's military genius.
    Answer: (1) Recognizing the initial gunfire from the village as a diversionary tactic, and (2) laying ground panels under direct enemy fire to guide IAF air strikes onto camouflaged enemy mortar positions.
  2. Why was retreating to Srinagar airfield an unacceptable option despite dwindling ammunition?
    Answer: Retreating would have allowed the 500 raiders to occupy the elevated Badgam ridge, placing the airfield within direct mortar range and stopping reinforcement transport aircraft from landing.
  3. Explain how D Company’s stand altered the strategic outcome of the 1947 Kashmir War.
    Answer: Holding out for six hours inflicted over 300 enemy casualties, shattered raider momentum, and provided the critical window for the 1st Battalion, Punjab Regiment to land, save Srinagar, and liberate the valley.
  4. Which core disposition did Major Sharma embody when rejecting the withdrawal order?
    Answer: Supreme sense of duty and servant leadership—sacrificing personal survival to protect national sovereignty and civilian safety.
Case Study 2 Case Study 2: Social Entrepreneurship & The AMUL Dairy Cooperative Model
Case Background: Having studied metallurgy at Michigan State University, Dr. Verghese Kurien could have secured lucrative CEO positions at Tata Steel, generalship in the Indian Army, or affluent NRI life in America. Instead, he dedicated his life to Anand, Gujarat. Guided by mentor Tribhuvandas Patel and supported by his wife Molly, Kurien founded AMUL, transforming milk-deficient India into the world's largest milk producer through Operation Flood. In 1999, awarded the Padma Vibhushan, he challenged his grandson Siddharth to earn his own rewards, maintaining that “life is a privilege, and to waste it would be wrong.”

    Answer: It eliminated exploitative middleman contractors (Pestonjee Polson syndicate) and gave dairy farmers direct ownership of processing plants and retail profits.

    Answer: Students must not rest on the privileges or accomplishments of their parents, but must strive to build their own merit, integrity, and social contribution through disciplined hard work.

CBSE Grammar Section PYQ 4: What is the proximity rule in Subject-Verb Agreement?
Topper Model Answer
  • The Proximity Rule: When two subjects are connected by correlative conjunctions such as either...or, neither...nor, or not only...but also, the verb agrees in number and person with the subject that is physically closest to it.
  • Illustrative Examples:
    • Neither the teacher nor the students were present in the lab. (Students is plural = were)
    • Neither the students nor the teacher was present in the lab. (Teacher is singular = was)

6.1 Five Critical Exam Hall Traps & How to Avoid Them

CBSE evaluators frequently observe bright students losing marks on predictable syntactical traps and thematic misinterpretations. Master these bulletproof fixes:

Trap 1: Intervening Prepositional Phrases in Subject-Verb Concord

The Blunder: Writing “The sound of marching feet echo through the valley.”
Why Students Stumble: Students instinctively look at the nearest word (feet), which is plural, and mistakenly pick the plural verb echo.
The Bulletproof Fix: Mentally strike out intervening prepositional phrases starting with of, with, in, along with, as well as. The true head subject is 'The sound' (singular). Therefore, it must be “The sound of marching feet echoes...”.

Trap 2: The Apostrophe Trap with Possessive Pronouns

The Blunder: Writing “The battalion defended it’s post” or writing “This medal is your’s and their’s.”
Why Students Stumble: Students assume that because nouns show possession with apostrophes (e.g., Rohan's bravery), pronouns must do the same.
The Bulletproof Fix: Possessive pronouns (its, yours, theirs, ours, hers) NEVER take an apostrophe! It's is strictly a contraction of it is. The correct sentence is: “The battalion defended its post” and “This medal is yours and theirs.”

Trap 3: Dash Confusion: En Dash (–) vs. Em Dash (—) vs. Hyphen (-)

The Blunder: Using a short hyphen for spans (e.g., 1923-1947) or confusing the En Dash with the Em Dash.
Why Students Stumble: Keyboards make typing hyphens easiest, so students overlook typographical rules.
The Bulletproof Fix: • For numerical spans, page ranges, or dates, always use the En Dash (–): 1923–1947, pages 50–75.
• For dramatic parenthetical breaks or emphasis, use the Em Dash (—): “Writing—even if it is a letter—not only conveys...”.

Trap 4: Misinterpreting the Boy's Pride in "Somebody's Mother"

The Blunder: Stating in MCQs or short answers that the young boy was arrogant or boasting because he was 'proud' that his feet were firm and strong.
Why Students Stumble: 'Proud' often carries a negative connotation of vanity in modern speech.
The Bulletproof Fix: In classical poetry, 'proud' signifies gratitude and noble joy. The boy was grateful for his youthful strength because it allowed him to be of service to an elderly person. His action was humble, chivalrous, and devoid of conceit.

Trap 5: Misconstruing Dr. Kurien's Definition of Failure

The Blunder: Writing that Dr. Kurien believed failure means losing money or failing to achieve an ambitious business target.
Why Students Stumble: Modern culture equates failure strictly with lack of financial or professional output.
The Bulletproof Fix: Dr. Kurien explicitly defines failure as a compromise of moral character: “Failure is not about not succeeding.” Real failure occurs when one refuses to stand up for one's principles, acts selfishly, or compromises on ethical integrity.
