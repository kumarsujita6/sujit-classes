Section 02

Deep-Dive Core Concepts & Teacher Explanations

1. The Wonder of Fractals

What is a Fractal? A fractal is a geometric shape that exhibits self-similarity. If you zoom in on any small part, it looks identical or nearly identical to the whole shape!
Famous Examples:
• Sierpiński Carpet: A square divided into 9 sub-squares with the central square removed, repeated endlessly.
• Koch Snowflake: Started from an equilateral triangle, adding smaller triangular bumps on the middle third of every edge. It has an infinite perimeter enclosing a finite area!
• In Nature: Romanesco broccoli, fern fronds, branching rivers, blood vessels, and coastlines.

2. Polyhedra & Euler's Formula

A 3D solid bounded strictly by flat polygonal faces is called a polyhedron.

Euler's Formula for Any Convex Polyhedron:
Faces (F) + Vertices (V) - Edges (E) = 2 Let's verify for standard solids:
• Cube: F = 6, V = 8, E = 12 ⇒ 6 + 8 - 12 = 2!
• Triangular Prism: F = 5, V = 6, E = 9 ⇒ 5 + 6 - 9 = 2!
• Square Pyramid: F = 5, V = 5, E = 8 ⇒ 5 + 5 - 8 = 2!
• Octahedron: F = 8, V = 6, E = 12 ⇒ 8 + 6 - 12 = 2!

3. Nets of 3D Solids

A net is a 2D flat skeleton which, when folded along its edges, forms the boundary of a 3D solid.

  • Cube: Has exactly 11 distinct nets (unfolded layouts of 6 squares)!
  • Cylinder: Formed by 1 flat rectangle rolled into a tube and 2 circular discs capping the top and bottom.
  • Cone: Formed by a sector of a circle and 1 circular base.
  • Sphere: Can a sphere have a flat net? No! A sphere has curved surface that cannot be unrolled flat without stretching or tearing (which is why flat world maps distort country shapes!).

4. Orthographic Projections: Front, Side, and Top Views

Architects and engineers visualize 3D structures on 2D blueprints using 3 standardized viewing angles:

Section 03

In-Text Activities & Classroom Explorations (Fully Solved)

Activity 1 (Page 7): How many sub-squares are removed in the first 3 stages of creating a Sierpiński Carpet?

Stage 1: 1 central square is removed out of 9 (8 remain).

Stage 2: In each of the remaining 8 squares, 1 square is removed: 8 × 1 = 8 squares removed (total removed = 1 + 8 = 9).

Stage 3: In each of the 64 remaining tiny squares, 1 is removed: 8² = 64 squares removed.

Total removed up to Stage 3: 1 + 8 + 64 = 73 squares.

Activity 2 (Page 12): Why does a cube have exactly 11 unique nets? Can two opposite faces touch in a net?

Explanation: A net of a cube consists of 6 connected squares. Mathematicians proved by combinatorial exhaustion that out of 35 possible hexamino arrangements, exactly 11 fold without overlap to form a closed cube.

In any valid cube net, opposite faces CANNOT share an edge! If two squares share an edge, folding them makes them perpendicular (adjacent) faces, never opposite.

Activity 3 (Page 15): Can a sphere have a flat net made of paper?

Answer: No, a sphere cannot have a flat paper net.

A sphere has non-zero Gaussian curvature in two directions at every point. A flat sheet of paper has zero curvature. You cannot wrap a flat sheet onto a ball without wrinkles, creases, or cutting it into tiny petals (like orange peel gores).

Section 04

Complete End-of-Chapter 'Figure it Out' Exercises (Solved Step-by-Step)

Exercise 1: A polyhedron has 20 faces and 12 vertices. How many edges does it have? (Use Euler's Formula)
Formula: F + V - E = 2.
Given: Faces F = 20 (Icosahedron!), Vertices V = 12.
Substitute: 20 + 12 - E = 2 ⇒ 32 - E = 2.
Solve: E = 32 - 2 = 30.
Result: The polyhedron has 30 edges.
Exercise 2: Can a polyhedron have 10 faces, 20 edges, and 15 vertices?
Test Euler: F + V - E = 10 + 15 - 20 = 25 - 20 = 5.
Compare: Since F + V - E = 5 ≠ 2, it violates Euler's formula!
Result: No, such a polyhedron cannot exist.
Exercise 3: Draw the Front, Top, and Side views of a solid cylinder resting upright on its circular base.
Top View: Looking from directly above, we see a flat Circle.
Front View: Looking horizontally at eye level, the curved surface projects as a Rectangle.
Side View: Looking from the side, it also projects as an identical Rectangle.
Result: Top View = Circle; Front & Side Views = Rectangles.

Section 06

Quick Revision Notes & Common Pitfalls

Pitfall: Trying to apply Euler's formula to curved solids like cylinders and cones!
Euler's formula F + V - E = 2 applies ONLY to polyhedra (shapes with flat polygonal faces). Cylinders, cones, and spheres have curved surfaces and are NOT polyhedra!
