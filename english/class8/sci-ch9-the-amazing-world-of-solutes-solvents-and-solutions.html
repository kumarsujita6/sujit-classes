2.1 The Magic of Everyday Mixtures: Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS)

Imagine you run in the hot sun during a school sports day and feel dizzy and tired. Your teacher or mother immediately gives you a glass of Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS). ORS is a life-saving drink prepared by dissolving a pinch of salt and a spoonful of sugar in clean drinking water.

Here is a wonderful question to ponder: Why does every single sip of your homemade ORS taste exactly the same? Why doesn't the first sip taste super salty and the last sip taste super sweet?

  • Even Distribution: When salt and sugar crystals are stirred into water, they break up into microscopic particles (ions and molecules) that spread out uniformly throughout every drop of liquid.
  • No Separation: No matter whether you take a sip from the very top, the middle, or the bottom of the glass, the ratio of sugar molecules, salt ions, and water molecules is identical.
  • Scientific Term: This kind of evenly blended combination is known as a uniform mixture or a solution.
Core Definition: Solution

A solution is a uniform (homogeneous) mixture of two or more substances in which the individual particles are thoroughly mixed at the microscopic level and do not settle down over time.

2.2 Uniform (Homogeneous) vs Non-Uniform (Heterogeneous) Mixtures

Let us compare what happens when we mix different common substances with clean water:

Uniform Mixture (Homogeneous)

Salt or Sugar in Water

• The solid particles completely disappear from view.
• The liquid remains completely clear and transparent.
• The mixture looks identical everywhere.
• If left undisturbed on a table for hours, the salt or sugar particles never settle down at the bottom.

Non-Uniform Mixture (Heterogeneous)

Chalk Powder, Sand, or Sawdust in Water

• The solid particles remain visibly separate and cloudy.
• You can see the individual grains with the naked eye or a hand lens.
• Heavy sand grains quickly sink to the bottom of the beaker.
• Light sawdust particles float on top of the water.
• The mixture does not have the same composition throughout.

Teacher's Thought Experiment

Why do chalk particles stay visible while salt particles vanish?
Salt crystals dissolve into individual sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions, which are less than 1 nanometre across (smaller than the wavelength of visible light!). Chalk powder (calcium carbonate) is insoluble; its particles stay grouped in giant clusters of millions of molecules that reflect light and make the water cloudy.

2.3 Anatomy of a Solution: The Golden Duo — Solute & Solvent

Every simple solution consists of two fundamental parts working together in harmony:

  • 1. The Solute: The substance that gets dissolved. In a solid-liquid solution, the solid component is the solute (for example, salt, sugar, baking soda, copper sulfate). In a liquid-liquid mixture, it is the liquid present in a smaller quantity.
  • 2. The Solvent: The medium in which the solute dissolves. In a solid-liquid solution, the liquid component is the solvent (such as water, alcohol, or oil). In a liquid-liquid mixture, it is the liquid present in a larger quantity.
The Master Solution Formula

Solute + Solvent = Solution

Memory Hack: "Solute is tiny (lesser amount), Solvent is enormous (greater amount)!"

2.4 Multiphase Solutions: Solutions Are Not Just Solid in Liquid!

Many school students mistakenly believe that a solution must always be a solid dissolved in water. In science, solutions can exist in any physical state (gas, liquid, or solid):

  • Liquid in Liquid: Lemon juice added to water, or pure vinegar (acetic acid dissolved in water). The liquid present in smaller quantity is the solute (acetic acid), and the liquid in larger quantity is the solvent (water).
  • Gas in Liquid: Aerated soft drinks contain carbon dioxide (CO₂) gas dissolved under high pressure in water. River and ocean water contains dissolved oxygen gas (O₂), which fishes breathe through their gills.
  • Gas in Gas: Clean atmospheric air is a homogeneous gaseous solution! Nitrogen gas forms 78% of air (the solvent), while oxygen (21%), argon (0.9%), and carbon dioxide (0.04%) are the gaseous solutes dissolved evenly within it.
  • Solid in Solid: Metal alloys like Brass (zinc dissolved in copper) and Bronze (tin dissolved in copper) are solid solutions formed by melting metals together and cooling them uniformly.

2.5 The Sweet Mystery: Chashni of Gulab Jamun

Have you ever helped your family make Chashni (sugar syrup) for hot, delicious Gulab Jamuns? A traditional halwai boils a massive amount of white sugar (say, 500 grams) in a tiny amount of water (say, 200 mL).

The Classic Exception Rule

Normally, we say the substance present in greater amount is the solvent. However, in Chashni, sugar is a solid and water is a liquid. Even though the mass of sugar (500 g) is much larger than the mass of water (200 g), water is still called the solvent and sugar is called the solute! Why? Because the final syrup retains the liquid state of water, and water acts as the dissolving medium.

2.7 Saturation States: Unsaturated vs Saturated Solutions

Activity 9.1 reveals a crucial law of physical chemistry: a fixed quantity of solvent has a strict physical limit to how much solute it can hold at a given temperature.

  • Unsaturated Solution: A solution in which more solute can be dissolved at that given temperature. The solvent molecules still have available empty spaces between them.
  • Saturated Solution: A solution in which no more solute can dissolve at that particular temperature. The solvent has reached its maximum holding capacity.
  • Dynamic Molecular Equilibrium: In a saturated solution with undissolved salt sitting at the bottom, dissolving does not stop! Rather, for every 100 salt ions that dissolve into the water, exactly 100 dissolved ions bump into the crystal surface and precipitate out. Because both rates are equal, the total amount of dissolved salt remains strictly constant.

2.8 Solution Concentration: Dilute vs Concentrated Solutions

The word concentration tells us how much solute is packed inside a given amount of solution:

  • Dilute Solution: Contains a relatively small amount of solute dissolved in a large quantity of solvent (for example, 1 teaspoon of salt in a large 1-litre jug of water).
  • Concentrated Solution: Contains a relatively large amount of solute dissolved in the same quantity of solvent (for example, 6 teaspoons of salt in a small half-glass of water).
  • Relative Terms: "Dilute" and "concentrated" are comparative words, just like "tall" and "short". A solution with 3 spoons of salt is concentrated compared to a 1-spoon solution, but dilute compared to a 6-spoon solution!

2.9 Defining Solubility: The Quantitative Measurement

Scientists need an exact mathematical number rather than just saying "a lot" or "a little". That quantitative metric is called solubility.

Scientific Definition: Solubility

Solubility is the maximum mass of solute (in grams) that can dissolve in a fixed quantity (traditionally 100 g or 100 mL) of a solvent at a specific temperature.

Standard Benchmark: At 20 °C, the solubility of common salt (NaCl) in water is approximately 36 grams per 100 mL of water.

2.10 Activity 9.2 Deconstructed: How Does Temperature Affect Solubility?

What happens if we take a saturated solution with solid residue at the bottom and put it over a flame? Let us examine the NCERT demonstration using baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO₃):

  1. Take 50 mL of water in a Pyrex glass beaker. Measure its starting room temperature with a laboratory thermometer (say, 20 °C).
  2. Add baking soda spoonful by spoonful while stirring continuously until some solid white powder remains undissolved at the bottom. The solution is now saturated at 20 °C.
  3. Place the beaker on a wire gauze over a tripod stand and gently heat it using a spirit lamp or Bunsen burner until the thermometer reads 50 °C. Stir with a glass rod.
  4. Observation 1: The undissolved baking soda at the bottom completely disappears! The warm water dissolves it effortlessly!
  5. Add more baking soda at 50 °C until it again stops dissolving. Then heat the beaker further to 70 °C.
  6. Observation 2: Once again, the newly undissolved powder dissolves completely!
The Golden Thermal Solubility Rule for Solids

For almost all solid solutes, solubility INCREASES as temperature rises!
Why? Heating provides thermal energy to water molecules, making them vibrate faster and spread further apart. This creates larger empty gaps between water molecules to swallow and hold more solute particles! Therefore, a saturated solution at 20 °C becomes unsaturated when heated to 50 °C.

2.11 Ancient Indian Scientific Heritage: Solvents in Ayurveda and Siddha

Thousands of years before modern pharmacy existed, classical Indian medical texts like the Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita made brilliant use of specialized solvents to extract life-saving medicines from plants and minerals:

  • Water as the Prime Solvent (Kashaya & Kwatha): Boiling herbs in water extracts water-soluble active alkaloids and polyphenols to make herbal teas and decoctions.
  • Hydro-Alcoholic Extractions (Asavas and Arishtas): Ancient Indian physicians fermented sweet herbal juices in sealed clay jars. The natural yeast produced mild bio-ethanol (alcohol). Because alcohol dissolves organic resins and essential oils that pure water cannot dissolve, these self-generated alcoholic formulations extracted potent active ingredients with extraordinary shelf life!
  • Lipid-Based Solvents (Sneha Kalpana): Medicated oils (Taila) and clarified butter (Ghrita or ghee) were used as non-polar solvents to dissolve fat-soluble compounds, enabling deep absorption through cell membranes.

2.13 Solubility of Gases in Liquids: The Vital Lifeline of Aquatic Species

Do gases dissolve in liquids? Absolutely! Every glass of tap water contains tiny amounts of dissolved nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and dissolved oxygen (O₂).

Although oxygen dissolves in water only in minute quantities (about 8 to 10 milligrams per litre at room temperature), this microscopic trace of dissolved oxygen is what sustains all aquatic life on Earth! Fishes, crabs, and aquatic tadpoles filter water through their gills to absorb this dissolved oxygen directly into their bloodstream.

The Opposite Temperature Rule: Gas Solubility!

Unlike solids, the solubility of gases in liquids DECREASES as temperature increases!

  • Cold Water Holds More Gas: In cold mountain streams or chilled winter lakes, gas molecules lose kinetic energy and stay trapped tightly between water molecules. Cold water is rich in dissolved oxygen!
  • Warm Water Loses Gas: As water heats up, dissolved gas molecules gain kinetic energy, break free from liquid attraction, and bubble out into the air. If you boil water in a pan, you will see tiny bubbles forming on the bottom long before 100 °C — that is dissolved air escaping!
  • Thermal Pollution Danger: When factories or power stations discharge hot wastewater into rivers, the water temperature rises, dissolved oxygen drops drastically, and aquatic fish can suffocate and die!

2.14 Why Do Objects Float or Sink in Water?

Drop an iron nail into a bucket of water, and it shoots straight to the bottom like a pebble. Yet, a massive dry wooden log or a giant bamboo pole floats effortlessly on the surface. Why does this happen?

In daily life, people often say "heavy things sink and light things float". But wait! A huge tree trunk weighs 500 kilograms — far heavier than a tiny 2-gram iron pin — yet the tree trunk floats while the iron pin sinks! Clearly, total mass alone does not decide whether something floats or sinks. What truly matters is how much mass is squeezed into a given amount of space — a fundamental property known as density.

2.15 What Is Density? The Secret of Physical Compactness

To understand density, think of two everyday school scenarios:

  • A Crowded School Bus: On a rainy morning, 60 students are packed tightly inside a small bus. You can barely move your elbows. That bus has a very high density of students!
  • An Empty School Bus: In the afternoon, only 5 students sit comfortably inside the same big bus with plenty of open space. That bus has a very low density of students!
  • A Dense Forest: A jungle where thick Sal and Teak trees grow inches apart is a dense forest, whereas an open meadow with just two trees per acre is less dense.
Scientific Definition: Density

Density is defined as the mass present in a unit volume of a substance. It tells us how tightly matter is packed together inside an object.

Density = Mass ÷ Volume   ⇒   D = M ÷ V

Units of Density and Their Conversions

Because Density = Mass ÷ Volume, its unit is simply the unit of mass divided by the unit of volume:

  • SI Unit (Standard International): Mass is in kilograms (kg) and volume is in cubic metres (m³). Therefore, the SI unit is kg/m³ (kilogram per cubic metre).
  • Laboratory / Practical Units: In school laboratories, measuring in metres and kilograms is too large! We use g/cm³ (gram per cubic centimetre) or g/mL (gram per millilitre).
  • Conversion Benchmark:
    1 g/cm³ = 1 g/mL = 1,000 kg/m³
    Pure liquid water at room temperature has a density of approximately 1.0 g/cm³ (or 1,000 kg/m³).

2.16 Mass vs Weight: A Critical Scientific Distinction

In everyday street language, people say "I am checking my weight" and express it in kilograms. In science, this causes immense confusion! Let us review the fundamental difference:

Scientific Parameter Mass (m) Weight (W)
Scientific Meaning Total quantity of matter contained within an object. Gravitational pull exerted by Earth on that object.
Formula Fundamental physical scalar quantity (m). W = m × g (where g ≈ 9.8 m/s² on Earth).
SI Unit Kilogram (kg) or gram (g). Newton (N).
Constancy Across the Universe Remains strictly constant everywhere (Earth, Moon, deep outer space). Changes with local gravity (on the Moon, weight is only 1/6th of Earth weight!).
Measuring Instrument Two-pan beam balance or digital balance (calibrated to read mass). Spring balance.

2.17 Activity 9.3 Deconstructed: How to Measure Mass with a Digital Balance

In modern laboratories, we use a digital electronic balance for measuring mass. Here is how a real scientist measures the mass of a solid stone or a liquid:

  1. Place the digital balance on a firm, completely horizontal, vibration-free laboratory table.
  2. Press the ON / POWER button. The screen turns on and should read 0.000 g.
  3. Place a dry watch glass, plastic container, or butter paper on the metal weighing pan. The balance now displays the mass of the watch glass (say, 14.250 g).
  4. Now press the magical TARE (or ZERO/RESET) button! The display instantly resets back to 0.000 g, ignoring the container's weight!
  5. Carefully place your irregular stone onto the watch glass. The screen now directly shows the true, net mass of the stone alone (say, 16.400 g).
  6. To measure a liquid: Simply replace the watch glass with an empty beaker, press TARE to reset to zero, and pour in your liquid!

2.18 Measuring Volume of Liquids: Apparatus and Least Count

Volume is the amount of three-dimensional space occupied by an object. Its SI unit is the cubic metre (m³), while liquid volumes are measured in litres (L) and millilitres (mL). Remember: 1 mL = 1 cm³ (1 cc).

To measure liquid volume accurately, we use a graduated measuring cylinder — a narrow, tall, transparent cylinder made of glass or polypropylene with etched measurement lines.

What Is Least Count?

The least count of an instrument is the smallest measurement that can be accurately read from its scale.

Least Count = (Value of One Main Division) ÷ (Number of Small Subdivisions)

Example: In a 100 mL cylinder, between the 10 mL and 20 mL marks there is a difference of 10 mL, divided into 10 small lines. Therefore, Least Count = 10 mL ÷ 10 = 1 mL.

2.19 Activity 9.5 Deconstructed: The Art of Reading the Meniscus

When you pour water into a glass measuring cylinder, you will notice that the surface of the water is not completely flat! It curves upward at the edges touching the glass wall, forming a U-shaped bowl. This curved liquid surface is called the meniscus (derived from the Greek word for "crescent moon").

  • Why does water form a curved meniscus? Water molecules are attracted more strongly to the glass surface (adhesion) than to each other (cohesion), pulling the liquid slightly up the sides.
  • Rule for Clear / Colourless Liquids (Water, Alcohol): Always read the graduation mark that aligns with the VERY BOTTOM OF THE CONCAVE MENISCUS!
  • Rule for Opaque / Deeply Coloured Liquids (Potassium permanganate, Dark syrup): Because the bottom curve cannot be seen clearly, read the TOP LEVEL of the liquid surface!
  • Parallax Error Warning: Your line of sight must be exactly horizontal and level with the bottom of the meniscus. Looking from above makes the reading appear too high; looking from below makes it appear too low!

2.20 Determining Volume of Regular Solids by Geometry

For regular geometrical solids, we don't need water or cylinders — we can calculate volume directly with a standard measuring ruler using mathematical formulas:

  • Cuboid (Shoe box, Book, Wooden block):
    Volume = Length × Width × Height   ⇒   V = l × w × h
    Example (Activity 9.6): A science textbook of length 25 cm, width 18 cm, and height 2 cm has:
    Volume = 25 cm × 18 cm × 2 cm = 900 cm³.
  • Cube (Dice, Sugar cube):
    Volume = Side × Side × Side = s³

2.21 Activity 9.7 Deconstructed: The Water Displacement Method for Irregular Solids

What if you have a rough, jagged stone or an antique metal key? You cannot measure its length or width with a straight ruler! How did the ancient Greek polymath Archimedes solve this? Through the famous Water Displacement Method!

Archimedes' Principle of Displacement

Scientific Principle: When a solid object is completely submerged in a liquid, it pushes aside (displaces) a volume of liquid exactly equal to its own three-dimensional volume!

Volume of Object = Final Water Level (V₂) − Initial Water Level (V₁)

  1. Pour water into a 100 mL graduated measuring cylinder up to an easily readable mark, say exactly V₁ = 50 mL.
  2. Tie a clean, thin thread around your irregular stone. (Using thick rope will displace extra water and cause an error!).
  3. Tilt the cylinder slightly and lower the stone gently into the water until it is completely submerged. (Never drop it heavily, or water might splash out or crack the glass!).
  4. Observe the water level rising up the cylinder marks. Record the new, higher level: V₂ = 55 mL.
  5. Subtract the initial volume from the final volume:
    Volume of displaced water = 55 mL − 50 mL = 5 mL.
    Since 1 mL = 1 cm³, the solid volume of the stone is exactly 5 cm³!
  6. If the mass of this stone was measured on a digital balance as 16.400 g, its density is calculated instantly:
    Density = Mass ÷ Volume = 16.400 g ÷ 5 cm³ = 3.28 g/cm³.

Figure 2.5: Quantitative metrology & buoyancy manipulation • Water displacement measures irregular volume directly. Adding salt to water increases its density, turning a sinker into a floater!

2.22 Planetary Density Stratification: The Concentric Layers of Earth

Did you know that our entire home planet, Earth, is an enormous natural density sorting column? When the early Earth was molten billions of years ago, gravity sorted all materials based strictly on their density:

  • Earth's Crust (Top Layer): Composed of light silica and aluminium rocks (granite, basalt). It has the lowest density, ranging from 2.7 to 3.0 g/cm³.
  • Mantle (Upper & Lower): Denser silicate rocks rich in iron and magnesium. Density increases from 3.4 to 5.5 g/cm³.
  • Outer Core (Liquid): Highly compressed liquid iron and nickel alloy. Density surges to 9.9 to 12.2 g/cm³!
  • Inner Core (Solid): Pure crystallized iron-nickel solid under colossal pressure of millions of atmospheres. Density reaches an astonishing 12.8 to 13.1 g/cm³!

Figure 2.6: Earth's internal density gradient • Heavy iron and nickel sank to form the core, while lightweight silicates floated to form the outer crust.

2.23 Ancient Marine Engineering: Why Do Bamboo Rafts Float?

In ancient times, long before giant steel ships were built, river communities in Odisha, Assam, and Bengal used bamboo poles and wooden logs to cross broad rivers:

  • Hollow Cellular Structure: Bamboo is not a solid mass of heavy wood; its stem consists of large hollow chambers filled with trapped air!
  • Extremely Low Bulk Density: Because of the vast air volume inside each segment, the average bulk density of a bamboo pole is only about 0.3 to 0.4 g/cm³ — far less than water (1.0 g/cm³).
  • Immense Buoyant Capacity: When several bamboo poles are lashed together into a raft, they can support the heavy weight of people and cargo without sinking.

2.24 Temperature vs Density: Why Does Hot Air Rise?

What happens to the density of matter when it is heated?

  • When a substance is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy, vibrate faster, and push each other further apart. This causes the substance to expand in volume.
  • However, heating does not create new atoms; the mass remains strictly unchanged!
  • Since Density = Mass ÷ Volume, an increase in volume causes the density to decrease!
  • Hot Air Balloon Physics: The giant propane burner under a hot air balloon heats the air inside the nylon envelope. The hot air expands, spills some air out, becomes less dense than the cool surrounding atmospheric air, and creates a net upward buoyant lift that carries the balloon high into the sky!

2.25 Effect of Pressure on Density: Gases vs Incompressible Liquids & Solids

How does squeezing or compressing matter change its density?

  • Gases Are Highly Compressible: Gas molecules have enormous empty spaces between them. Increasing pressure forces molecules close together, drastically shrinking volume. Therefore, the density of a gas increases greatly with pressure (e.g., compressed natural gas in CNG cylinders).
  • Liquids and Solids Are Practically Incompressible: In liquids and solids, constituent molecules are already touching each other. Applying everyday pressure cannot squeeze them closer. Thus, pressure has virtually zero effect on the density of liquids and solids!

2.26 The Miracle of Floating Ice: Water's Unique Density Anomaly at 4 °C

Almost every liquid on planet Earth contracts (shrinks) continuously as it gets colder, becoming denser and denser until it freezes. Liquid water is the extraordinary exception to this rule!

  • Maximum Density at 4 °C: As warm water cools down, it contracts normally until it reaches 4 °C, where water achieves its highest possible density (exactly 1.0000 g/cm³).
  • The Anomalous Expansion: When water cools further from 4 °C down to 0 °C, something astonishing happens: it begins to expand! The water molecules begin arranging themselves into a rigid hexagonal crystal lattice held by open hydrogen bonds with wide hollow spaces.
  • Ice Floats: Because solid ice at 0 °C occupies more volume than liquid water, its density drops to approximately 0.917 g/cm³! Because 0.917 < 1.0, ice floats on liquid water!
  • Ecology & Winter Life Support: In freezing sub-zero winters in Ladakh, Kashmir, or the Arctic, the top surface of a lake freezes into a thick floating sheet of ice. This ice layer acts as a thermal blanket, trapping heat underneath. The heavy, dense water at 4 °C stays safely at the bottom of the lake, allowing fish, frogs, and aquatic weeds to swim, breathe, and survive throughout the coldest winter!

2.29 The Modeling Clay Riddle: Cube vs Flat Sheet vs Boat

  • However, if Reema shapes the clay into a hollow boat: The open hollow shape encloses a large volume of air! 2.31 Extreme Natural Waters: The Hyper-Saline Dead Sea

Located between Jordan and Israel, the Dead Sea is one of the world's saltiest bodies of water:

  • Normal ocean seawater has a salinity of about 3.5% (density ≈ 1.025 g/cm³).

Probe 3: Why do sugar and salt dissolve in water but not in oil? Why is water considered a good solvent?
Step 1: Core Concept / Definition

This phenomenon is governed by the universal chemical rule of dissolution: "Like dissolves like" and the unique polar nature of water.

Step 2: Scientific Principle

• Water (H₂O) is a polar solvent with slight positive and negative electrical charges on its molecules.
• Cooking oil is a non-polar liquid with no electrical charge distribution.
• Salt (NaCl) is ionic (Na⁺ and Cl⁻), and sugar (sucrose) has polar hydroxyl (−OH) groups.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Mechanism

1. Why salt and sugar dissolve in water: The positive and negative ends of water molecules strongly pull on the sodium and chloride ions of salt, tearing the crystal lattice apart and hydrating the ions. Sugar molecules form strong hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
2. Why they do not dissolve in oil: Non-polar oil molecules have no electrical charges to attract ions or polar sugar molecules. The attraction between salt ions is far stronger than any attraction between salt and oil, so salt remains insoluble.
3. Why water is a "universal solvent": Water dissolves a wider variety of substances (salts, acids, bases, sugars, gases) than any other known liquid due to its high polarity and high dielectric constant.

Step 4: Final Precise Answer

Salt and sugar are polar/ionic substances that dissolve easily in polar water, but cannot interact with non-polar oil molecules. Water is an extraordinary solvent because its polar molecular structure attracts and dissolves a vast range of substances.

Step 5: Topper's Golden Insight

Remember the golden chemistry phrase: "Like dissolves like" (Polar dissolves polar; non-polar dissolves non-polar).

Why Is There No Aquatic Life in the Dead Sea?

• Hyper-Salinity: The Dead Sea contains nearly 340 grams of dissolved minerals and salts per litre of water (almost 10 times saltier than the ocean!).
• Severe Osmotic Shock: In such extreme salt water, water is rapidly pulled out of the cells of fish, plants, and amphibians by osmosis, causing instant dehydration and cellular death.
• Other Similar Water Bodies: Great Salt Lake (Utah, USA), Lake Retba (Senegal — famous pink lake), and Sambhar Salt Lake (Rajasthan, India — India's largest inland salt lake).

Project 2: Comparative Solubility

Dissolving Salt in Water, Vinegar, and Oil

• In Water (Polar solvent): Salt dissolves rapidly and in large quantities (up to ~36 g/100 mL).
• In Vinegar (Acetic acid + water): Salt dissolves very well because vinegar is approximately 95% polar water.
• In Cooking Oil (Non-polar solvent): Salt completely fails to dissolve; salt crystals sit visibly intact at the bottom.
• Conclusion: Polarity determines solubility — ionic salt requires a polar solvent to break its crystal lattice.

• Proposition Arguments (Pro-Water): Water dissolves more chemical substances than any other liquid on Earth. It sustains all biochemistry, blood transport, sap circulation in trees, and marine ecosystems.

• Verdict: Water is called the "Universal Solvent" of biological life and aqueous chemistry, but non-polar solvents remain indispensable for industrial chemistry.

  • (c) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (d) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
AR 1: Assertion (A): Ice floats on liquid water.
Reason (R): Water has its maximum density at 4 °C and expands as it freezes into ice at 0 °C.
Step 1: Evaluation of Assertion (A)

Ice floats on liquid water — TRUE. In nature, icebergs, ice cubes, and frozen lake covers float with about 90% submerged.

Step 2: Evaluation of Reason (R)

Water expands when cooled from 4 °C down to 0 °C, reaching maximum density at 4 °C — TRUE. This is known as the anomalous expansion of water.

Step 3: Causal Linkage

Because water expands upon freezing, a given mass of ice occupies more volume than liquid water. Hence, density of ice (0.917 g/cm³) is less than water (1.0 g/cm³), directly explaining why it floats.

Step 4: Final Precise Answer

Correct Option: (a) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

AR 2: Assertion (A): Clean atmospheric air is classified scientifically as a solution.
Reason (R): Solutions can only exist in the liquid state.
Step 1: Evaluation of Assertion (A)

Clean air is a homogeneous mixture of gases, meeting all criteria of a solution — TRUE.

Step 2: Evaluation of Reason (R)

Reason states that solutions can only exist in the liquid state — FALSE. Solutions can exist in solid (alloys), liquid (saline), or gaseous (air) states.

Step 3: Final Precise Answer

Correct Option: (c) (A) is true, but (R) is false.

AR 3: Assertion (A): A hot saturated solution of common salt becomes unsaturated when heated to a higher temperature.
Reason (R): For most solid solutes, solubility in liquids increases with an increase in temperature.
Step 1: Evaluation of Assertion (A)

A saturated solution at 20 °C behaves as an unsaturated solution when heated to 50 °C — TRUE.

Step 2: Evaluation of Reason (R)

Solubility of solid solutes increases with temperature — TRUE.

Step 3: Causal Linkage

Because higher temperature increases the solvent's maximum capacity to hold solid solute, the existing amount of dissolved solute is now below the new capacity limit, making the solution unsaturated.

Step 4: Final Precise Answer

Correct Option: (a) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

AR 4: Assertion (A): A raw whole egg sinks in pure tap water but floats in concentrated salt water.
Reason (R): Adding salt to water decreases the density of the solution.
Step 1: Evaluation of Assertion (A)

The egg sinks in fresh water and floats in concentrated brine — TRUE.

Step 2: Evaluation of Reason (R)

Reason asserts that adding salt decreases the density of water — FALSE! Dissolving salt adds mass without increasing volume, which increases liquid density.

Step 3: Final Precise Answer

Correct Option: (c) (A) is true, but (R) is false.

AR 5: Assertion (A): Relative density has no units.
Reason (R): Relative density is the ratio of two identical physical quantities (density of substance divided by density of water).
Step 1: Evaluation of Assertion (A)

Relative density is a pure dimensionless number without units — TRUE.

Step 2: Evaluation of Reason (R)

It is defined as (Density of substance) ÷ (Density of water) — TRUE.

Step 3: Causal Linkage

When two quantities with the identical units (g/cm³ ÷ g/cm³) are divided, the units cancel out completely, leaving a pure ratio. Hence, (R) correctly explains (A).

Step 4: Final Precise Answer

Correct Option: (a) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

5.4 Long Answer Questions (5 Marks with Official Marking Schemes)

LA 1 (5 Marks): Describe an experiment to determine the volume and density of an irregular jagged stone. Include apparatus, formula, step-by-step procedure, observations, and precautions.
Step 1: Aim & Apparatus (1 Mark)

• Aim: To determine the volume and density of an irregular solid stone using the water displacement method.
• Apparatus: Digital weighing balance, 100 mL graduated measuring cylinder, irregular stone, fine cotton thread, water, and dropper.

Step 2: Mathematical Formulas (1 Mark)

1. Volume of Stone (V) = Final Water Level (V₂) − Initial Water Level (V₁)
2. Density of Stone (D) = Mass (M) ÷ Volume (V)

Step 3: Step-by-Step Procedure (1.5 Marks)

1. Measuring Mass: Place a watch glass on the digital balance, press TARE, place the dry stone on it, and record mass M in grams.
2. Setting Initial Volume: Place the measuring cylinder on a flat bench. Pour clean water up to a clear mark (say, 50 mL). Read the bottom of the concave meniscus at eye level. Record initial volume V₁.
3. Immersion: Tie the stone with a thin thread. Gently lower it into the cylinder until fully submerged, ensuring no bubbles adhere to its surface.
4. Recording Final Volume: Note the raised water level V₂ at eye level.

Step 4: Calculation & Sample Data (1 Mark)

• Let Mass M = 16.4 g.
• Initial Volume V₁ = 50 mL, Final Volume V₂ = 55 mL.
• Volume of stone V = 55 − 50 = 5 mL = 5 cm³.
• Density = 16.4 g ÷ 5 cm³ = 3.28 g/cm³.

Step 5: Two Essential Precautions (0.5 Mark)

1. Use a thin thread (thick string displaces extra water, skewing volume).
2. Lower the stone gently so that no water splashes out of the cylinder.

LA 2 (5 Marks): (a) Define solubility. (b) Explain how temperature affects the solubility of solids versus gases in liquids with real-life examples. (c) A solution is saturated at 60 °C. What happens when it cools to 20 °C?
Model Answer & Step-by-Step Marking Scheme

(a) Definition of Solubility (1 Mark):
Solubility is the maximum mass of solute (in grams) that can dissolve in a fixed quantity (typically 100 g or 100 mL) of a solvent at a specific temperature.

(b) Effect of Temperature (2 Marks):
• Solids in Liquids (1 Mark): Solubility of solids increases with an increase in temperature. Heating increases intermolecular space in the liquid, allowing more solid particles to dissolve. Example: Sugar dissolves far more in hot tea or warm Chashni than in iced water.
• Gases in Liquids (1 Mark): Solubility of gases decreases with an increase in temperature. Warming increases the kinetic energy of gas molecules, causing them to escape from the liquid. Example: Cold mountain streams hold abundant dissolved oxygen for trout fish, whereas hot factory wastewater causes fish suffocation.

(c) Cooling a Saturated Solution (2 Marks):
When a solution saturated at 60 °C is cooled to 20 °C, the solvent's dissolving capacity decreases sharply. The excess dissolved solute that cannot remain dissolved at 20 °C separates out of the solution in the form of pure, sparkling crystals at the bottom of the beaker. This process is called crystallization.

LA 3 (5 Marks): (a) State the mathematical formula and SI unit of density. (b) An empty graduated cylinder weighs 120 g. When 80 mL of an unknown liquid is poured into it, the total mass becomes 192 g. Calculate: (i) mass of liquid, (ii) density of liquid, (iii) relative density with respect to water. Predict whether this liquid will float or sink in water.
Step-by-Step Numerical Solution (5 Marks)

(a) Formula & SI Unit (1 Mark):
Density = Mass ÷ Volume  (D = M ÷ V). SI Unit: kg/m³.

(b) Numerical Step-by-Step Working (3 Marks):
• (i) Mass of liquid alone [1 Mark]:
  Mass of liquid = Total mass − Mass of empty cylinder
  Mass = 192 g − 120 g = 72 g.

• (ii) Density of liquid [1 Mark]:
  Volume of liquid = 80 mL = 80 cm³.
  Density = 72 g ÷ 80 cm³ = 0.90 g/cm³ (or 0.90 g/mL).
  In SI units: 0.90 × 1,000 = 900 kg/m³.

• (iii) Relative Density (RD) [1 Mark]:
  RD = Density of Liquid ÷ Density of Water
  RD = 0.90 g/cm³ ÷ 1.00 g/cm³ = 0.90 (NO UNITS!).

(c) Flotation Prediction [1 Mark]:
Since the relative density is 0.90 (which is less than 1.0), this unknown liquid is lighter than water and will FLOAT on top of water.

Case Study 1: The Winter Freeze & Aquatic Survival

Scenario: The Frozen High-Altitude Lake of Ladakh

During January in Ladakh, atmospheric air temperatures plunge to −20 °C. School student Tenzing observes that the surface of Pangong Tso and local freshwater ponds is completely frozen into solid, white ice sheets several inches thick. Local herders walk and skate across the ice safely. Yet, through cracks in the ice, Tenzing is astonished to see fish swimming actively in clear liquid water below. Nearby, at a trout hatchery, an aerator pump keeps water moving because workers know that oxygen levels dictate fish health.

Question 1: Why does ice form only on the surface of the lake rather than freezing from the bottom up?

Answer: Due to the anomalous expansion of water. Water contracts and becomes denser as it cools down to 4 °C, causing 4 °C water to sink to the bottom. Below 4 °C, water expands and becomes less dense. Freezing 0 °C water and ice (density 0.917 g/cm³) are lighter than liquid water, so ice forms and stays exclusively on the surface.

Question 2: What is the approximate temperature of the liquid water at the very bottom of this frozen lake?

Answer: Exactly 4 °C. Water reaches its maximum density (1.000 g/cm³) at 4 °C, so this heaviest water layer remains stably at the lake floor, insulated from freezing air.

Question 3: How does the floating ice sheet benefit the aquatic organisms trapped below?

Answer: Ice is a poor conductor of heat. The floating ice sheet acts as a natural insulating thermal shield that prevents heat from escaping from the water beneath, preventing the entire lake from turning into a solid block of ice.

Question 4: Why is dissolved oxygen higher in cold winter water than in summer water?

Answer: Gas solubility in liquids increases as temperature decreases. In cold water, oxygen molecules have lower kinetic energy and remain trapped between water molecules, providing ample oxygen for fish respiration.

Case Study 2: Industrial Metrology & Adulteration

Scenario: The Dairy Quality Inspector's Lactometer Test

Sunita is a food safety inspector visiting a village milk collection centre in Cuttack, Odisha. Pure cow's milk has a normal density of 1.028 to 1.032 g/cm³ due to dissolved sugars (lactose), minerals, and proteins. Some dishonest suppliers dilute pure milk with tap water (density 1.000 g/cm³) to increase liquid volume and make extra profit. Sunita uses an instrument called a Lactometer (a specialized glass hydrometer) that floats in the milk sample to measure its density directly.

Question 1: On what scientific principle does a lactometer operate?

Answer: It operates on the principle of flotation and density. A hydrometer sinks deeper in less dense liquids, and floats higher in denser liquids.

Question 2: If a milk sample is heavily adulterated with water, will the lactometer sink deeper or float higher? Explain.

Answer: The lactometer will sink deeper. Adding tap water (density 1.000 g/cm³) lowers the overall density of the milk below 1.028 g/cm³. The less dense liquid provides less buoyant upward force, causing the instrument to sink lower.

Question 3: A sample of 500 mL milk weighs 515 g. Calculate its density and state whether it is pure.

• Density = Mass ÷ Volume = 515 g ÷ 500 mL = 1.030 g/cm³.
• Since 1.030 g/cm³ lies squarely within the pure milk range (1.028 – 1.032 g/cm³), the sample is pure and unadulterated.

Question 4: Why does cream (fat) rise and float on top of milk when left undisturbed?

Answer: Milk fat (cream) has a density of approximately 0.93 g/cm³, which is significantly lower than the density of skimmed milk water (1.03 g/cm³). By the law of buoyancy, less dense fat globules float to the top.

5.6 Previous Years Questions (PYQs) & Most Probable Exam Questions

PYQ 1: An empty glass bottle with its cap tightly screwed on floats in water. When the bottle is filled with water and capped, it sinks. Explain why. [CBSE Exam]
Model Answer (2 Marks)

• Empty capped bottle: The bottle encloses a large volume of low-density air. The combined mass of the glass and air divided by the large outer volume gives an average density much less than water (1.0 g/cm³). Hence, it floats.
• Water-filled bottle: When water replaces air, the mass increases enormously while the volume remains the same. The combined density of solid glass (2.5 g/cm³) and water now exceeds 1.0 g/cm³. Hence, it sinks.

PYQ 2: Why is common salt added to ice in traditional kulfi makers? [CBSE Exam]
Model Answer (2 Marks)

• Adding common salt (solute) to melting ice creates a freezing mixture.
• Dissolving salt depresses (lowers) the freezing point of water below 0 °C (down to −15 °C to −20 °C).
• This sub-zero freezing mixture rapidly freezes the sweet kulfi cream solid without needing an electric freezer!

PYQ 3: Why can you easily float on your back in the Dead Sea without swimming? [State Board Exam]
Model Answer (2 Marks)

• The Dead Sea contains nearly 34% dissolved salts, giving its water an extraordinary density of approximately 1.24 g/cm³.
• The average density of the human body (with lungs filled with air) is approximately 0.98 to 1.00 g/cm³.
• Since Density of Human Body < Density of Dead Sea Water, the upward buoyant force exerted by the dense water is far greater than the person's weight, keeping the body floating effortlessly.

PYQ 4: What is dynamic equilibrium in a saturated solution? Does dissolving stop completely? [Exemplar Question]
Model Answer (2 Marks)

• Dissolving does NOT stop at the molecular level!
• In a saturated solution with undissolved solute at the bottom, Rate of Dissolution = Rate of Crystallization.
• The number of solute particles dissolving into the liquid per second equals the number of dissolved particles precipitating back out as solid crystals per second. Because both opposing processes occur at identical speeds, the net concentration of dissolved solute remains constant.

PYQ 5: Hot air balloons fly high in the sky. On what scientific principle do they rise, and how does the pilot make them descend? [CBSE Exam]
Model Answer (3 Marks)

• Principle of Ascent (1.5 Marks): When the propane burner heats the air inside the balloon, the air expands in volume while mass spills out. Its density becomes less than the cool ambient air outside. The surrounding denser air exerts an upward buoyant force, causing the balloon to rise.
• Principle of Descent (1.5 Marks): To descend, the pilot reduces the flame or opens a parachute vent at the top of the envelope to let hot air escape. Cooler air enters, the interior air contracts and becomes denser, and the balloon gently descends to the ground.

6.1 Five Fatal Exam Hall Blunders (And How to Dodge Them)

Fatal Blunder 1

Flipping Solute & Solvent in Sugar Syrup (Chashni)

The Student Mistake: Looking at 600 g of sugar dissolved in 200 mL of water and writing: "Sugar is the solvent because 600 g > 200 g."

How to Dodge It: In a solid-in-liquid solution, the solid is ALWAYS the solute and the liquid is ALWAYS the solvent, because the final mixture is in the liquid state! The "larger quantity = solvent" rule is reserved strictly for liquid-liquid mixtures (like alcohol in water).

Fatal Blunder 2

Thinking Density Halves When an Object Is Cut

The Student Mistake: When asked: "An iron block of density 7.9 g/cm³ is cut into two equal halves. What is the density of each half?" students write 7.9 ÷ 2 = 3.95 g/cm³!

How to Dodge It: Density is an intensive property that depends on atomic packing, NOT size. When you cut the block in half, both mass AND volume halve simultaneously: (M/2) ÷ (V/2) = M/V = 7.9 g/cm³! Each half has the exact same density of 7.9 g/cm³!

Fatal Blunder 3

Assuming Gas Solubility Increases with Temperature

The Student Mistake: Generalizing that "all substances dissolve better when heated" and writing that hot water contains more dissolved oxygen than cold water.

How to Dodge It: Remember the thermal divergence rule: Solids dissolve MORE upon heating, but gases dissolve LESS! Heat gives gas molecules the kinetic punch needed to escape into the air. Cold water is rich in dissolved oxygen!

Fatal Blunder 4

Reading the Top of the Meniscus for Clear Water

The Student Mistake: Looking down at a measuring cylinder and taking the reading where the water creeps up the glass walls.

How to Dodge It: For clear, colourless liquids (like water or alcohol), you must always squat to eye level and align with the BOTTOM of the concave meniscus. Only deeply coloured or opaque liquids are read from the top!

Fatal Blunder 5

Adding Units to Relative Density

The Student Mistake: Calculating relative density correctly as 2.7, but then writing: "Relative Density = 2.7 g/cm³ or 2.7 kg/m³." (Instant 1-mark penalty!).

How to Dodge It: Relative density is the ratio of two identical densities: (g/cm³) ÷ (g/cm³). The units completely cancel out! Relative Density is a pure number with NO units!

6.2 15 Golden Revision Points (5-Minute Rapid Pre-Exam Recall)

  1. Solution Anatomy: A solution is a uniform (homogeneous) mixture where Solute + Solvent = Solution. Particles are smaller than 1 nanometre and never settle.
  2. Phase Identification: In solid-in-liquid mixtures, solid is always the solute. In liquid-liquid solutions, the minor volume is solute and the major volume is solvent.
  3. Chashni Rule: Water is the solvent in Gulab Jamun sugar syrup even if the mass of dissolved sugar exceeds the mass of water.
  4. Unsaturated Solution: Can dissolve more solute at that specific temperature.
  5. Saturated Solution: Has reached maximum solute capacity at that temperature; excess solute settles at the bottom.
  6. Dynamic Equilibrium: In a saturated solution, Rate of Dissolution = Rate of Crystallization. Dissolution does not stop; net concentration remains constant.
  7. Solubility Benchmark: Maximum grams of solute dissolved in 100 mL of solvent at a specific temperature (NaCl ≈ 36 g/100 mL at 20 °C).
  8. Thermal Divergence Law: Heating increases the solubility of solids, but decreases the solubility of gases in liquids.
  9. Density Fundamental: Density = Mass ÷ Volume (D = M ÷ V). SI unit: kg/m³. Lab unit: g/cm³ (1 g/cm³ = 1,000 kg/m³ = 1 g/mL).
  10. Flotation Rule: An object floats if its density is less than the liquid (D_obj < D_liq); it sinks if its density is greater (D_obj > D_liq).
  11. Anomalous Expansion of Water: Water reaches peak density at 4 °C (1.0000 g/cm³). Freezing 0 °C ice expands to 0.917 g/cm³, allowing it to float as an insulating blanket that protects aquatic life beneath.
  12. Peeled vs Unpeeled Orange: Unpeeled orange floats due to air trapped in its spongy rind; peeled orange sinks because dense sugary pulp lacks air pockets.
  13. Buoyancy Manipulation: A fresh egg sinks in tap water (~1.0 g/cm³), but floats in concentrated salt brine (~1.15 g/cm³) because dissolved salt increases liquid density.
  14. Water Displacement Method: For irregular solids, Volume = V₂ − V₁ (where 1 mL of displaced water equals exactly 1 cm³ of solid volume).
  15. Indian Living Heritage: Ningel village in Manipur preserves ancient artisanal brine wells lined with 100-year-old tree trunks to boil and mould traditional sacred "salt cakes".
