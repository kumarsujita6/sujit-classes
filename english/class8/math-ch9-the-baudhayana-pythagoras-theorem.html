2.1 Doubling a Square — The Ancient Enigma of the Śulba-Sūtras

In ancient India (c. 800 BCE), Vedic ceremonies required constructing sacred fire altars (called vedis) made of carefully placed baked bricks. One common problem encountered by priest-architects was:

The Altar Construction Problem (Baudhāyana Śulba-Sūtra, c. 800 BCE)

"How can one construct a square fire altar having exactly double the area of a given square fire altar?"

The Intuitive Trap: Why Doubling the Side Does NOT Double the Area

When young students first encounter this question, their immediate instinctive guess is almost always: "Simply double the length of each side!"

Let us test this assumption mathematically. Suppose the original square has side length s = 1 unit.

  • Original Area = s² = 1 × 1 = 1 sq. unit.
  • If we double the side length to 2s = 2 × 1 = 2 units, then:
  • New Area = (2s)² = 2 × 2 = 4 sq. units!

Notice what happened: doubling the side length did not double the area; it quadrupled (4×) the area! In fact, four identical copies of the original square fit neatly inside this newly expanded square.

Baudhāyana’s Elegant Solution (Śulba-Sūtra, Verse 1.9)

Baudhāyana gave an astonishingly simple and elegant answer in Verse 1.9:

"The diagonal of a square produces a square of double the area of the original square."

In Sanskrit, the diagonal cord is called sama-caturasra-akṣṇayā-rajju. Baudhāyana states that if you take the diagonal of the given square and build a new square using that diagonal as the side length, the new square will have exactly double the surface area!

Original Square (PEAR): Composed of exactly 2 triangles (▲1 + ▲2). • Diagonal Square (REST): Composed of exactly 4 congruent triangles (▲1 + ▲3 + ▲4 + ▲5). Ratio of Areas = 4 / 2 = 2 : 1 (Exact Doubling!)

Figure 2.1: Geometric proof of Baudhāyana Śulba-Sūtra Verse 1.9 — The square built on the diagonal contains exactly four congruent half-square triangles, whereas the original contains two.

Why Does the Dotted Square Have Double Area? (East-West / North-South Line Proof)

In the Śulba-Sūtras, Baudhāyana frequently relies on drawing perpendicular horizontal and vertical reference lines, called 'east-west' (prācī) and 'north-south' (udīcī) lines.

By extending the sides of the original square horizontally and vertically:

  1. The original square is cleanly halved by its diagonal into 2 congruent right isosceles triangles.
  2. The square constructed on the diagonal encompasses 4 congruent right isosceles triangles of the exact same size.
  3. Because each of these small triangles is congruent (identical in base, height, and area):
    Area(Square on Diagonal) = 4 × Area(Triangle) = 2 × [2 × Area(Triangle)] = 2 × Area(Original Square)
Hands-On Verification: Doubling a Square Using Paper

Students can prove this physically in class:

  1. Cut out two identical squares of paper (Square 1 and Square 2) of side 10 cm.
  2. Leave Square 1 intact.
  3. Fold Square 2 along both of its diagonals and cut along the creases to produce 4 identical right isosceles triangles (pieces 5, 6, 7, and 8).
  4. Place pieces 5, 6, 7, and 8 along each of the four outer edges of Square 1.
  5. Observe that the newly formed outer shape is a large, perfect square whose edges equal the diagonal of Square 1, and whose surface area is exactly twice Square 1!

2.2 Halving a Square — Reversing the Vedic Construction

Suppose we are given a large square of side length s and surface area s². How can we construct a square whose surface area is exactly half (s²/2)?

The Halving Side Fallacy: Why Side s/2 Gives One-Quarter (¼) Area

Just as before, halving the side length (s/2) does NOT halve the area.

Area of square with side (s/2) = (s/2) × (s/2) = s²/4 = ¼ of original area!

It takes 4 such smaller squares to fill the original square completely.

The Midpoint Inscription Method (Paper Folding Inward)

To produce a square of half area, we reverse Baudhāyana’s doubling construction:

  1. Locate the midpoints of the four sides of the square: P, Q, R, and S.
  2. Connect these midpoints consecutively to form an inscribed quadrilateral PQRS.
  3. Fold the four corner triangles inward along the creases PQ, QR, RS, and SP.
Rigorous Geometric Proof of the Inscribed Square PQRS

Claim 1: Area of PQRS is exactly half the area of the original square.
Let the original square ABCD have side length s. Each corner right triangle has perpendicular legs of length s/2.
Area of each corner triangle = ½ × (s/2) × (s/2) = s²/8.
Total area of the 4 corner triangles = 4 × (s²/8) = s²/2.
Area of inside shape PQRS = Total Area – 4 Corner Triangles = s² – s²/2 = s²/2 (Exactly Half!).

Claim 2: PQRS is an exact geometric square.
In right triangle △PBQ, since PB = BQ = s/2, the base angles are each 45°. Similarly, in △QCR, the base angles are 45°.
At vertex Q along the straight edge BC, the straight angle is 180°:
∠PQB + ∠PQR + ∠RQC = 180° ⇒ 45° + ∠PQR + 45° = 180° ⇒ ∠PQR = 90° By identical symmetry, all four interior angles are 90°. Furthermore, by SAS or Pythagoras on each corner, all four sides PQ = QR = RS = SP = √( (s/2)² + (s/2)² ) = s/√2. Hence, PQRS is a true square of area (s/√2)² = s²/2!

2.3 Hypotenuse of an Isosceles Right Triangle & The Mystery of √2

Consider an isosceles right triangle with equal perpendicular sides of length a = 1 unit. What is the length of its hypotenuse c?

Two such triangles combine to form a unit square PEAR of side 1 (Area = 1). The square constructed on the hypotenuse ER has double the area of PEAR:

Area(REST) = 2 × Area(PEAR) = 2 × 1 = 2 sq. units.

Since the area of any square of side c is c × c = c², we obtain the fundamental relation:

c² = 2 ⇒ c = √(2) units.

What is the Exact Nature of the Number √2? (Decimal Bounds)

Young students often ask: "Can we write √2 as a regular decimal number, like 1.4 or 1.41?" Let us investigate this by squeezing √2 between upper and lower bounds:

  • Integer Bounds: Since 1² = 1 and 2² = 4, and 2 lies between 1 and 4, we have:
    1 < √(2) < 2
  • One-Decimal Bounds (Tenths): Testing squared tenths:
    1.1² = 1.21, 1.2² = 1.44, 1.3² = 1.69, 1.4² = 1.96, 1.5² = 2.25
    Since 2 lies between 1.96 and 2.25:
    1.4 < √(2) < 1.5
  • Two-Decimal Bounds (Hundredths):
    1.41² = 1.9881 and 1.42² = 2.0164.
    Since 2 lies between 1.9881 and 2.0164:
    1.41 < √(2) < 1.42
  • Three-Decimal Bounds (Thousandths):
    1.414² = 1.999396 and 1.415² = 2.002225.
    Since 2 lies between 1.999396 and 2.002225:
    1.414 < √(2) < 1.415

Why √2 Can NEVER Be a Terminating Decimal

Could √2 ever end after some finite number of decimal digits (for example, after 100 digits)?

Mathematical Law: Non-Termination of √2

Suppose √2 were a terminating decimal. Then its representation must end in some non-zero final digit d in {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9}.

When any terminating decimal is squared, its last digit after the decimal point is determined entirely by d × d:

  • If d = 1 or 9, the square ends in 1.
  • If d = 2 or 8, the square ends in 4.
  • If d = 3 or 7, the square ends in 9.
  • If d = 4 or 6, the square ends in 6.
  • If d = 5, the square ends in 5.

Notice that the square of any terminating decimal ALWAYS ends in a non-zero digit (1, 4, 5, 6, or 9). It can NEVER end in pure zeroes like 2.0000...!

Therefore, no terminating decimal can ever square to exactly 2. The decimal expansion of √2 continues forever without stopping: √(2) = 1.4142135623730950488... (Non-terminating!)

Euclid’s Proof of Irrationality via Prime Factor Parity

In Euclid’s Elements (c. 300 BCE), a celebrated proof demonstrates why √2 can never be expressed as a fraction m/n where m and n are counting numbers.

The Prime Factor Parity Invariant Argument

Suppose for contradiction that √2 could be written as a fraction:

√(2) = (m / n) ⇒ 2 = (m^2 / n^2) ⇒ 2n^2 = m^2

Now recall the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: every counting number has a unique prime factorization. Furthermore:

  • When any number is squared, every prime factor in its factorization has its exponent doubled. Thus, every prime factor in a perfect square appears an EVEN number of times.
  • Therefore, in m² (the right side of our equation), the prime factor 2 must occur an EVEN number of times (e.g., 0, 2, 4, 6, ... times).
  • Similarly, in n², the prime factor 2 occurs an even number of times.
  • However, on the left side, we have 2 × n². Multiplying by 2 adds exactly one more factor of 2. An even number plus one is always ODD!
  • This means the prime factor 2 occurs an ODD number of times on the left side, but an EVEN number of times on the right side!

An odd number can NEVER equal an even number! This fundamental impossibility proves that our original assumption was completely false.

Conclusion: √2 cannot be expressed as a ratio of counting numbers. It is an irrational number.

2.4 Combining Two Different Squares — Baudhāyana’s General Theorem

Doubling a square taught us how to combine two identical squares into one larger square. But what if we wish to combine two squares of DIFFERENT sizes (say, side a and side b) to form a new single square whose area is the sum of both?

Baudhāyana Śulba-Sūtra (Verse 1.12 & Verse 2.1)

In Verse 1.12, Baudhāyana formulated the universal theorem:

"The area of the square produced by the diagonal is the sum of the areas of the squares produced by the two sides."

To make the proof undeniable, Baudhāyana provided an explicit geometric construction in Verse 2.1:

"To combine different squares, mark a rectangular portion of the larger square using a side of the smaller square. The diagonal of this rectangle is the side of a square that has area equal to the sum of the areas of the smaller squares."
Area of Outer Square: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² • Area of 4 Triangles: 4 × (½ × a × b) = 2ab ⇒ Area of Inscribed Square (c²) = (a + b)² – 2ab = a² + b²

Step-by-Step Algebraic and Angle Proof

  1. Why are the sides of the tilted inner shape all equal?
    The four right triangles T, U, X, and W are all congruent (each has perpendicular sides of length a and b). By congruence, their hypotenuses are identical in length: c.
  2. Why are all four corner angles exactly 90° (making it a square)?
    In each right triangle, the two acute angles sum to 90°. If one acute angle is x, the other is 90° – x.
    At each outer vertex along the straight boundary of 180°:
    x + Interior Angle + (90° – x) = 180° ⇒ 90° + Interior Angle = 180° ⇒ Interior Angle = 90°.
    Since all four sides equal c and all four angles equal 90°, the figure is guaranteed to be a true geometric square of area c²!
  3. Area Conservation Equation:

    Area(c^2) = (a + b)^2 - 2ab = (a^2 + 2ab + b^2) - 2ab = a^2 + b^2
    This completes the glorious proof: a² + b² = c²!

2.5 Right-Angled Triangles Having Integer Sidelengths (Baudhāyana Triples)

In many right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse is an irrational square root (such as √2, √13, √74). However, there exist special sets of three counting numbers (a, b, c) where all three sides are exact whole numbers (integers) satisfying a² + b² = c²!

In his Śulba-Sūtra (Verse 1.13), Baudhāyana cataloged an impressive list of these integer triples:

(3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25), (12, 35, 37), (15, 36, 39)

The Scaling Theorem for Triples

If (a, b, c) is a Baudhāyana triple, then multiplying each number by any positive integer k yields another valid Baudhāyana triple (ka, kb, kc):

(ka)^2 + (kb)^2 = k^2 a^2 + k^2 b^2 = k^2(a^2 + b^2) = k^2 c^2 = (kc)^2

For instance, taking the base triple (3, 4, 5):

  • For k = 2: (6, 8, 10) ⇒ 6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2.
  • For k = 3: (9, 12, 15) ⇒ 9^2 + 12^2 = 81 + 144 = 225 = 15^2.
  • For k = 10: (30, 40, 50).
  • For k = 100: (300, 400, 500).

This proving immediately that there are infinitely many Baudhāyana triples!

Primitive vs Non-Primitive Triples

  • Primitive Baudhāyana Triple: A triple (a, b, c) where the three integers have no common factor other than 1 (coprime, i.e., gcd(a, b, c) = 1). Examples: (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25).
  • Non-Primitive (Scaled) Triple: A triple obtained by scaling a primitive triple by k > 1. Examples: (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (10, 24, 26).

Generating Infinite Primitive Triples Using Consecutive Odd Numbers

Recall the famous pattern connecting odd numbers and perfect squares:

1 = 1^2, 1 + 3 = 2^2, 1 + 3 + 5 = 3^2, ... sum_{i=1}^n (2i - 1) = n^2

The sum of the first n odd numbers is n², and the sum of the first (n – 1) odd numbers is (n – 1)². The n-th odd number is 2n – 1. Thus:

(n - 1)^2 + (2n - 1) = n^2

Now comes the brilliant breakthrough: If the nth odd number (2n – 1) is itself a perfect square (say k²), then we have the sum of two squares equaling a third square!

2n - 1 = k^2 ⇒ 2n = k^2 + 1 ⇒ n = (k^2 + 1 / 2)
Leg 1 = k, Leg 2 = n - 1 = (k^2 - 1 / 2), Hypotenuse = n = (k^2 + 1 / 2)

Let us generate triples using odd values of k:

  • For k = 3 (k² = 9): n = (9 + 1)/2 = 5.
    Sides: a = 3, b = 5 - 1 = 4, c = 5 ⇒ (3, 4, 5).
  • For k = 5 (k² = 25): n = (25 + 1)/2 = 13.
    Sides: a = 5, b = 13 - 1 = 12, c = 13 ⇒ (5, 12, 13).
  • For k = 7 (k² = 49): n = (49 + 1)/2 = 25.
    Sides: a = 7, b = 25 - 1 = 24, c = 25 ⇒ (7, 24, 25).
  • For k = 9 (k² = 81): n = (81 + 1)/2 = 41.
    Sides: a = 9, b = 41 - 1 = 40, c = 41 ⇒ (9, 40, 41).
  • For k = 11 (k² = 121): n = (121 + 1)/2 = 61.
    Sides: a = 11, b = 61 - 1 = 60, c = 61 ⇒ (11, 60, 61).
Critical Insight: Why This Method Only Produces Triples with c – b = 1

Notice that in all triples produced by this odd-square method: c - b = n - (n - 1) = 1 The hypotenuse and the longer leg differ by exactly 1!

Because n and n – 1 are consecutive integers, they are always coprime (gcd(n, n–1) = 1). Therefore, this method ALWAYS yields primitive triples.

However, there exist primitive triples where the hypotenuse and the longer leg differ by more than 1 (such as (8, 15, 17) where 17 – 15 = 2, and (20, 21, 29) where 29 – 21 = 8). Those belong to the even-number triple family generated by Brahmagupta’s and Euclid’s generalized formulas.

2.6 A 350-Year Open Problem — Fermat’s Last Theorem

We have seen that there are infinitely many integer solutions to the equation a² + b² = c².

This remarkable fact led the brilliant French mathematician Pierre de Fermat (1601–1665) to ponder a profound question:

Fermat’s Famous Marginal Note (1637)

"Can a perfect cube ever be written as the sum of two perfect cubes? Can a fourth power ever be written as the sum of two fourth powers? In general, does the equation: x^n + y^n = z^n have any positive integer solutions when n > 2?"

In the margin of his copy of Diophantus’ ancient Greek textbook Arithmetica, Fermat wrote that no such solutions exist for any power greater than 2! He tantalizingly added:

"I have discovered a truly marvelous demonstration of this proposition that this margin is too narrow to contain."

Fermat died without publishing his proof. For more than 350 years, the world’s greatest mathematical minds (Euler, Sophie Germain, Gauss, Dirichlet, Kummer) attempted to prove what became known as Fermat’s Last Theorem, but all failed.

In 1963, a 10-year-old schoolboy in England named Andrew Wiles read a book about Fermat’s Last Theorem in his local library. Fascinated by the mystery, he resolved that he would be the one to solve it. Thirty-one years later, in 1994, Sir Andrew Wiles (now a professor at Princeton University) finally presented a complete, rigorous proof using advanced modern algebraic geometry and modular elliptic curves!

The area of any square of side x is given by the quadratic scaling law: Area = x². Scaling linear dimensions by a factor k scales the 2-dimensional area by k².

In-Text p.39 Euclid’s Proof of Irrationality: Why can √2 never be expressed as a fraction m/n?
1

Assume that √2 is a rational fraction m/n where m and n are counting numbers (positive integers).

In the unique prime factorization of any perfect square number, every prime factor appears with an EVEN exponent.

3

√(2) = (m / n) ⇒ 2 = (m^2 / n^2) ⇒ 2n^2 = m^2
  1. In m² (the right-hand side), the prime factor 2 must occur an EVEN number of times (e.g. 0, 2, 4, 6... times) because it is a square number.
  2. In n², the prime factor 2 also occurs an EVEN number of times.
  3. However, on the left-hand side, we have 2 × n². Multiplying by 2 increases the exponent of 2 by exactly 1:
    Exponent of 2 on LHS = 1 + Even = extbf{ODD}
  4. By the uniqueness of prime factorization, an integer cannot have an ODD number of prime factors of 2 on one side and an EVEN number on the other side!
4
Final Conclusion
Odd = Even is a complete contradiction. Therefore, √2 CANNOT be expressed as m/n; it is strictly irrational.

LAQ 4 • 5M Comprehensive Investigation of √2: (a) Prove that 1.414 < √2 < 1.415, (b) Prove √2 cannot be a terminating decimal, (c) Rigorous prime parity proof.
1
Part (a): Thousandths Squeeze Bounds [1.5 Marks]

We evaluate the exact squares of 1.414 and 1.415:

1.414^2 = 1.414 × 1.414 = 1.999396
1.415^2 = 1.415 × 1.415 = 2.002225

Since 1.999396 < 2 < 2.002225, taking square roots gives: 1.414 < √(2 < 1.415).

2
Part (b): Impossibility of Terminating Decimal [1.5 Marks]

Suppose √2 were a terminating decimal. Then it has a finite number of decimal places ending in a non-zero digit d in {1, 2, ..., 9}. When squared, the last decimal digit of the product is given by d² (mod 10):

  • If d = 1, 9: ends in 1.
  • If d = 2, 8: ends in 4.
  • If d = 3, 7: ends in 9.
  • If d = 4, 6: ends in 6.
  • If d = 5: ends in 5.

In all cases, the squared terminating decimal ends in a non-zero digit. It can NEVER equal 2.000...! Thus, the decimal representation must be strictly non-terminating.

3
Part (c): Euclid’s Prime Factor Parity Proof [2 Marks]

Assume for contradiction that √(2) = m / n for positive integers m, n.

2 = (m^2 / n^2) ⇒ 2n^2 = m^2
  • By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, every integer has a unique prime factorization.
  • In any perfect square (m² and n²), every prime factor appears with an EVEN power.
  • Right-hand side (m²): Power of 2 is 2k (EVEN).
  • Left-hand side (2n²): Power of 2 is 1 + 2j (ODD).
  • An odd number cannot equal an even number. This contradiction proves that √2 cannot be written as m/n.
4
Final Conclusion
√2 is strictly irrational, non-terminating, and squeezed tightly between 1.414 and 1.415.

Exam Defense

5 Common Student Blunders & Conceptual Traps

Analysis of thousands of student exam scripts reveals these five recurring blunders. Memorize how to avoid them:

Trap Alert 1: Confusing Side Doubling with Area Doubling
The Student Blunder:
Writing: "To construct a square with double the area of a square of side 5 cm, make a square of side 10 cm."
Why It Fails:

Area scales quadratically! A square of side 10 cm has area 10² = 100 cm², which is 4 times (quadruple) the original area (5² = 25 cm²), not 2 times!

The Golden Rule / Pro Tip:
To double the area, construct the new square on the diagonal of the original square: New Side = s√(2) = 5√(2) cm. Its area will be (5√(2)² = 50 cm² (exact doubling).
Trap Alert 2: The Square Root Addition Fallacy: √(a² + b²) ≠ a + b
The Student Blunder:
Writing: c = √(a^2 + b^2) = a + b. For legs 3 and 4, writing c = √(3^2 + 4^2) = 3 + 4 = 7!
Why It Fails:

Square root does NOT distribute over addition! √(9 + 16) = √(25) = 5, whereas 3 + 4 = 7. In fact, by the triangle inequality, the hypotenuse c must always be strictly less than the sum of the legs (c < a + b)!

The Golden Rule / Pro Tip:
Always evaluate the squares first, add the numbers together, and then take the square root: √(a² + b²). Never distribute the radical!
Trap Alert 3: Wrong Hypotenuse Assignment in Solving Unknown Leg
The Student Blunder:
When given short side 8 and hypotenuse 17, blindly adding: b = √(17^2 + 8^2) = √(289 + 64) = √(353)!
Why It Fails:

The hypotenuse is the sum of squares (c² = a² + b²). When finding an unknown leg, you must SUBTRACT: b² = c² - a²!

The Golden Rule / Pro Tip:
Identify the side opposite the 90° angle first. If finding the hypotenuse, ADD: c = √(a² + b²). If finding a leg, SUBTRACT: Leg = √(Hyp² - Other Leg²) (√(17² - 8²) = √(225) = 15).
Trap Alert 4: Using Full Diagonals Instead of Half-Diagonals for Rhombus Side
The Student Blunder:
Given diagonals 24 and 70, writing: Side = √(24^2 + 70^2) = √(576 + 4900) = √(5476) = 74!
Why It Fails:

The right triangle inside the rhombus is formed by the halves of the diagonals, because diagonals bisect each other! The legs are 24/2 = 12 and 70/2 = 35, not 24 and 70.

The Golden Rule / Pro Tip:
Always divide both rhombus diagonals by 2 before applying the theorem: Side = √(d₁/2)² + (d₂/2)²) = √(12² + 35²) = √(144 + 1225) = √(1369) = 37!
Trap Alert 5: Assuming All Baudhāyana Triples are Primitive
The Student Blunder:
Claiming that (6, 8, 10) is a primitive Baudhāyana triple because it forms a right triangle.
Why It Fails:

While (6, 8, 10) is a valid Baudhāyana triple, it is NOT primitive because gcd(6, 8, 10) = 2 ≠ 1. It is a scaled multiple of the primitive triple (3, 4, 5).

The Golden Rule / Pro Tip:
A triple is primitive if and only if gcd(a, b, c) = 1. Always divide by the greatest common divisor to reduce to the base primitive triple.
