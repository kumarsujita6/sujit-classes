The Foundational Nature of 4-Sided Polygons

The word Quadrilateral originates from two classical Latin roots: quadri (meaning "four") and latus (meaning "side"). In Euclidean plane geometry, a quadrilateral is defined as a closed two-dimensional figure enclosed by four non-collinear line segments intersecting only at their four endpoints (called vertices).

Unlike three-sided polygons (triangles), which are naturally rigid and cannot change shape without altering side lengths, four-sided figures are inherently flexible linkages. A simple hinged rectangular frame can wobble and shear into an infinite family of parallelograms. To lock a quadrilateral into structural rigidity, human engineers and carpenters must introduce a diagonal brace, triangulating the interior space into two rigid triangles!

2.1 Etymology, Elements & The Convex-Concave Distinction

In our study of plane geometry, we transition from three-sided polygons (triangles) to four-sided polygons. The word Quadrilateral comes from the Latin words quadri (four) and latus (sides). When four non-collinear points in a plane are connected in sequential order by straight line segments that intersect only at their endpoints, the resulting closed polygon is a quadrilateral.

Geometric Element Count Symbolic Notation Geometric Role & Meaning
Vertices 4 A, B, C, D The corner endpoints where two adjacent sides meet. Non-collinear points.
Sides 4 AB, BC, CD, DA The straight boundary line segments enclosing the interior region.
Interior Angles 4 ∠A, ∠B, ∠C, ∠D Angles formed inside the polygon between adjacent sides. Sum equals 360°.
Diagonals 2 AC, BD Line segments joining non-consecutive (opposite) vertices. They provide structural bracing.
Pairs of Opposite Sides 2 (AB, CD) and (BC, DA) Sides that do not share any common vertex.
Pairs of Adjacent Sides 4 (AB, BC), (BC, CD), (CD, DA), (DA, AB) Sides that share a common vertex.
Convex vs. Concave Quadrilaterals

Quadrilaterals are classified into two broad topological categories depending on the location of their diagonals and the measure of their interior angles:

  • Convex Quadrilateral: All four interior angles are strictly less than 180° (i.e., acute, right, or obtuse). Both diagonals (AC and BD) lie completely inside the figure. Any line segment joining two points inside the quadrilateral lies entirely inside the figure. All standard quadrilaterals studied in school (squares, rectangles, parallelograms, rhombuses, trapeziums, convex kites) are convex.
  • Concave Quadrilateral (Re-entrant): Exactly one interior angle is a reflex angle (greater than 180° but less than 360°). One of its diagonals lies entirely outside the interior region of the figure! A classic example is the arrowhead or dart shape. Even though one angle is reflex, the sum of all four interior angles is still strictly 360°!

2.2 Section 4.1: Rectangles and Squares (The Carpenter's Problem & Deductions 1 to 5)

The NCERT Ganita Prakash curriculum introduces quadrilaterals through a very practical hands-on problem faced by craftspeople: The Carpenter's Problem.

The Carpenter's Problem (Textbook Page 83)

A carpenter needs to put together two thin strips of wood so that when a thread is stretched taut around their endpoints, it forms a perfect rectangle. She already has one 8 cm long strip.

The Core Questions:

  1. What must be the length of the second wooden strip?
  2. Where along their lengths should the two strips be joined (pinned/hinged)?
  3. Does the angle at which the two strips cross matter?

To answer these questions, let us analyze the geometric model. The two wooden strips represent the diagonals of the quadrilateral ABCD, say diagonals AC and BD, intersecting at point O.

Figure 2.1: The Carpenter's Problem and the two cornerstone diagonal deductions for a rectangle.

Deduction 1: Why Diagonals of a Rectangle Have Equal Lengths

Let ABCD be a rectangle where all angles are 90° and opposite sides are equal (AB = CD and AD = BC). Consider the two overlapping right triangles sharing base AD: △ADC and △DAB.

  • AD = AD (Common base to both triangles)
  • ∠ADC = ∠DAB = 90° (Every angle of a rectangle is a right angle)
  • CD = BA (Opposite sides of a rectangle are equal)

Therefore, by the Side-Angle-Side (SAS) Congruence Criterion, we have:
△ADC ≅ △DAB.

Since corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal (CPCTC), their hypotenuses must be identical:
AC = BD.
Conclusion: The second strip must also be exactly 8 cm long!

Deduction 2: Why Diagonals Intersect at Their Exact Midpoints (Bisection)

To find where the carpenter must pin the two strips, consider the opposite triangles formed inside the rectangle: △AOB and △COD.

  • Since opposite sides of a rectangle are parallel (AB ∥ CD) with diagonal BD as a transversal, alternate interior angles are equal: ∠ABO = ∠CDO.
  • Similarly, with diagonal AC as transversal, alternate interior angles are equal: ∠BAO = ∠DCO.
  • Opposite sides are equal: AB = CD.

Therefore, by the Angle-Side-Angle (ASA) or Angle-Angle-Side (AAS) condition:
△AOB ≅ △COD.

By CPCTC, OA = OC and OB = OD. This proves that point O is the exact midpoint of both AC and BD. In geometric terminology, the diagonals bisect each other.
Conclusion: The carpenter must drill the hinge hole at the exact center (4 cm from each end) of both 8 cm strips!

Deduction 3: Does the Angle Between Diagonals Matter?

Suppose the carpenter opens the hinged 8 cm strips at an arbitrary angle, say x = 60° (or any angle x between 0° and 180°). Will the taut thread around the four endpoints ALWAYS form a rectangle?

Let us prove this algebraically:

  1. Because OA = OB = OC = OD = 4 cm, all four small interior triangles (△AOB, △BOC, △COD, △DOA) are isosceles triangles!
  2. Let the angle at the hinge be ∠AOB = x. Then vertically opposite angle ∠COD = x.
  3. The adjacent linear pair angles are ∠AOD = ∠BOC = 180° − x.
  4. In isosceles △AOB, the base angles are equal:
    a + a + x = 180° ⇒ 2a = 180° − x ⇒ a = 90° − (x/2).
  5. In adjacent isosceles △AOD, the base angles are equal:
    b + b + (180° − x) = 180° ⇒ 2b = x ⇒ b = x/2.
  6. Now, calculate the total vertex angle ∠BAD:
    ∠BAD = a + b = [90° − (x/2)] + [x/2] = 90°!

Notice that the variable x cancels out completely! No matter what angle x the carpenter chooses, every single corner angle is guaranteed to be exactly 90°.

The Four Fundamental Properties of a Rectangle
  1. All four interior angles are right angles (90°).
  2. Opposite sides are equal in length (AB = CD and AD = BC).
  3. Opposite sides are parallel to each other (AB ∥ CD and AD ∥ BC).
  4. The two diagonals are equal in length (AC = BD) and bisect each other (OA = OC = OB = OD).

Deductions 4 & 5: From Rectangles to Squares

Deduction 4: What if a quadrilateral simply has four 90° angles? Does it automatically have equal opposite sides?
Yes! In quadrilateral ABCD with ∠A = ∠B = ∠C = ∠D = 90°, drawing diagonal BD creates two right triangles: △BAD and △DCB. Because ∠A = ∠C = 90°, and alternate angles are equal (due to consecutive interior angles summing to 180°), △BAD ≅ △DCB by AAS. By CPCTC, AB = CD and AD = BC. Thus, a rectangle can be defined simply as: "A quadrilateral in which all four angles are 90°."

Deduction 5: What makes a Rectangle a Square?
A Square is a special rectangle where all four sides are equal (AB = BC = CD = DA). What happens to the angle between the diagonals in a square?

  • Consider adjacent triangles △AOB and △COB at the center O.
  • AB = CB (All four sides of a square are equal).
  • OA = OC (Diagonals bisect each other).
  • OB = OB (Common side).
  • Therefore, by SSS Congruence: △AOB ≅ △COB!
  • By CPCTC: ∠AOB = ∠COB.
  • Since ∠AOB and ∠COB form a linear pair along straight diagonal AC:
    ∠AOB + ∠COB = 180° ⇒ 2(∠AOB) = 180° ⇒ ∠AOB = 90°!

Furthermore, in right triangle △ADC where AD = DC, the diagonal creates an isosceles right triangle where the base angles are (180° − 90°)/2 = 45° each! Thus, the diagonals of a square bisect the 90° corner angles into two 45° angles.

2.3 Section 4.2: Angles in a Quadrilateral (The 360° Angle Sum Theorem)

Every student learns in Class 7 that the angles of a triangle sum to 180°. What is the universal law for a four-sided polygon?

Theorem: The Angle Sum Property of a Quadrilateral

Statement: The sum of the interior angle measures of any quadrilateral is strictly 360°.

Formal Geometric Proof:

  1. Let SOME be any arbitrary quadrilateral with interior angles ∠S, ∠O, ∠M, ∠E.
  2. Draw diagonal SM. This diagonal segments the quadrilateral into two distinct triangles: △SEM and △SOM.
  3. In △SEM, let the angles be ∠1, ∠2, ∠3. By the triangle angle sum theorem:
    ∠1 + ∠2 + ∠3 = 180° ...(Equation 1)
  4. In △SOM, let the angles be ∠4, ∠5, ∠6. By the triangle angle sum theorem:
    ∠4 + ∠5 + ∠6 = 180° ...(Equation 2)
  5. Adding Equation 1 and Equation 2:
    (∠1 + ∠2 + ∠3) + (∠4 + ∠5 + ∠6) = 180° + 180° = 360°.
  6. Regrouping the angles at the shared vertices S and M:
    (∠1 + ∠4) + ∠2 + (∠3 + ∠6) + ∠5 = 360°
    ∠S + ∠E + ∠M + ∠O = 360°.

Does this hold for Concave Quadrilaterals?
Yes! In textbook problem 10, consider arrowhead quadrilateral ADCB where vertex C is pushed inward, creating a reflex angle at C. Drawing diagonal BD still partitions the interior into two triangles: △ADB and △BDC. The angles of △ADB sum to 180° and the angles of △BDC sum to 180°. Adding them gives exactly 360°!

Why a Quadrilateral Cannot Have Exactly Three 90° Angles

Students often ask: "Can I draw a quadrilateral where three angles are 90° and the fourth angle is 80°?"

Mathematical Impossibility: By the Angle Sum Property:
∠1 + ∠2 + ∠3 + ∠4 = 360°
90° + 90° + 90° + ∠4 = 360°
270° + ∠4 = 360° ⇒ ∠4 = 360° − 270° = 90° strictly!
Therefore, if three angles of a quadrilateral are right angles, the fourth angle is mathematically forced to be 90° as well. It is physically impossible to have three right angles without the figure being a rectangle!

2.4 Section 4.3: Parallelograms (Deductions 6 to 8)

If we relax the strict condition that all angles must be 90°, but keep the condition that both pairs of opposite sides are parallel, we obtain the broad and versatile family of Parallelograms.

Figure 2.2: Mathematical proofs for Parallelograms (Deductions 6–8) and Rhombuses (Deductions 9–10).

Deduction 6: Parallelogram Angle Relationships

In parallelogram ABCD, opposite sides are parallel by definition: AB ∥ CD and AD ∥ BC.

  • Treat side AD as a transversal intersecting the parallel lines AB and CD. By the parallel line theorem, consecutive interior angles on the same side of a transversal are supplementary:
    ∠A + ∠D = 180° ⇒ ∠D = 180° − ∠A.
  • Treat side AB as a transversal intersecting parallel lines AD and BC:
    ∠A + ∠B = 180° ⇒ ∠B = 180° − ∠A.
  • Treat side CD as a transversal intersecting parallel lines AD and BC:
    ∠C + ∠D = 180° ⇒ ∠C = 180° − ∠D = 180° − (180° − ∠A) = ∠A!

Key Discoveries:
1. Adjacent angles are supplementary: (∠A + ∠B = 180°, ∠B + ∠C = 180°, ∠C + ∠D = 180°, ∠D + ∠A = 180°).
2. Opposite angles are equal: (∠A = ∠C and ∠B = ∠D).

Deductions 7 & 8: Parallelogram Sides and Diagonal Bisection

Deduction 7 (Opposite sides equal): Draw diagonal BD. Because AD ∥ BC, alternate interior angles ∠ADB = ∠CBD. Because AB ∥ CD, alternate interior angles ∠ABD = ∠CDB. With common side BD, △ABD ≅ △CDB by AAS/ASA. By CPCTC:
AB = CD and AD = BC.

Deduction 8 (Diagonals bisect each other): In opposite triangles △AOB and △COD, AB = CD, ∠OAB = ∠OCD, and ∠OBA = ∠ODC. By ASA congruence:
△AOB ≅ △COD ⇒ OA = OC and OB = OD.

2.5 Section 4.4: Rhombuses (Deductions 9 and 10)

A Rhombus is defined as a quadrilateral in which all four sides have equal length.

Since all sides are equal, opposite sides are equal, which automatically makes every rhombus a parallelogram! Therefore, a rhombus inherits all properties of a parallelogram (opposite angles equal, adjacent angles supplementary, diagonals bisect). But a rhombus possesses two extraordinary additional properties:

The Two Super-Properties of a Rhombus
  1. Diagonals Bisect the Vertex Angles: In rhombus GAME, diagonal AE divides ∠G AM into two equal angles (a = b = c = d), and diagonal GM divides ∠G and ∠M into equal halves.
  2. Diagonals Intersect at Exactly 90° (Perpendicular Bisectors): As proven in Deduction 10, adjacent triangles △GEO and △MEO are congruent by SSS. The linear pair angles at the center are equal: ∠GOE = ∠MOE = 180°/2 = 90°!

2.6 Section 4.5: Playing with Quadrilaterals (Geoboard & Triangle Joining)

In Section 4.5, students explore the structural behavior of quadrilaterals through physical experimentation:

  • Geoboard Exploration:
    1. Placing two equal rubber bands perpendicular to each other so they bisect at 90° forms a Square.
    2. Extending one diagonal equally on both ends (so diagonals are perpendicular, bisect each other, but have unequal lengths) produces a Rhombus.
    3. Extending one diagonal on only one end (so diagonals are perpendicular, but only one is bisected) produces a Kite!
  • Joining Triangles:
    1. Joining two identical equilateral triangles (side 8 cm) along their shared base produces a Rhombus with angles 60°, 120°, 60°, 120°.
    2. Joining two identical isosceles triangles (8 cm, 8 cm, 6 cm) along the 6 cm base yields a Rhombus (all 4 sides = 8 cm). Joining them along an 8 cm leg yields a Kite (adjacent sides 8 cm, 8 cm and 6 cm, 6 cm) or a Parallelogram if one triangle is flipped!
    3. Joining two identical scalene triangles (6 cm, 9 cm, 12 cm) along corresponding equal sides produces a Parallelogram (by translation/rotation) or a Kite (by reflection)!

2.7 Section 4.6: Kites and Trapeziums (Isosceles Trapezium RHS Proof)

The Kite: Anatomy & Symmetry

A Kite is a quadrilateral with two distinct pairs of adjacent sides of equal length (AB = BC and CD = DA).

Core Properties of a Kite:

  • One Axis of Reflection Symmetry: The main diagonal BD acts as a line of mirror symmetry: △ABD ≅ △CBD by SSS.
  • Perpendicular Diagonals: Diagonals intersect at right angles: AC ⊥ BD.
  • Only ONE Diagonal is Bisected: The symmetry diagonal BD bisects the cross diagonal AC (i.e., AO = OC). However, AC does NOT bisect BD! (OB ≠ OD in general).
  • One Pair of Equal Opposite Angles: The angles between the unequal sides are equal: ∠BAD = ∠BCD. The other pair of opposite angles are unequal: ∠ABC ≠ ∠ADC.

Figure 2.3: Anatomy of a Kite. Note that the main diagonal BD bisects the cross diagonal AC, but AC does not bisect BD.

The Trapezium & Isosceles Trapezium

A Trapezium is defined as a quadrilateral with at least one pair of parallel opposite sides.

  • Property 1 (Co-interior Angles): If PQ ∥ SR, then consecutive angles along each non-parallel leg add up to 180°:
    ∠S + ∠P = 180° and ∠R + ∠Q = 180°.
  • Isosceles Trapezium: When the non-parallel legs are equal in length (UX = VW), the figure becomes an Isosceles Trapezium.

Figure 2.4: Rigorous RHS congruence proof showing that base angles are equal in an isosceles trapezium.

Proof: In an Isosceles Trapezium, Base Angles are Equal (Property 2)

Let UVWX be an isosceles trapezium with UV ∥ XW and UX = VW.

  1. Drop perpendiculars from top vertices X and W down to the base UV, meeting UV at points Y and Z respectively:
    XY ⊥ UV and WZ ⊥ UV.
  2. Since XW ∥ YZ and both altitudes are perpendicular to the parallel lines, XY and WZ are parallel and represent the perpendicular distance between two parallel lines. Thus, XY = WZ, and XWZY is a rectangle.
  3. Now compare the two outside right-angled triangles: △UXY and △VWZ:
    • ∠UYX = ∠VZW = 90° (Right angle)
    • UX = VW (Hypotenuses are equal, given by definition of isosceles trapezium)
    • XY = WZ (Perpendicular distance between parallel lines)
  4. Therefore, by the Right angle-Hypotenuse-Side (RHS) Congruence Criterion:
    △UXY ≅ △VWZ!
  5. By CPCTC, the corresponding base angles are equal:
    ∠U = ∠V.
  6. Because consecutive co-interior angles sum to 180°:
    ∠X = 180° − ∠U and ∠W = 180° − ∠V. Since ∠U = ∠V, we also have ∠X = ∠W!

Conclusion: In an isosceles trapezium, both pairs of base angles are equal (∠U = ∠V and ∠X = ∠W), and by SSS or SAS congruence, its diagonals are also equal: UW = VX!

Textbook Page 83: The Carpenter's Problem (Strip Length & Hinge Location)

Textbook Prompt: A carpenter needs to put together two thin strips of wood so that when a thread is passed through their endpoints, it forms a rectangle. She already has one 8 cm long strip. What should be the length of the other strip? Where should they both be joined?

Step 1: Given Data & Geometric Objective

We are given a wooden strip of length 8 cm representing one diagonal (AC) of a quadrilateral. The goal is to determine the length of the second strip (diagonal BD) and the exact point O where the two strips must be pinned together such that quadrilateral ABCD is guaranteed to be a rectangle.

Step 2: Core Geometric Properties of a Rectangle

By the definition and properties of a rectangle: (1) Diagonals of a rectangle are equal in length (AC = BD). (2) Diagonals bisect each other (OA = OC and OB = OD).

Step 3: Step-by-Step Deduction & Calculation

1. Length of Second Strip: Since diagonals of a rectangle are equal, the second strip must have the same length as the first: Length of second strip = 8 cm.
2. Hinge Location: Since the diagonals bisect each other, the pivot hole must be drilled at the exact midpoint of both strips: Midpoint = 8 cm ÷ 2 = 4 cm from either end.
3. When the two 8 cm strips are pinned at their 4 cm midpoints, the distance from O to all four endpoints is equal: OA = OB = OC = OD = 4 cm.

Step 4: Verification & Consistency Check

Let the angle between the strips be x. In the four resulting isosceles triangles, the base angles are a = 90° − x/2 and b = x/2. Each vertex angle is a + b = 90° − x/2 + x/2 = 90°. Since all four angles are 90°, ABCD is strictly a rectangle for any arbitrary opening angle.

Step 5: Final Result & Key Takeaway

Result: The second strip must be exactly 8 cm long, and both strips must be joined at their exact midpoints (4 cm from each end).

Textbook Page 84: Deduction 1 (Why Diagonals of a Rectangle Are Equal)

Textbook Prompt: Prove using triangle congruence that the diagonals of a rectangle always have the same length.

Step 1: Given Data & Geometric Objective

Let ABCD be a rectangle where all angles are 90° and opposite sides are equal (AB = CD and AD = BC). We need to prove that diagonal AC = diagonal BD.

Step 2: Congruence Criterion Selected

Consider the two overlapping right-angled triangles sitting on the base AD: △ADC and △DAB. We apply the Side-Angle-Side (SAS) congruence criterion.

Step 3: Formal Step-by-Step Proof

In △ADC and △DAB:
• AD = AD (Common base side)
• ∠ADC = ∠DAB = 90° (Angles of a rectangle are right angles)
• CD = BA (Opposite sides of a rectangle are equal)
By the SAS Congruence Criterion, △ADC ≅ △DAB.

Step 4: CPCTC Deduction

Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are congruent (CPCTC). The hypotenuse of △ADC is AC, and the hypotenuse of △DAB is BD. Therefore, AC = BD.

Step 5: Final Conclusion

The diagonals of any rectangle are always equal in length. Hence, if one diagonal is 8 cm, the other must be 8 cm.

Textbook Page 84: Deduction 2 (Why Diagonals of a Rectangle Bisect Each Other)

Textbook Prompt: What is the point of intersection of the two diagonals? Prove that O is the midpoint of AC and BD.

Step 1: Given Data & Geometric Objective

In rectangle ABCD, diagonals AC and BD intersect at O. We must prove OA = OC and OB = OD.

Step 2: Congruence Tool

We compare opposite interior triangles: △AOB and △COD, using parallel line transversal properties (AB ∥ CD).

Step 3: Formal Step-by-Step Proof

In △AOB and △COD:
• AB = CD (Opposite sides of rectangle are equal)
• ∠OAB = ∠OCD (Alternate interior angles with transversal AC)
• ∠OBA = ∠ODC (Alternate interior angles with transversal BD)
By the ASA (or AAS) Congruence Criterion, △AOB ≅ △COD.

Step 4: CPCTC Deduction

By CPCTC: OA = OC (O is midpoint of AC) and OB = OD (O is midpoint of BD).

Step 5: Final Conclusion

The diagonals of a rectangle bisect each other at their point of intersection.

Textbook Pages 86–87: Deduction 3 (Generalizing the Angle Between Diagonals)

Textbook Prompt: If the diagonals are equal and bisect each other, but intersect at an arbitrary angle x (such as 60°), prove that the quadrilateral formed is always a rectangle.

Step 1: Given Data & Model

Diagonals AC and BD satisfy AC = BD and OA = OC = OB = OD. The acute angle between diagonals at O is ∠AOB = x.

Step 2: Isosceles Triangle Angle Sum Law

Because OA = OB and OA = OD, △AOB and △AOD are isosceles triangles. The sum of angles in any triangle is 180°.

Step 3: Algebraic Calculation

1. In △AOB: Base angles are equal to a. Thus, a + a + x = 180° ⇒ 2a = 180° − x ⇒ a = 90° − (x/2).
2. In △AOD: Angle at center is 180° − x (linear pair). Base angles are equal to b. Thus, b + b + (180° − x) = 180° ⇒ 2b = x ⇒ b = x/2.
3. Vertex angle ∠BAD is the sum of a and b: ∠BAD = a + b = [90° − (x/2)] + [x/2] = 90°.

Step 4: Consistency Across All Vertices

By identical reasoning at vertices B, C, and D, each vertex angle evaluates to 90°. The variable x cancels out in every single corner!

Step 5: Final Conclusion

Regardless of the angle x chosen between the diagonals, if two diagonals of equal length bisect each other, the resulting quadrilateral is always a rectangle.

Textbook Pages 88–90: Deduction 4 (Quadrilateral with Four 90° Angles)

Textbook Prompt: What is the shape of a quadrilateral with all four angles equal to 90°? Prove that its opposite sides must be equal.

Step 1: Given Data & Objective

Given quadrilateral ABCD with ∠A = ∠B = ∠C = ∠D = 90°. We must prove AB = CD and AD = BC.

Step 2: Transversal Parallelism Rule

Since ∠A + ∠B = 90° + 90° = 180°, consecutive interior angles on the same side of transversal AB sum to 180°. Hence, AD ∥ BC. Similarly, ∠A + ∠D = 180°, so AB ∥ CD.

Step 3: Triangle Congruence Proof

Draw diagonal BD. In △BAD and △DCB:
• ∠BAD = ∠DCB = 90° (Given)
• ∠ABD = ∠CDB (Alternate interior angles as AB ∥ CD)
• BD = BD (Common hypotenuse)
By the AAS Congruence Criterion, △BAD ≅ △DCB.

Step 4: CPCTC Deduction

By CPCTC: AB = CD and AD = BC. Thus, opposite sides are equal.

Step 5: Definition Simplification

A rectangle can be defined simply as: A quadrilateral in which all four angles are 90°.

Textbook Pages 92–93: Deduction 5 (Why Diagonals of a Square Intersect at 90°)

Textbook Prompt: In a square, what should be the angle formed by the diagonals at their point of intersection?

Step 1: Given Data & Objective

Let ABCD be a square where AB = BC = CD = DA and ∠A = ∠B = ∠C = ∠D = 90°. Diagonals AC and BD bisect at O. We must prove ∠AOB = 90°.

Step 2: SSS Congruence Selection

Compare adjacent interior triangles meeting at O: △AOB and △COB.

Step 3: Congruence Proof

In △AOB and △COB:
• AB = CB (All sides of a square are equal)
• OA = OC (Diagonals bisect each other)
• OB = OB (Common shared side)
By SSS Congruence: △AOB ≅ △COB.

Step 4: Linear Pair Angle Calculation

By CPCTC: ∠AOB = ∠COB. Because AC is a straight line segment, ∠AOB and ∠COB form a linear pair:
∠AOB + ∠COB = 180° ⇒ 2(∠AOB) = 180° ⇒ ∠AOB = 90°.

Step 5: Final Result

The diagonals of a square are equal in length and bisect each other at right angles (90°).

Textbook Pages 96–97: Deduction 6 (Parallelogram Angles Proof)

Textbook Prompt: Prove that in any parallelogram, adjacent angles are supplementary (add up to 180°) and opposite angles are equal.

Step 1: Given Data & Objective

Parallelogram ABCD has AB ∥ CD and AD ∥ BC. We must prove ∠A + ∠B = 180°, ∠A + ∠D = 180°, and ∠A = ∠C, ∠B = ∠D.

Step 2: Transversal Angle Law

When two parallel lines are cut by a transversal, the sum of consecutive interior angles on the same side of the transversal equals 180°.

Step 3: Mathematical Deduction

1. With AB ∥ CD and transversal AD: ∠A + ∠D = 180° ⇒ ∠D = 180° − ∠A.
2. With AD ∥ BC and transversal AB: ∠A + ∠B = 180° ⇒ ∠B = 180° − ∠A.
3. With AD ∥ BC and transversal CD: ∠C + ∠D = 180° ⇒ ∠C = 180° − ∠D = 180° − (180° − ∠A) = ∠A.
4. With AB ∥ CD and transversal BC: ∠B + ∠C = 180° ⇒ ∠D = ∠B.

Step 4: Verification

Sum of all four angles: ∠A + ∠B + ∠C + ∠D = ∠A + (180° − ∠A) + ∠A + (180° − ∠A) = 360°, satisfying the Angle Sum Property.

Step 5: Final Result

In every parallelogram, adjacent angles add up to 180° and opposite angles are equal.

The opposite sides of every parallelogram are equal in length.

Opposite angles are equal. Therefore, the four angles are 80°, 100°, 80°, 100°.

SAQ 2 (2 Marks): Rhombus with Perimeter 52 cm and Diagonal 10 cm

Question: The perimeter of a rhombus is 52 cm, and one of its diagonals is 10 cm. Find the length of the second diagonal.

Step 1: Side Length Calculation

Perimeter = 4a = 52 cm ⇒ Side a = 52 ÷ 4 = 13 cm.

Step 2: Half Diagonal Calculation

Given d₁ = 10 cm ⇒ Half diagonal = d₁ / 2 = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 cm.

Step 3: Pythagoras Theorem at 90° Intersection

Diagonals intersect perpendicularly. In right △AOB:
(d₂ / 2)² = a² − (d₁ / 2)² = 13² − 5² = 169 − 25 = 144.
d₂ / 2 = √144 = 12 cm.

Step 4: Final Result

Total length of second diagonal d₂ = 2 × 12 cm = 24 cm.

SAQ 3 (3 Marks): Quadrilateral Angles in Ratio 3 : 5 : 9 : 13

Question: The four angles of a quadrilateral are in the ratio 3 : 5 : 9 : 13. Find the measure of each angle. Is this quadrilateral convex or concave?

Step 1: Algebraic Setup

Let the angles be 3x, 5x, 9x, and 13x.

Step 2: Angle Sum Property

3x + 5x + 9x + 13x = 360° ⇒ 30x = 360° ⇒ x = 12°.

Step 3: Angle Values

• ∠1 = 3 × 12° = 36°
• ∠2 = 5 × 12° = 60°
• ∠3 = 9 × 12° = 108°
• ∠4 = 13 × 12° = 156°

Step 4: Convexity Verification

Since every interior angle is strictly less than 180° (none is reflex), the quadrilateral is strictly Convex.

SAQ 4 (3 Marks): Angle Bisectors of Adjacent Angles in a Parallelogram

Question: In a parallelogram ABCD, the bisectors of consecutive angles ∠A and ∠B intersect at point P. Prove that ∠APB = 90°.

Step 1: Given Data

Parallelogram ABCD with AP bisecting ∠A and BP bisecting ∠B.

Step 2: Adjacent Angle Property

In a parallelogram, consecutive adjacent angles sum to 180°: ∠A + ∠B = 180°.

Step 3: Halving the Equation

Dividing the equation by 2:
½(∠A) + ½(∠B) = 180° ÷ 2 = 90°.
Since AP and BP are angle bisectors: ∠PAB = ½(∠A) and ∠PBA = ½(∠B).
Therefore, ∠PAB + ∠PBA = 90°.

Step 4: Triangle Angle Sum

In △APB: ∠PAB + ∠PBA + ∠APB = 180° ⇒ 90° + ∠APB = 180° ⇒ ∠APB = 90°. (Hence proved!)

SAQ 5 (3 Marks): Kite Diagonals with Sides 5 cm and 12 cm

Question: In kite ABCD, adjacent sides AB = AD = 5 cm and BC = CD = 12 cm. If the cross diagonal BD = 6 cm, find the length of the main diagonal AC.

Step 1: Intersection Geometry

The main diagonal AC is the perpendicular bisector of cross diagonal BD at O. Thus, OB = OD = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 cm, and ∠AOB = ∠COB = 90°.

Step 2: Calculate OA

In right △AOB: OA² = AB² − OB² = 5² − 3² = 25 − 9 = 16 ⇒ OA = 4 cm.

Step 3: Calculate OC

In right △COB: OC² = BC² − OB² = 12² − 3² = 144 − 9 = 135 ⇒ OC = √135 = 3√15 ≈ 11.62 cm.

Step 4: Total Diagonal Length

Total AC = OA + OC = 4 + 3√15 ≈ 15.62 cm.

SAQ 6 (3 Marks): Height & Area of Isosceles Trapezium

Question: An isosceles trapezium has parallel sides of lengths 10 cm and 22 cm, and non-parallel legs of 10 cm each. Find its height and area.

Step 1: Partitioning the Base

Let parallel bases be a = 10 cm and b = 22 cm. Drop altitudes from top vertices to bottom base. The horizontal projection of each leg is: x = (22 − 10) ÷ 2 = 12 ÷ 2 = 6 cm.

Step 2: Calculate Height using Pythagoras

In the outer right triangle: h² = Leg² − x² = 10² − 6² = 100 − 36 = 64 ⇒ Height h = √64 = 8 cm.

Step 3: Area Computation

Area = ½ × (Sum of Parallel Bases) × Height = ½ × (10 + 22) × 8 = ½ × 32 × 8 = 128 cm².

5.4 Long Answer Questions with Step-by-Step Marking Schemes (5M - 4 Items)

LAQ 1 (5 Marks): Angle Sum Theorem Proof & Concave Extension

Question: State and prove the Angle Sum Property of a quadrilateral. Does this theorem hold true for a concave (dart-shaped) quadrilateral? Justify with a diagram and rigorous mathematical proof.

Marking Scheme Breakdown [Total: 5 Marks]

• Statement of Theorem: 1 Mark
• Construction & Convex Proof: 2 Marks
• Concave Quadrilateral Identification & Proof: 2 Marks

Part 1: Statement & Convex Proof

Statement: The sum of the interior angle measures of any planar quadrilateral is 360°.
Proof: Let ABCD be a convex quadrilateral. Draw diagonal AC. This divides ABCD into △ABC and △CDA.
In △ABC: ∠CAB + ∠B + ∠BCA = 180° ...(1)
In △CDA: ∠CAD + ∠D + ∠DCA = 180° ...(2)
Adding (1) and (2):
(∠CAB + ∠CAD) + ∠B + (∠BCA + ∠DCA) + ∠D = 180° + 180° = 360°
⇒ ∠A + ∠B + ∠C + ∠D = 360°.

Part 2: Concave Quadrilateral Proof

Let ADCB be a concave quadrilateral where vertex C forms an interior reflex angle (> 180°).
Draw internal diagonal BD connecting vertices B and D. This diagonal lies entirely inside the figure and partitions the concave quadrilateral into two triangles: △ADB and △BDC.
In △ADB: ∠DAB + ∠ABD + ∠ADB = 180°
In △BDC: ∠CBD + ∠BDC + ∠BCD = 180°
Adding both equations:
∠DAB + (∠ABD + ∠CBD) + (∠ADB + ∠BDC) + ∠BCD = 180° + 180° = 360°
⇒ ∠A + ∠B + ∠D + ∠C = 360°.
Conclusion: The theorem holds universally for ALL quadrilaterals, convex or concave!

LAQ 2 (5 Marks): Necessary and Sufficient Diagonal Bisection Theorem

Question: Prove that a quadrilateral is a parallelogram if and only if its diagonals bisect each other.

Marking Scheme Breakdown [Total: 5 Marks]

• Part 1: Proving Parallelogram ⇒ Diagonals Bisect: 2.5 Marks
• Part 2: Proving Diagonals Bisect ⇒ Parallelogram (Converse): 2.5 Marks

Part 1: Given Parallelogram ABCD, Prove OA = OC and OB = OD

In parallelogram ABCD, AB ∥ CD and AB = CD (opposite sides). Diagonals AC and BD intersect at O.
In △AOB and △COD:
• AB = CD (Opposite sides of parallelogram are equal)
• ∠OAB = ∠OCD (Alternate interior angles, AB ∥ CD with transversal AC)
• ∠OBA = ∠ODC (Alternate interior angles, AB ∥ CD with transversal BD)
By ASA Congruence: △AOB ≅ △COD.
By CPCTC: OA = OC and OB = OD. (Diagonals bisect each other).

Part 2: Converse — Given OA = OC and OB = OD, Prove ABCD is Parallelogram

In △AOB and △COD:
• OA = OC (Given)
• OB = OD (Given)
• ∠AOB = ∠COD (Vertically opposite angles)
By SAS Congruence: △AOB ≅ △COD.
By CPCTC: AB = CD and ∠OAB = ∠OCD.
Since alternate interior angles ∠OAB and ∠OCD are equal, AB ∥ CD.
Since one pair of opposite sides (AB and CD) is BOTH equal and parallel, quadrilateral ABCD is guaranteed to be a Parallelogram!

LAQ 3 (5 Marks): Midpoint Quadrilateral of a Rhombus is a Rectangle

Question: ABCD is a rhombus. P, Q, R, and S are the midpoints of sides AB, BC, CD, and DA respectively. Prove that the quadrilateral PQRS is a rectangle.

Marking Scheme Breakdown [Total: 5 Marks]

• Midpoint Theorem application to show PQRS is a parallelogram: 2 Marks
• Using Rhombus Diagonals at 90°: 1.5 Marks
• Proving Corner Angle of PQRS = 90°: 1.5 Marks

Step 1: Apply Midpoint Theorem

Draw diagonal AC of rhombus ABCD.
In △ABC, P is midpoint of AB and Q is midpoint of BC. By Midpoint Theorem: PQ ∥ AC and PQ = ½ AC.
In △ADC, S is midpoint of AD and R is midpoint of CD. By Midpoint Theorem: SR ∥ AC and SR = ½ AC.
Therefore, PQ ∥ SR and PQ = SR. Since one pair of opposite sides is equal and parallel, PQRS is a parallelogram.

Step 2: Prove One Angle of PQRS is 90°

Draw diagonal BD of rhombus ABCD. By the properties of a rhombus, diagonals are perpendicular: AC ⊥ BD.
Let AC and BD intersect at O. Let PQ intersect BD at M, and QR intersect AC at N.
• In quadrilateral OMQN:
MQ ∥ ON (since BD ∥ QR by Midpoint theorem in △BCD)
NQ ∥ OM (since AC ∥ PQ)
Therefore, OMQN is a parallelogram!
• In parallelogram OMQN, opposite angles are equal: ∠MQN = ∠MON.
Since AC ⊥ BD, ∠MON = 90°. Therefore, ∠MQN = ∠PQR = 90°.

Step 3: Final Conclusion

A parallelogram with one right angle is a Rectangle. Hence, PQRS is strictly a rectangle!

LAQ 4 (5 Marks): The Carpenter's Practical Geometry Proof

Question: A furniture maker wants to construct a perfectly square window frame of diagonal 10 cm using two wooden strips. Explain using Deductions 1, 2, 3, and 5 how the carpenter can guarantee that the completed frame is an exact square without measuring a single corner angle with a protractor.

Marking Scheme Breakdown [Total: 5 Marks]

• Strip length & bisection deduction: 1.5 Marks
• Perpendicularity condition (Deduction 5): 1.5 Marks
• Proof of corner angles = 90° (Deduction 3): 1 Mark
• Proof of equal side lengths (SSS / Pythagoras): 1 Mark

Step-by-Step Practical Procedure

1. Cut Two Equal Strips (Deduction 1): Cut two rigid wooden strips of exactly 10 cm each.
2. Locate Midpoints (Deduction 2): Measure 5 cm from either end of both strips and drill a pivot hole at the exact midpoint O. Pin the strips together so that OA = OB = OC = OD = 5 cm.
3. Set Perpendicular Orientation at 90° (Deduction 5): Adjust the opening angle until the strips cross at exactly 90°. (This can be verified by checking that the four corner thread lengths are identical, or using a carpenter's 3-4-5 framing square).
4. Connect Endpoints: Fasten the four outer frame battens to endpoints A, B, C, D.
5. Mathematical Guarantee:
• By Deduction 3: Since OA = OB = OC = OD, each vertex angle is (90° − 90°/2) + 90°/2 = 90°.
• By Deduction 10 & Pythagoras: Each side = √(5² + 5²) = √50 ≈ 7.07 cm. All four sides are equal.
The resulting frame is mathematically guaranteed to be a Square!

• In bottom right △COB: OC = √(BC² − OB²) = √(17² − 8²) = √(289 − 64) = √225 = 15 m.

Area of Kite = ½ × d₁ × d₂ = ½ × AC × BD = ½ × 21 m × 16 m = 168 m².

Question 3: Determine the perimeter and boundary piping length

Perimeter = AB + BC + CD + DA = 10 + 17 + 17 + 10 = 54 m.

Question 4: Which diagonal acts as the structural line of symmetry?

Spine AC acts as the line of symmetry because it bisects the canopy into two congruent mirror halves (△ABC ≅ △ADC).

PYQ 5 (Exemplar Challenge): Proving Square from Diagonals

Question: Prove that if the diagonals of a quadrilateral are equal in length and bisect each other at right angles, then the quadrilateral is a square.

Step 1: Given Conditions

Quadrilateral ABCD has: (1) AC = BD, (2) OA = OC and OB = OD, (3) AC ⊥ BD (∠AOB = 90°).

Step 2: Prove Parallelogram

Since diagonals bisect each other (OA = OC, OB = OD), ABCD is a Parallelogram.

Step 3: Prove Rhombus

In △AOB and △COB: OA = OC, OB = OB, ∠AOB = ∠COB = 90°. By SAS Congruence: △AOB ≅ △COB ⇒ AB = BC. A parallelogram with equal adjacent sides is a Rhombus.

Step 4: Prove Rectangle & Square

In △ABC and △BAD: AB = AB, BC = AD, AC = BD (given). By SSS Congruence: △ABC ≅ △BAD ⇒ ∠ABC = ∠BAD. Since adjacent angles in a parallelogram sum to 180°: ∠ABC + ∠BAD = 180° ⇒ 2(∠ABC) = 180° ⇒ ∠ABC = 90°.
Since ABCD is a rhombus with a 90° angle, it is strictly a Square!

Section 6: Exam Hall Trap Alerts (5 Student Blunders) & 15 Revision Points

Arm yourself against the most frequent negative-marking traps and review the 15 golden conceptual takeaways before stepping into the examination hall.

The 5 Most Fatal Student Blunders in Quadrilateral Exams

Blunder 1: The "Perpendicular Diagonals = Rhombus" Fallacy

Students read "Diagonals intersect at 90°" and automatically write that the quadrilateral must be a Rhombus or Square.

Fatal Exam Mistake:
"Since diagonals AC ⊥ BD, ABCD is definitely a rhombus." (Loses marks!)
Topper's Precision:
"A Kite also has perpendicular diagonals (AC ⊥ BD), but its adjacent sides are unequal in pairs and its diagonals do not mutually bisect! A quadrilateral is only a rhombus if the diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other."
Blunder 2: The "Equal Diagonals = Square" Fallacy

Assuming that equal diagonals (AC = BD) automatically guarantee a four-sided figure is a square.

Fatal Exam Mistake:
"The diagonals are equal (8 cm each), so the shape is a square."
Topper's Precision:
"Equal bisecting diagonals define a Rectangle! Furthermore, an Isosceles Trapezium also has equal diagonals without even being a parallelogram! For a square, diagonals must be equal, bisecting, AND perpendicular (90°)."
Blunder 3: Confusing Adjacent and Opposite Angles in Parallelograms

Students accidentally equate adjacent angles instead of making them supplementary, or sum opposite angles to 180°.

Fatal Exam Mistake:
Writing: "Adjacent angles are equal, so (2x + 10) = (3x − 20)." (Zero marks!)
Topper's Precision:
Remember the mnemonic: O-E & A-S!
• Opposite angles are EQUAL: ∠A = ∠C and ∠B = ∠D.
• Adjacent angles are SUPPLEMENTARY: ∠A + ∠B = 180° and ∠B + ∠C = 180°.
Blunder 4: The Concave Quadrilateral Angle Sum Misconception

Believing that the 360° interior angle sum theorem fails when a quadrilateral is dart-shaped or has a reflex angle.

Fatal Exam Mistake:
"Angle sum is 360° only for convex shapes; for concave dart shapes it is different."
Topper's Precision:
"Drawing internal diagonal BD always partitions ANY four-sided polygon into two non-overlapping triangles. Each triangle contributes 180°, so the sum is strictly 360° for every quadrilateral without exception!"
Blunder 5: Over-Generalizing Kite Symmetry

Assuming that in a kite, both pairs of opposite angles are equal or both diagonals bisect each other.

Fatal Exam Mistake:
"In kite ABCD, ∠B = ∠D and OA = OC, OB = OD."
Topper's Precision:
"A kite has only ONE line of symmetry! Only the angles between unequal sides are equal (∠A = ∠C). The other pair is unequal (∠B ≠ ∠D). The main diagonal bisects the cross diagonal (AO = OC), but the cross diagonal does NOT bisect the main diagonal (OB ≠ OD)!"

15 High-Yield Golden Revision Points for Toppers

1
Etymology & Definition: Quadri (four) + latus (side). A closed 2D polygon bounded by 4 non-collinear segments with 4 vertices, 4 sides, 4 angles, and 2 diagonals.
2
Angle Sum Property: Interior angles always sum to 360° [proof: (4 − 2) × 180° = 360° via diagonal triangulation]. Valid for convex & concave quads.
3
The Rigidity Principle: Unlike triangles (which are rigid), four-sided frames are flexible linkages. A diagonal brace triangulates the interior to provide structural rigidity.
4
Carpenter's Problem Law: If two equal strips bisect each other at any arbitrary angle x, the taut thread joining their tips always forms a Rectangle.
5
Minimal Rectangle Definition: A rectangle can simply be defined as a quadrilateral in which all four angles are 90° (opposite sides are then proven equal via AAS).
6
Square Perfection: A square is the unique regular quadrilateral. It is simultaneously a rectangle, a rhombus, a parallelogram, a trapezium, and a kite.
7
Square Diagonal Signature: Diagonals of a square are equal, bisect each other at 90°, and bisect vertex angles into 45° each.
8
Parallelogram Definition: A quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel (AB ∥ CD and AD ∥ BC).
9
Parallelogram Angle & Side Rules: Opposite sides are equal (AB = CD), opposite angles are equal (∠A = ∠C), and adjacent angles are supplementary (∠A + ∠B = 180°).
10
Minimal Parallelogram Condition: If ONE pair of opposite sides of a quadrilateral is BOTH equal and parallel, it is guaranteed to be a parallelogram!
11
Rhombus Equilateral Nature: All 4 sides equal. Diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other and bisect the opposite vertex angles.
12
Trapezium Fundamentals: At least one pair of parallel opposite sides. Consecutive angles along non-parallel legs sum to 180° (∠P + ∠S = 180°).
