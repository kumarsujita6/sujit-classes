2.1 The Dawn of Sovereign India: Self-Governance & Representation

Before 15 August 1947, the people of India lived under British colonial subjugation. Foreign rulers enacted laws in distant London or through colonial councils that did not reflect the needs, dignity, or aspirations of Indian citizens. Freedom was not a gift; it was earned through decades of immense sacrifices by freedom fighters, farmers, women, workers, and leaders across every region of our motherland.

With independence came the sovereign right of Indians to govern themselves. The most profound milestone in this new epoch was the drafting of the Constitution of India by the Constituent Assembly. At the heart of our new constitutional republic lay the principle of Universal Adult Franchise (Article 326). Under this golden principle, every Indian citizen aged 18 and above possesses the constitutional right to cast one vote in periodic elections, irrespective of their social caste, economic wealth, gender, educational status, race, or religion.

Through direct elections based on universal franchise, Indian citizens choose their representatives to the Lok Sabha (House of the People). The political party or coalition of parties that secures a clear majority (more than 50% of seats) in the Lok Sabha forms the government. The Parliament acts as the supreme legislative organ of our nation—it makes laws, scrutinizes government actions, sanctions every rupee spent, and guides national policies.

Historical Fact: 18 Lok Sabhas Since 1952

Since India's very first General Elections were held in 1951–52, the Indian electorate has democratically constituted 17 Lok Sabhas. In June 2024, following the massive nationwide General Elections, the 18th Lok Sabha was officially constituted, continuing India's glorious tradition as the world's largest vibrant democracy.

2.2 The Architectural Metaphor: Old Samvidhan Sadan vs New Sansad Bhavan

In NCERT Chapter 6, five curious Grade 8 students from Nalanda Vidyalaya—Usha, Sukhwinder, Anjali, John, and Farida—take an educational study tour to New Delhi to explore India's parliamentary architecture. Their lively observations open up a fascinating window into the evolution of our democratic institutions.

The Historic Old Parliament (Samvidhan Sadan)

The iconic circular Parliament House was designed by British architects Sir Edwin Lutyens and Sir Herbert Baker and constructed in the 1920s (inaugurated in 1927). For more than 75 years, this circular colonnaded building served as the epicenter of Indian destiny. It was within its Central Hall that our founding mothers and fathers of the Constituent Assembly deliberated and drafted the Constitution. Today, this historic building is reverently designated as Samvidhan Sadan (Constitution House), preserving our living democratic memory.

The New Parliament Building (Sansad Bhavan)

As India's population expanded from 36 crores in 1947 to over 140 crores today, the old building faced acute physical limitations: overcrowding, lack of modern digital infrastructure, fire safety hazards, acoustic strain, and structural age. Furthermore, after 2026, when the freeze on constituency delimitation is scheduled to be reviewed, more elected representatives will be needed to ensure fair democratic representation. Hence, a brand-new, ultra-modern Parliament building was constructed as part of the Central Vista Project and inaugurated in 2023.

2.3 The Sacred Heritage of the Sengol: Power Governed by Dharma

A crown jewel of the new Lok Sabha chamber is the sacred Sengol, positioned right beside the Speaker's chair. The word Sengol is derived from the classical Tamil word Semmai, meaning "righteousness, truth, and moral virtue."

Dating back to the golden age of the Chola Dynasty of southern India, the Sengol was a ceremonial sceptre handed over by the Rajaguru (high priest) to a newly crowned king. This sacred handover was not a celebration of military conquest or absolute power; it served as a solemn ethical reminder that the ruler was merely a custodian of the people, bound by Dharma (righteous duty) and justice.

On the historic eve of Indian Independence—the night of 14 August 1947—on the wise suggestion of C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji), the revered priests of the Thiruvavaduthurai Adheenam matha from Tamil Nadu consecrated and handed this magnificent 5-foot gold-plated silver sceptre to India's first Prime Minister, Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru, marking the sacred transfer of power from the British Empire to sovereign India.

Pedagogical Memory Anchor: What Does the Sengol Symbolize?

At the very crest of the Sengol sits a meticulously carved sculpture of Nandi the Bull. In Indian tradition, Nandi symbolizes unyielding steadfastness, justice, and righteousness. Placing the Sengol beside the Lok Sabha Speaker's chair reminds our elected MPs that political power is never absolute—it must always remain subordinated to the welfare of the people and moral truth.

2.4 Composition and Bicameral Architecture of Sansad (Article 79)

Under Article 79 of the Indian Constitution, the Parliament of India is formally composed of:

  1. The President of India (The Constitutional Head of State);
  2. The Lok Sabha (The House of the People / Lower House); and
  3. The Rajya Sabha (The Council of States / Upper House).

This two-house structure is known as a bicameral legislature (derived from Latin: bi = two, and camera = chamber). Why did the architects of our Constitution choose two houses instead of a single assembly?

  • Federal Representation: India is a Union of States. While the Lok Sabha represents the individual citizens of India, the Rajya Sabha directly represents the interests, rights, and autonomy of the States.
  • A Constitutional "Cooling Chamber": Directly elected assemblies can sometimes pass hasty, populist, or emotionally driven legislation. The Rajya Sabha, filled with experienced elders, jurists, and regional representatives, subjects every bill to thorough second scrutiny, acting as a sobering check on impulsiveness.
  • Administrative Continuity: Even when the Lok Sabha is dissolved during national elections or political crises, the permanent Rajya Sabha remains in session to address urgent national emergencies.

Lok Sabha vs Rajya Sabha: The Curricular Contrast

The two Houses differ markedly in composition, election methods, tenure, and constitutional roles:

  • Lok Sabha (543 MPs): Directly elected by the adult citizens of India using the first-past-the-post electoral method. Its normal term is 5 years, but it can be dissolved earlier by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister. The chamber is decorated with peacock motifs and green furnishings.
  • Rajya Sabha (245 MPs): 233 members are elected indirectly by the elected Members of State Legislative Assemblies (MLAs) using proportional representation, while 12 eminent members are nominated by the President for their distinguished contributions to literature, science, arts, and social service. The Rajya Sabha is a permanent body that is never dissolved; its members serve 6-year terms, with one-third retiring every two years. It is adorned with lotus motifs and royal red furnishings.

2.5 Presiding Officers & Multilingual Digital Democracy

Orderly debate in such large assemblies requires impartial, authoritative presiding officers:

  • The Speaker of the Lok Sabha: Elected by the Lok Sabha MPs from amongst themselves. The Speaker conducts sittings, maintains discipline, decides who may speak, rules on procedural points, certifies whether a draft law is a "Money Bill," and casts a vote only in the event of an equality of votes (casting vote).
  • The Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha: The Vice-President of India acts as the ex-officio Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha. The House also elects a Deputy Chairperson from among its members to preside in the Chairperson's absence.
Digital Inclusivity: Simultaneous Interpretation in 18 Indian Languages

To ensure that every elected representative can express the heartfelt grievances of their regional constituents, the Parliament provides world-class digital simultaneous interpretation headsets. Originally available in 12 languages (Hindi, English, Assamese, Bengali, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam, Marathi, Odia, Punjabi, Tamil, and Telugu), the system recently expanded to include 6 additional languages: Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Manipuri, Urdu, and Sanskrit, bringing the total to 18 official languages.

2.6 The Four Pillars of Parliamentary Legislative Functions

The Constitution of India entrusts the Parliament with four indispensable categories of functions:

Pillar 1: Constitutional Functions

The Parliament is not merely an ordinary legislature; it is the constitutional guardian of our republic. Its primary constitutional functions include:

  • Participating in the election of the President of India (via an Electoral College alongside elected MLAs).
  • Electing the Vice-President of India (by MPs of both Houses).
  • Amending the Constitution under Article 368 to keep our supreme law dynamic and attuned to the needs of each generation.
  • Conducting impeachment proceedings to remove the President, Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, and the Comptroller and Auditor General for proven misconduct or constitutional violation.
  • Creating new States or altering State boundaries and names under Article 3.

Pillar 2: The Lawmaking Highway (From Bill to Act)

The core duty of the legislature is lawmaking. A proposed draft of a law introduced in Parliament is called a Bill. A bill goes through a rigorous, transparent 7-stage highway before it receives Presidential assent and becomes an enforceable Act.

Pillar 4: Financial Accountability & Power of the Purse

Democracy was born out of the struggle against arbitrary taxation. In India, the Constitution establishes complete parliamentary control over public finance through two foundational doctrines:

  • Article 265: "No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law." The Executive cannot invent a new tax or hike tax rates without passing an Act through Parliament.
  • Article 266: All government revenues are deposited into the Consolidated Fund of India. Not a single rupee can be withdrawn or spent by any Ministry without the formal sanction of an Appropriation Bill passed by Parliament.
  • Article 112: Every financial year, the government must present the Annual Financial Statement (Union Budget) to Parliament, detailing estimated receipts and proposed expenditures.

2.7 The Union Executive: Nominal Head vs Real Authority

Under Part V, Chapter I of the Constitution, the Union Executive consists of:

  1. The President of India;
  2. The Vice-President of India; and
  3. The Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister.

The President of India (De Jure / Nominal Executive)

The President is the supreme Head of State and the first citizen of the republic. All executive actions of the Government of India are formally conducted in the President's name. The President appoints the Prime Minister, Council of Ministers, Supreme Court and High Court judges, Governors, and ambassadors, summons and prorogues Parliament, delivers opening addresses, and grants assent to bills.

However, under Article 74, the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. The President possesses critical discretionary powers during political crises:

  • Appointing a Prime Minister when no single party or pre-poll alliance secures a clear majority in a "hung" Lok Sabha.
  • Dismissing a Council of Ministers that has lost the confidence of the Lok Sabha but refuses to resign.
  • Returning a non-money bill back to Parliament once for reconsideration.

The Prime Minister & Council of Ministers (De Facto / Real Executive)

The Prime Minister is the real executive authority. Appointed by the President as the leader who commands majority support in the Lok Sabha, the Prime Minister:

  • Selects Ministers and allocates ministerial portfolios.
  • Presides over Union Cabinet meetings and steers national policy.
  • Coordinates the work of all Ministries and departments.
  • Serves as the principal communicator between the Cabinet and the President.
  • Acts as the supreme spokesperson of the nation on domestic and international affairs.
Core Constitutional Principle: Collective Responsibility (Article 75(3))

Article 75(3) mandates that the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This means:

  • The Ministry functions as a single unified team. Even if an individual Minister disagrees with a Cabinet decision, they must defend it publicly or resign.
  • If a motion of no-confidence is passed in the Lok Sabha, or if a crucial government bill or the budget is defeated, the entire Council of Ministers falls together. In political science terms, "Ministers swim or sink together!"
Moral Responsibility: Lal Bahadur Shastri's Historic 1956 Resignation

In November 1956, a tragic train accident occurred at Ariyalur in Tamil Nadu, claiming the lives of 142 passengers. Although Union Railway Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri was not personally responsible for any technical failure, he believed that a democratic minister must uphold the highest moral standards. He tendered his resignation to Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru. While Nehru initially refused, Shastri insisted, declaring that accepting his resignation would set a golden precedent for administrative ethics and ministerial accountability. Later, Shastri became one of India's most beloved Prime Ministers!

2.9 The Judiciary and the System of Checks and Balances

While the Legislature makes laws and the Executive implements them, what prevents them from abusing their vast powers? That role belongs to the third organ of state: The Independent Judiciary (headed by the Supreme Court of India and High Courts).

The Judiciary acts as the custodian and guardian of the Constitution. Its vital constitutional mechanisms include:

  • Judicial Review: The Supreme Court and High Courts have the constitutional authority to examine any law passed by Parliament or State Assemblies, as well as any executive notification. If a law violates Fundamental Rights or the Basic Structure of the Constitution, the courts can declare it unconstitutional, null, and void.
  • Settling Federal Disputes: The Supreme Court resolves legal disputes between the Centre and States or between two different States under its Original Jurisdiction (Article 131).
  • Safeguarding Fundamental Rights: Under Articles 32 and 226, citizens can directly petition the Supreme Court and High Courts for writs (Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo-Warranto, and Certiorari) to stop unlawful executive actions.

2. Disruptions and Decline in Productivity (M. Venkaiah Naidu's Statement)

On Page 20, the textbook cites the alarming 2021 farewell statement of former Vice-President and Rajya Sabha Chairman M. Venkaiah Naidu:

"...the productivity of Rajya Sabha during 2004–14 has been about 78% and it declined to about 65% since then. Of the 11 sessions that I presided over, four clocked low productivity of 6.80%, 27.30%, 28.90%, and 29.55%; during the year 2018, the Rajya Sabha recorded the lowest-ever productivity of 35.75% under the impact of disruptions."

Frequent walkouts, sloganeering in the well of the House, and forced adjournments wash out the Question Hour, shielding ministers from scrutiny and forcing complex bills to be passed hastily via voice votes without debate.

3. Criminalisation of Politics and Declining Decorum

Civil society organizations (such as ADR) report that a significant percentage of MPs and MLAs face pending criminal charges. Furthermore, televised debates increasingly resemble angry shouting matches rather than deep philosophical deliberations on national welfare.

2.13 Citizen Empowerment: Strengthening the Democratic Future

A parliamentary democracy does not run on auto-pilot. Its strength depends directly on the vigilance, participation, and moral character of its citizens. The textbook emphasizes key ways to rejuvenate democracy:

  • Active Civic Participation: Moving beyond casting a vote once every five years to participating in public discussions, town halls, and engaging constructively with local MPs and MLAs.
  • Digital Democracy Platforms: Using government platforms such as MyGov.in for pre-legislative public consultations, where citizens can read draft bills and submit clauses, criticisms, and suggestions before Parliament votes.
  • Right to Information (RTI Act, 2005): Empowering citizens to demand audited files, expenditure receipts, and project timelines from public departments.
Historic Milestone: Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023

In September 2023, the Indian Parliament created history in the new Sansad Bhavan by passing the Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam). This historic statute reserves 33% (one-third) of all seats in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies for women. First introduced in 1996, the bill took over 27 years of continuous consensus-building across parties to overcome patriarchal hesitations and finally become law!

The Immortal Words of Atal Bihari Vajpayee (NCERT Page 22)

During a historic confidence debate in the Lok Sabha, former Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee delivered these immortal words that define the sanctity of our parliamentary institutions:

"Sarkaarein aayengi, jaayengi. Paartiyaan banengi, bigadengi. Lekin yeh desh rehna chaahiye, is desh ka loktantra amar rehna chaahiye!"

In-Text Big Question 1 (Page 1): What is India's parliamentary system, and how is it structured?
Step 1: Core Concept

India's parliamentary system is a democratic form of representative governance where the executive branch is drawn from and held collectively accountable to the elected legislature.

Step 2: Constitutional Foundation

Under Article 79 of the Constitution of India, the Parliament (Sansad) consists of the President of India and two legislative houses: the Lok Sabha (House of the People) and the Rajya Sabha (Council of States).

Step 3: Step-by-Step Structural Analysis
  • Electoral Base: Indian citizens directly elect representatives to the Lok Sabha based on Universal Adult Franchise (Article 326).
  • Government Formation: The political party or alliance that wins a majority in the Lok Sabha forms the Union Government.
  • Bicameral Structure: The Lok Sabha (543 MPs) represents the people directly; the Rajya Sabha (245 MPs) represents the federal States and Union Territories.
  • Presiding Officers: The Speaker leads the Lok Sabha; the Vice-President of India presides as ex-officio Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha.
Step 4: Real-World Illustration

In June 2024, following the 18th General Elections, the leader commanding the majority coalition in the Lok Sabha was sworn in as Prime Minister by the President of India, demonstrating the smooth transition of democratic power.

Step 5: CBSE Exam Keyword Wrap-Up

Keywords: Representative democracy, Article 79, Bicameral, Lok Sabha (543), Rajya Sabha (245), Universal Adult Franchise, Executive accountability.

In-Text Big Question 2 (Page 1): What are the key functions of the Parliament?
Step 1: Core Concept

The Parliament of India is the supreme legislative and supervisory forum of the nation, performing four foundational categories of functions.

Step 2: Constitutional Foundation

Articles 79 to 122 of Part V of the Constitution specify the legislative, electoral, financial, and oversight functions of Parliament.

Step 3: The 4 Functional Pillars
  • 1. Constitutional Functions: Elects the President and Vice-President; amends the Constitution under Article 368; conducts impeachment proceedings for constitutional offices.
  • 2. Lawmaking: Drafts, debates, amends, and passes statutory laws (from Bill to Act) on subjects in the Union and Concurrent Lists.
  • 3. Executive Accountability: Keeps ministers answerable through Question Hour, Zero Hour, Standing Committees, and No-Confidence Motions.
  • 4. Financial Accountability: Sanctions the Union Budget (Article 112); controls the Consolidated Fund of India; enforces the rule that no tax can be levied without statutory authority (Article 265).
Step 4: Real-World Illustration

When the Right to Education (RTE) Act was passed in 2009, Parliament exercised both its lawmaking power and its financial role by sanctioning thousands of crores to build new schools across India.

Step 5: CBSE Exam Keyword Wrap-Up

Keywords: Constitutional, Lawmaking, Executive accountability, Financial power of the purse, Question Hour, Budget scrutiny.

In-Text Big Question 3 (Page 1): What are the roles of the legislature and the executive in India's Parliamentary democracy?
Step 1: Core Concept

In our parliamentary system, the Legislature acts as the lawmaking and supervising body representing the will of the people, while the Executive acts as the implementing and administering body.

Step 2: Constitutional Foundation

Part V divides governance into Chapter I (The Executive: President, PM, Cabinet) and Chapter II (The Parliament: Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha).

Step 3: Comparative Functional Breakdown
  • Role of Legislature: Debates public issues, scrutinizes government policies, enacts statutory laws, grants taxation authority, and holds ministers accountable daily.
  • Role of Executive: Introduces government bills, formulates public policies, prepares the national budget, and executes laws through career civil servants.
  • Key Difference: The Executive is drawn from the Legislature and remains in power only as long as it enjoys the majority confidence of the Lok Sabha.
Step 4: Real-World Illustration

When Parliament enacted the Digital Data Protection Act, the Legislature formulated and debated the standards, while the Executive (Ministry of Electronics & IT) was tasked with framing administrative rules and setting up the Data Protection Board.

Step 5: CBSE Exam Keyword Wrap-Up

Keywords: Lawmaking vs Implementation, Subordination to Lok Sabha, Fusion of powers, Everyday oversight.

In-Text Big Question 4 (Page 1): How are the legislature and the executive organised at the union and state levels?
Step 1: Core Concept

India follows a parallel, symmetrical governance design where the institutional setup at the State level closely mirrors the structure at the Union level.

Step 2: Constitutional Foundation

Part V governs the Union Government (Articles 52–151), while Part VI governs the State Governments (Articles 152–237).

Step 3: Parallel Structural Matrix
  • Nominal Constitutional Head: President at Centre (elected for 5 years) ⟷ Governor in States (appointed by President for 5 years).
  • Real Executive Authority: Prime Minister & Union Cabinet ⟷ Chief Minister & State Cabinet.
  • Lower House: Lok Sabha (543 MPs, 5-year term) ⟷ Vidhan Sabha (Legislative Assembly, 5-year term).
  • Upper House: Rajya Sabha (strictly bicameral at Centre) ⟷ Vidhan Parishad (present in only 6 States: UP, Bihar, Maharashtra, Karnataka, AP, Telangana).
  • Odisha Context: Odisha possesses a unicameral Vidhan Sabha with 147 elected MLAs.
Step 4: Real-World Illustration

Just as the Union Budget is introduced exclusively in the Lok Sabha by the Union Finance Minister, the Odisha State Budget is introduced exclusively in the Odisha Vidhan Sabha by the State Finance Minister.

Step 5: CBSE Exam Keyword Wrap-Up

Keywords: Parallel structure, President/Governor, PM/CM, Lok Sabha/Vidhan Sabha, Unicameral vs Bicameral.

5.4 Long Answer Questions (5M With Complete Marking Schemes)

Question 17 (LAQ - 5 Marks): Trace the complete 7-stage journey of a bill from initial introduction to becoming an enforceable Act in the Indian Parliament.
Step-by-Step Marking Scheme (5 Marks Total):
• Stage 1 & Stage 2 (Drafting, 1st Reading & Standing Committee Scrutiny): 1.5 Marks
• Stage 3 & Stage 4 (2nd Reading Clause-by-Clause Debate & 3rd Reading Passage): 1.5 Marks
• Stage 5 (Transmission to Second House & Joint Sitting contingency): 1.0 Mark
• Stage 6 & Stage 7 (Presidential Assent & Gazette Publication): 1.0 Mark

Comprehensive Model Answer:

  1. Stage 1: First Reading (Introduction): The legislative process begins when a draft proposal (Bill) is introduced in either the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha (unless it is a Money Bill, which must begin in the Lok Sabha). The introducing Minister or MP seeks the leave of the House, and the bill's title and statement of objects and reasons are formally read out and circulated.
  2. Stage 2: Parliamentary Standing Committee Scrutiny: The presiding officer generally refers the bill to the relevant Department-related Standing Committee. This cross-party group of MPs from both Houses examines the bill in detail away from the media spotlight, seeking public feedback and taking evidence from domain experts before submitting an advisory report with recommendations.
  3. Stage 3: Second Reading (Clause-by-Clause Deliberation): This is the most demanding legislative phase. The House debates the general principles of the bill, followed by a clause-by-clause review. MPs can move formal amendments to individual sections. Each amendment and clause is debated and voted upon individually.
  4. Stage 4: Third Reading (Final Passage Vote): Deliberations are confined to supporting or rejecting the bill as a whole, without introducing new amendments. A division of votes is called. If a simple majority of members present and voting vote "Aye", the bill is passed by the originating House.
  5. Stage 5: Transmission to the Second House: The passed bill is sent to the other House, where it goes through the same three readings. The second house can pass it, reject it, or return it with proposed amendments. If a deadlock persists for six months on an Ordinary Bill, the President can convene a Joint Sitting (Article 108) presided over by the Lok Sabha Speaker, where numerical majority decides the outcome.
  6. Stage 6: Presidential Assent: Once passed by both Houses, the bill is presented to the President of India. The President may grant assent, withhold assent, or return a non-money bill once for parliamentary reconsideration. If Parliament passes the bill again (with or without amendments), the President is constitutionally bound to give assent.
  7. Stage 7: Publication in The Gazette of India: Following Presidential assent, the bill becomes an Act. It is published in the official public journal, The Gazette of India, and takes legal effect as binding national law on the specified enforcement date.
Question 18 (LAQ - 5 Marks): Compare the constitutional powers, composition, and functions of the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha. Which house is more powerful and why?
Step-by-Step Marking Scheme (5 Marks Total):
• Composition, Election, and Tenure Comparison: 1.5 Marks
• Financial and Executive Control Powers Comparison: 1.5 Marks
• Special Exclusive Powers of Rajya Sabha: 1.0 Mark
• Analytical Conclusion on Lok Sabha's Democratic Primacy: 1.0 Mark

Comprehensive Model Answer:

  • 1. Composition, Election & Tenure:
    • The Lok Sabha comprises 543 directly elected MPs representing citizens across territorial constituencies for a 5-year term. It can be dissolved prematurely.
    • The Rajya Sabha comprises 245 members (233 indirectly elected by State MLAs + 12 nominated by the President). It is a permanent body where members serve 6-year terms, with one-third retiring every two years.
  • 2. Financial Primacy:
    • Under Article 109 and 110, Money Bills can originate ONLY in the Lok Sabha. The Rajya Sabha cannot reject or amend a Money Bill; it can only delay it for up to 14 days and offer recommendations.
    • The annual Union Budget and Demands for Grants are voted upon exclusively in the Lok Sabha.
  • 3. Control Over the Executive:
    • Under Article 75(3), the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible only to the Lok Sabha. A No-Confidence Motion can be moved and passed only in the Lok Sabha. If passed, the entire government falls. The Rajya Sabha can question ministers, but cannot dismiss them.
  • 4. Special Powers of the Rajya Sabha:
    • Under Article 249, the Rajya Sabha can pass a resolution by a two-thirds majority authorizing Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in the national interest.
    • Under Article 312, it alone can authorize the creation of new All-India Services (like IAS, IPS, or IFS).
  • Analytical Conclusion: While the Rajya Sabha plays a vital role as a federal cooling chamber, the Lok Sabha is constitutionally more powerful because it derives its authority directly from the people via universal adult franchise. In a democracy, the chamber directly accountable to the electorate holds the ultimate power of the purse and government survival.

  • Structural Challenges Facing Sansad:
    • 1. Steep Drop in Sittings: The 1st Lok Sabha (1952–57) met for an average of 135 days per year (677 sittings total). Today, the Parliament sits for only 55 to 65 days annually. Many State Assemblies meet for fewer than 25 days a year.
    • 2. Disruptions & Low Productivity: As revealed by former Rajya Sabha Chairman M. Venkaiah Naidu, average Upper House productivity dropped from 78% (2004–14) to 65%, with four sessions recording disastrous productivity levels between 6.8% and 29.5%. Frequent shouting matches in the well of the House wash out the Question Hour.
    • 3. Bypassing Committee Scrutiny: Crucial, multi-faceted bills are increasingly passed within minutes via voice votes without referral to Department-related Standing Committees.
    • 4. Criminalisation of Lawmakers: Data from ADR indicates a growing proportion of legislators face serious criminal charges, undermining the moral authority of the legislature.
  • Four Practical Institutional Reforms:
    1. Mandatory Minimum Sitting Days: Amend parliamentary rules to legally require Parliament to sit for at least 100 to 120 days each year, with fixed annual legislative calendars to prevent sessions from being cut short.
    2. Strict Penalties for Willful Disruption: Enforce automatic suspensions and salary reductions for MPs and MLAs who storm the well of the House or persistently halt proceedings.
    3. Compulsory Pre-Legislative Committee Review: Mandate that every substantive bill must undergo review by a Departmental Standing Committee, complete with public consultation, before it can be brought to a floor vote.
    4. Dedicated Opposition Days: Adopt the British Westminster convention of reserving specific days where the opposition sets the parliamentary agenda, ensuring pressing public grievances are heard without resorting to floor disruptions.
Question 20 (LAQ - 5 Marks): Explain the doctrine of "Checks and Balances" between the Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary in India. How does it prevent autocracy while preserving coordination?
Step-by-Step Marking Scheme (5 Marks Total):
• Definition & Conceptual Foundation: 1.0 Mark
• Legislative Controls over Executive and Judiciary: 1.5 Marks
• Executive Controls over Legislature and Judiciary: 1.0 Mark
• Judicial Review & Constitutional Guardianship: 1.5 Marks

Comprehensive Model Answer:

  • Conceptual Framework: The doctrine of Checks and Balances ensures that sovereign state power is not concentrated in any single authority. Each of the three branches possesses constitutional mechanisms to scrutinize and restrain the other two, preventing tyranny while facilitating governance.
  • 1. How the Legislature Checks the Other Organs:
    • Checks Executive: Question Hour, budget approvals, and the power of dismissal via a No-Confidence Motion.
    • Checks Judiciary: Exercises constituent power to amend laws (within the Basic Structure) and holds the power to impeach judges for proven misconduct under Article 124(4).
  • 2. How the Executive Checks the Other Organs:
    • Checks Legislature: The President formally summons, addresses, and prorogues Parliament, and can return non-money bills once for reconsideration.
    • Checks Judiciary: The President formally appoints Supreme Court and High Court judges (acting on the advice of the Collegium).
  • 3. How the Judiciary Checks the Other Organs:
    • Checks Legislature: Can declare any Act passed by Parliament unconstitutional through Judicial Review if it violates Fundamental Rights or the Basic Structure.
    • Checks Executive: Strikes down arbitrary administrative orders and issues protective constitutional writs (Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, etc.) under Articles 32 and 226.
  • Conclusion: This triangular system ensures that power is not concentrated in one place. Each branch operates within clear boundaries, with the Constitution as the supreme governing law.

5.5 Competency-Based Case Studies (Real-World Analysis)

Question 21 (Case Study 1): The Right to Education (RTE) Act, 2009 & Standing Committee Scrutiny.
Source Text Extract (Adapted from NCERT Chapter 6, Pages 8–9):
"I came into existence in August 2009, though my idea began almost a century before. My roots lie in the Directive Principles of State Policy of the Constitution. In the early 1990s, public interest litigation argued in the Supreme Court that education was an inherent part of the Right to Life (Article 21). Parliamentary action began some years later with the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002, which mandated under Article 21A that the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged 6 to 14. Six years later, I was tabled as a bill in the Rajya Sabha. A Parliamentary Standing Committee studied me thoroughly and suggested crucial modifications. The major debate concerned funding: new schools, classrooms, and trained teachers for millions of children would cost thousands of crores. After the 2009 general elections, the new government pursued the matter, the Lok Sabha passed me, the President gave assent, and I became law."

Sub-Questions & Step-by-Step Model Solutions:

  • Sub-Question 21.1 (1 Mark): Which Constitutional Amendment Act formally inserted Article 21A into the Indian Constitution?
    Answer: The 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002 inserted Article 21A, establishing free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14 as a Fundamental Right.
  • Sub-Question 21.2 (1 Mark): Why was the RTE Bill referred to a Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee?
    Answer: It was referred to a Standing Committee so that a cross-party group of MPs could examine the complex financial and administrative requirements—such as hiring teachers, building classrooms, and budgeting funds—away from partisan floor politics.
  • Sub-Question 21.3 (2 Marks): How does the journey of the RTE Act demonstrate constructive cooperation between the Judiciary, Legislature, and Executive?
    Answer:
    • Judiciary: Interpreted the Right to Life (Article 21) in the Mohini Jain and Unni Krishnan cases to include basic education.
    • Legislature: Amended the Constitution (Article 21A) and subsequently drafted, debated, and enacted the enabling RTE statute in 2009.
    • Executive: Formulated the bill's clauses, committed the necessary budgetary funds, and implemented the law through the Ministry of Education and State Directorates.
Question 22 (Case Study 2): Ministerial Accountability and the Ariyalur Train Disaster (Lal Bahadur Shastri).
Source Text Extract (Adapted from NCERT Chapter 6, Page 14):
"In November 1956, a severe railway accident occurred at Ariyalur in Tamil Nadu, claiming the lives of 142 passengers. Although Union Railway Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri was not directly blamed or found technically responsible for the disaster, he believed that a democratic minister must accept full moral responsibility for anything that happens within their ministry. He tendered his resignation to Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru. Nehru initially refused, but Shastri insisted, arguing that accepting the resignation would set a golden precedent for administrative ethics and accountability. Finally, his resignation was accepted."

Sub-Questions & Step-by-Step Model Solutions:

  • Sub-Question 22.1 (1 Mark): Name the Union Railway Minister who resigned in 1956, and state the position he later held in the Government of India.
    Answer: Lal Bahadur Shastri resigned as Railway Minister in 1956; he later served as the second Prime Minister of India (1964–1966).
  • Sub-Question 22.2 (1 Mark): Was Lal Bahadur Shastri convicted or found legally culpable for the Ariyalur accident by a court of law?
    Answer: No. Shastri had no direct technical or legal guilt; he resigned purely to uphold the democratic principle of moral responsibility.
  • Sub-Question 22.3 (2 Marks): Why is individual moral responsibility vital in a parliamentary democracy, and how does it strengthen public trust in government?
    Answer: Individual moral responsibility establishes that high public office is a public trust rather than an entitlement. When leaders step down following major institutional failures, it demonstrates respect for public grief, holds the administrative hierarchy accountable, and reinforces citizens' faith that democracy values human life above political survival.

5.6 Previous Years Questions (PYQs) & Most Probable Exam Questions

Question 23 (PYQ / Most Probable - 3 Marks): "The Lok Sabha exercises more financial authority than the Rajya Sabha." Justify this statement with three constitutional arguments.

Topper Model Answer:

  • 1. Exclusive Introduction: Under Article 109, a Money Bill can be introduced ONLY in the Lok Sabha with prior presidential recommendation; it can never originate in the Rajya Sabha.
  • 2. 14-Day Limit: Once passed by the Lok Sabha, the Rajya Sabha cannot reject or amend the Money Bill; it must return the bill within 14 days with recommendations. The Lok Sabha is completely free to accept or reject any of those suggestions.
  • 3. Sole Authority Over Budget Demands: Demands for Grants in the annual Union Budget are debated and voted upon exclusively by the Lok Sabha under Article 113. The Rajya Sabha discusses the budget, but cannot vote on expenditure grants.
Question 24 (PYQ / Most Probable - 3 Marks): What is meant by "Zero Hour"? Explain its origins and how it differs from Question Hour.

Topper Model Answer:

  • Definition & Origins: Zero Hour is an informal procedural hour that begins at 12:00 PM, immediately following Question Hour. It is a unique Indian parliamentary innovation dating back to 1962; it does not exist in the formal Westminster rulebook.
  • How It Works: During Zero Hour, MPs can raise urgent national issues, constituency emergencies, or administrative failures without submitting advance notice (unlike the 10-day notice required for Question Hour).
  • Key Differences:
    • Question Hour is formally listed in parliamentary rules; Zero Hour is an informal convention.
    • Question Hour requires advance notice; Zero Hour allows spontaneous matters to be raised.
    • Ministers are legally bound to answer Question Hour queries; during Zero Hour, ministers are not required to provide an immediate on-the-spot response.
Question 25 (PYQ / Most Probable - 3 Marks): Explain three ways in which the Parliament of India controls and guides the Union Executive.

Topper Model Answer:

  • 1. Questioning & Interrogation: During Question Hour and Zero Hour, MPs question ministers regarding policies, project delays, and departmental actions, forcing transparency into executive work.
  • 2. Financial Control (Power of the Purse): The Executive cannot collect taxes (Article 265) or spend from the Consolidated Fund of India (Article 266) without parliamentary approval. MPs use cut motions and committee audits to review proposed expenditures.
  • 3. Parliamentary Motions & Censure: Through Calling Attention Motions, Adjournment Motions, and ultimately the No-Confidence Motion, the Lok Sabha can dismiss a government that loses its majority.
Question 26 (Most Probable 2026–27 - 3 Marks): What is the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023? Why is this constitutional amendment considered a milestone in Indian democracy?

Topper Model Answer:

  • Constitutional Identity: The Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023, popularly known as the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, reserves 33% (one-third) of all seats in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies for women.
  • Why It Is a Landmark Milestone:
    • Ending a 27-Year Deadlock: First introduced in 1996, the bill lapsed repeatedly over nearly three decades before finally achieving cross-party consensus in the new Sansad Bhavan.
    • Correcting Historical Gender Imbalance: Women make up nearly 50% of India's population, but held only ~14.4% of Lok Sabha seats and 8–9% of assembly seats. This reform guarantees a minimum of 181 women MPs in the Lok Sabha.
    • Empowering Policy Representation: Increased female representation brings deeper legislative focus to issues such as maternal healthcare, childcare, education, and social safety.
Question 27 (Most Probable 2026–27 - 3 Marks): Explain why the Indian Constitution provides for an independent Judiciary alongside a parliamentary fusion of legislative and executive powers.

Topper Model Answer:

  • 1. Impartial Constitutional Guardian: Because the Executive is drawn from the majority party in the Legislature, the two branches work closely together. An independent Judiciary is essential to serve as an impartial umpire, ensuring the ruling majority does not exceed constitutional limits.
  • 2. Safeguarding Fundamental Rights: Under Articles 32 and 226, citizens require a fearless court system that can issue binding writs against executive overreach or unjust legislative statutes.
  • 3. Arbiter of Federal Disputes: In a federal republic where power is shared between the Centre and States under the Seventh Schedule, an independent Supreme Court is needed to impartially resolve inter-governmental disputes under its Original Jurisdiction (Article 131).

Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 High-Yield Fast Recall Points

The 5 most dangerous conceptual pitfalls where students lose easy marks, paired with 15 crisp, fast-recall bullet points for night-before-exam revision.

5 Common Student Blunders & Topper Corrections

Trap Blunder 1: Conflating a "Bill" with an "Act"

❌ Common Student Blunder: Writing that a proposed draft becomes a "law" as soon as it is passed by the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha.

✅ Topper Correction: A legislative proposal remains a Bill throughout its journey across both Houses. It transforms into an Act (Law) only after the President of India accords their official assent and the statute is notified in The Gazette of India.

Trap Blunder 2: Confusing Member Tenure with House Tenure in Rajya Sabha

❌ Common Student Blunder: Stating that "The Rajya Sabha has a tenure of 6 years and is dissolved every 6 years."

✅ Topper Correction: The Rajya Sabha as a chamber is a Permanent Body that is NEVER dissolved. It is the individual Members of Parliament (MPs) who serve a 6-year term, with one-third of members retiring every two years (Article 83(1)). Only the Lok Sabha has a 5-year chamber life subject to dissolution.

Trap Blunder 3: Misunderstanding Rajya Sabha's Powers Over Money Bills

❌ Common Student Blunder: Believing the Rajya Sabha can reject a Money Bill or amend its tax provisions.

✅ Topper Correction: Money Bills (Article 110) can originate ONLY in the Lok Sabha with prior presidential recommendation. The Rajya Sabha has zero power to reject or amend a Money Bill; it can only delay it for up to 14 days and return it with recommendations. If not returned in 14 days, the bill is deemed passed automatically by both Houses!

Trap Blunder 4: Thinking Civil Servants Resign When Governments Change

❌ Common Student Blunder: Writing that civil servants (IAS/IPS officers) must resign from their posts when a ruling political party loses an election.

✅ Topper Correction: Only the Political Executive (Prime Minister and Ministers) resign when a government loses an election. The Permanent Executive (career civil servants) maintains strict political neutrality and remains in service until the age of retirement (60 years), ensuring administrative continuity.

Trap Blunder 5: Assuming All 28 Indian States Have Bicameral Legislatures

❌ Common Student Blunder: Stating that every state in India has both a Vidhan Sabha and a Vidhan Parishad, just like the Union Parliament.

✅ Topper Correction: While the Union Parliament is strictly bicameral, only 6 States have a bicameral legislature (Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Uttar Pradesh). The remaining 22 States (including Odisha) have a unicameral legislature consisting solely of the Vidhan Sabha.
