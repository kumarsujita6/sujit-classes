Understanding the Dual Theme: What are "Wit" and "Wisdom"?

At first glance, young students often confuse Wit with Wisdom. In English literature and real life, they are two complementary virtues:

  • Wit (ହାସ୍ୟରସିକ ଚତୁରତା): The spark of intellectual agility, quick humour, and clever verbal dexterity. It defuses tension, catches attention, and points out the truth without causing physical harm.
  • Wisdom (ଜ୍ଞାନ ଓ ପ୍ରଜ୍ଞା): Deep insight, maturity of judgement, understanding human feelings, and knowing when, where, and how to act rightly.
  • A proverb to remember: "Wit is the sharp knife that cuts through confusion; Wisdom is knowing not to hurt anyone with the blade."

2.1 Prose Deep Dive: "The Wit that Won Hearts"

Set during the golden epoch of the Vijayanagara Empire in southern India, this narrative explores the delicate interplay between royal pride, natural human vulnerability, and the transformative power of wit. Let us analyze the story scene-by-scene:

Scene 1: The Royal Poetry Recitation & The Involuntary Yawn

King Krishnadeva Raya was celebrated not only as a formidable military monarch and generous patron of the arts, but also as a passionate scholar and poet. One serene evening, after spending long hours crafting a new poem, the King invites his royal consort, Queen Thirumalambal, to his private chambers to hear his latest literary creation.

While the King recites his stanzas with dramatic flair and pride, the Queen listens respectfully. However, Queen Thirumalambal had endured a strenuous, exhausting day fulfilling the demanding administrative and charitable duties of the royal palace. Overcome by natural physical weariness, her jaw drops and she lets out an involuntary yawn.

Scientific Fact: The Involuntary Reflex of Yawning

A yawn (ହାଇ ମାରିବା) is a physiological reflex controlled by the autonomic nervous system. It occurs when the body requires increased oxygenation and brain cooling due to tiredness, boredom, or physical exhaustion. It is completely involuntary—a person cannot consciously prevent it simply by royal command.

Scene 2: The Egotistical Storm & The Palace Cold War

Blinded by self-importance and creative pride, King Krishnadeva Raya misinterprets the Queen's biological exhaustion as deliberate mockery and supreme contempt for his poetic talent. Instead of checking if his wife was tired, his royal ego feels deeply humiliated.

In a fit of fury, the King abruptly halts his recitation and storms off into his royal quarters. He imposes a rigid, hostile silence upon the palace, refusing to speak a single word to Queen Thirumalambal. Days stretch into weeks, and a suffocating hush falls over the royal residence. The King's courtiers notice the icy tension, but because they are terrified of incurring the monarch's anger, they maintain cowardly silence.

Scene 3: The Queen's Distress & The Secret Plea to Tenali Rama

Queen Thirumalambal is heartbroken. She loves her husband and deeply respects his artistic gifts, but she knows that arguing directly with an angry king will only make his wounded pride more defensive. She recognizes that brute facts cannot heal emotional injuries; what is required is tact, humour, and psychological diplomacy.

Desperate to mend the rift, the Queen secretly approaches Tenali Ramakrishna, the legendary court jester, poet, and advisor renowned for his unmatched wit. She confides: "The King thought I was disrespecting his poem and stormed off. Since then, he has not spoken to me... Rama, if I had known this would happen, I would have chosen another day. Help me, please. Only you can help the King understand."

Tenali Rama listens with deep empathy. He comforts the Queen and formulates a plan that will lead the King to self-realization without ever exposing the Queen's role or wounding the Emperor's dignity.

Scene 4: The Courtroom Masterstroke: The Magic Paddy Seeds

The next morning in the grand royal court, before all the assembled ministers, scholars, and nobles, Tenali Rama steps forward holding a decorated golden platter filled with grains of paddy. With theatrical gravity, he bows low before Emperor Krishnadeva Raya.

"Your Majesty," Tenali announces in a resonant voice, "I have procured these extraordinary, miraculous paddy seeds from a holy ascetic. If sown properly, they will produce three times the ordinary harvest and banish famine from the Vijayanagara Empire forever!"

Intrigued, the Emperor asks, "That sounds wondrous, Rama! Why have you not sown them in our royal fields yet?"

Tenali sighs sorrowfully: "Alas, Your Majesty! There is a stringent divine condition. These miraculous seeds must be planted only by a farmer who has never yawned in his entire life! If the sower yawns even once while scattering the seeds, the divine potency will vanish and the entire crop will wither away."

The entire court falls into stunned bewilderment. King Krishnadeva Raya bursts into spontaneous laughter: "Rama, have you lost your wits? What nonsense is this? Yawning is a universal, involuntary reflex! Every human being, animal, and bird yawns when weary! How could anyone find a human being who has never yawned?"

Scene 5: The King's Epiphany, Introspection & Joyous Reunion

In that electrifying instant, Tenali Rama bows with a gentle smile and delivers the masterstroke:

"Your Majesty speaks the absolute truth. Yawning is indeed a natural, involuntary bodily action beyond any human being's control. If even the humblest farmer cannot be blamed for a natural yawn, why was our beloved Queen condemned and banished to silence for yawning out of natural exhaustion while listening to your poetry?"

The entire durbar gasps. The words hit the King not as an accusation, but as an illuminating mirror. The Emperor suddenly catches his own breath. He sees how absurd, petty, and tyrannical his anger had been. His wounded pride dissolves into healthy humility.

Publicly acknowledging his mistake, King Krishnadeva Raya confesses: "I have been foolish. I see now that I overreacted. I let my pride blind me." The King immediately visits Queen Thirumalambal, asks for her forgiveness, showers Tenali Rama with costly jewels and robes of honour, and restores loving peace to the palace.

2.2 Poetry Deep Dive: "A Concrete Example" by Reginald Arkell

Written by British humorist and playwright Reginald Arkell (1882–1959), this delightful 18-line poem uses playful hyperbole, subtle wordplay, and situational irony to explore how human passions and standards of beauty differ from person to person.

📜 The Complete Poem: "A Concrete Example"

[Stanza 1]
My next-door neighbour, Mrs. Jones,
Has got a garden full of stones:
A crazy path, a lily pond,
A rockery and, just beyond,
A sundial with a strange device,
Which Mrs. Jones thinks rather nice.
[Stanza 2]
My next-door neighbour, Mrs. Jones,
Puts little plants between the stones.
They are so delicate and small,
They don't mean anything at all.
I can't think how she gets them in,
Unless she plants them with a pin.
[Stanza 3]
My next-door neighbour, Mrs. Jones,
Once asked me round to see her stones.
We stood and talked about a flower
For quite a quarter of an hour.
“Where is this lovely thing?” I cried.
“You're standing on it,” she replied.

Stanza-by-Stanza Literary Analysis

Stanza 1: The Eccentric Mineral Garden

The poem begins with a conventional expectation that is immediately subverted. While most suburban gardens feature lush green lawns, colourful rosebeds, and blooming shrubs, Mrs. Jones's garden is "full of stones".

  • A crazy path: A walkway constructed from irregularly cut paving stones fitted randomly together like a jigsaw puzzle.
  • A rockery: An ornamental garden arrangement consisting of mounds of rocks with rock-loving alpine plants nestled between them.
  • A sundial with a strange device: An antique time-keeping instrument featuring an engraved motto or emblem (a "strange device") that Mrs. Jones regards with personal affection.

Stanza 2: The Hyperbole of the Pin

In the second stanza, the speaker observes how Mrs. Jones cultivates flora in this stony landscape. Instead of large, showy flowers, she nurtures microscopic, fragile plants tucked into the tight crevices between the stones.

  • The speaker describes them as "so delicate and small, / They don't mean anything at all," demonstrating the speaker's utilitarian, conventional view that flowers ought to be visible and dramatic.
  • The speaker employs a brilliant hyperbole (humorous exaggeration): "I can't think how she gets them in, / Unless she plants them with a pin." A gardener's trowel would be far too coarse for such tiny plants, making the sewing pin a perfect comic metaphor.

Stanza 3: The Climax of Situational Irony

Mrs. Jones invites the narrator for a tour of her botanical treasures. They halt beside a patch of stones and spend fifteen full minutes ("quite a quarter of an hour") discussing a singular, prized flower that Mrs. Jones considers breathtaking.

  • Perplexed because he cannot spot this magnificent specimen anywhere, the speaker exclaims enthusiastically: "Where is this lovely thing?"
  • Mrs. Jones delivers the deadpan punchline: "You're standing on it!"
  • This is textbook situational irony: While the speaker was expecting a towering, radiant blossom, the flower was so microscopic that he has unknowingly flattened it beneath the sole of his shoe during their entire fifteen-minute conversation!

Let Us Learn Grammar 1 (Page 11): Distinguishing Main and Subordinate Clauses NCERT Page 11
Step 1: Question Core & Intent

Step 2: Contextual Reference / Evidence
Pages 10 and 11, NCERT Class 8 Poorvi Unit 1, Grammar section on Clauses.
Step 3: Conceptual Analysis & Rationale
The clause containing the subordinating conjunction (if, because, when, since, although) is always the Subordinate Clause. The clause that makes complete standalone sense is the Main Clause.
Step 4: Step-by-Step Model Answer
  1. "If you like, I will try my best to resolve this matter."
    • Subordinate Clause: If you like (Conditional clause, begins with 'If')
    • Main Clause: I will try my best to resolve this matter (Makes complete sense alone)
  2. "The King stopped speaking because his creative pride was deeply injured."
    • Main Clause: The King stopped speaking
    • Subordinate Clause: because his creative pride was deeply injured (Causal clause)
  3. "If I had known this would happen, I would have chosen another day."
    • Subordinate Clause: If I had known this would happen (Past conditional)
    • Main Clause: I would have chosen another day
Step 5: Teacher's Pro-Tip / Pitfall to Avoid
A comma is required after the subordinate clause if the sentence begins with 'If'. If the main clause comes first, no comma is needed!

LAQ 4 (5 Marks): Master Guide to Conditionals (Formula, Meaning & Sentence) 5 Marks / Grammar Synthesis
Step-by-Step CBSE Marking Scheme (5 Marks Total)
• 1 Mark: Zero Conditional definition, formula, and textbook example.
• 1 Mark: First Conditional definition, formula, and textbook example.
• 1 Mark: Second Conditional definition, formula, and textbook example.
• 1 Mark: Third Conditional definition, formula, and textbook example.
• 1 Mark: Overall clarity, linguistic precision, and accurate punctuation.
Model 5-Mark Syntactic Guide

1. Zero Conditional (Universal Laws & Scientific Realities):

  • Formula: If + Simple Present (V1), ... Simple Present (V1)
  • Function: Expresses conditions that invariably produce the same biological or physical outcome.
  • Example: "If a human being becomes deeply exhausted, the brain triggers an involuntary yawn."

2. First Conditional (Realistic Future Possibilities):

  • Formula: If + Simple Present (V1), ... will / can / may + Base Verb (V1)
  • Function: Refers to a realistic condition in the present with a probable future outcome.
  • Example: "If Tenali Rama presents his riddle tactfully, the King will understand his error."

3. Second Conditional (Hypothetical Present or Future):

  • Formula: If + Simple Past (V2 / were), ... would / could / might + Base Verb (V1)
  • Function: Deals with imaginary, counterfactual, or improbable scenarios in the present or future.
  • Example: "If the King were not an artist himself, he would not care so deeply about his poetry."

4. Third Conditional (Past Unreal Regret):

  • Formula: If + Past Perfect (had + V3), ... would have + Past Participle (V3)
  • Function: Reflects on a past action that cannot be changed, expressing historical regret or hypothetical alternatives.
  • Example: "If Queen Thirumalambal had known the King's reaction, she would have postponed her visit to his chambers."

6.1 Top 5 Exam Hall Blunders (Why Students Lose Marks)

Blunder 1: Placing 'Will' or 'Would' Inside the If-Clause

Common Student Mistake: Writing "If it will rain tomorrow, the durbar will be cancelled" or "If I would have known, I would have apologized."

Topper Correction: Modal auxiliaries (will, would, could) must never appear inside the dependent If-clause! The condition always takes present or past tense:
• Correct 1st Conditional: "If it rains tomorrow, the durbar will be cancelled."
• Correct 3rd Conditional: "If I had known, I would have apologized."

Blunder 2: Claiming Queen Thirumalambal Disliked the King's Poetry

Common Student Mistake: Writing in comprehension answers that "the Queen yawned because the King's poem was terrible, uninteresting, or poorly written."

Topper Correction: The textbook explicitly clarifies that the Queen's yawn was caused entirely by physical exhaustion after a long day of demanding royal duties. She loved and respected the King's artistic talents. It was the King's insecure ego that mistakenly assumed she disliked the poem.

Blunder 3: Confusing Hyperbole with Metaphor in "A Concrete Example"

Common Student Mistake: Identifying the line "Unless she plants them with a pin" as a metaphor or personification.

Topper Correction: This line is a textbook hyperbole (humorous exaggeration). The poet does not literally mean Mrs. Jones uses a sewing needle; he is comically exaggerating the microscopic scale of the plants to make readers smile.

Blunder 4: Explaining Only One Half of the Pun in the Title

Common Student Mistake: Answering a 3-mark question on the title "A Concrete Example" by simply stating: "It means Mrs. Jones's garden has concrete and stones."

Topper Correction: A pun requires explaining both meanings to secure full credit: (1) Literal: Concrete/hard stone garden elements, and (2) Figurative: A definite, tangible real-life example of how human tastes and standards of beauty differ.

Blunder 5: Forgetting the Comma When Starting with a Subordinate Clause

Common Student Mistake: Writing complex sentences without punctuation: "If you practice daily you will become fluent."

Topper Correction: Whenever the subordinate clause begins the sentence, you must separate it from the main clause with a comma: "If you practice daily, you will become fluent." However, if the main clause comes first, no comma is needed: "You will become fluent if you practice daily."
