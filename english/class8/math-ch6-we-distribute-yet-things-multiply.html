Why This Chapter Is Called "We Distribute, Yet Things Multiply"

In common language, to "distribute" means to divide, hand out, or break down into smaller portions, which often makes each share smaller. But in mathematics, when we distribute a multiplier across terms inside brackets—for instance, a(b + c) = ab + ac—the operation actually expands the expression, unlocks hidden patterns, and allows us to multiply large numbers with incredible speed! That is the beautiful paradox captured by the chapter’s title: we distribute parts, yet things multiply into harmonious algebraic structures.

The Five Pedagogical Pillars of Chapter 6

To achieve mastery in Class 8 algebra, students must navigate through five sequential cognitive stages:

  1. The Geometry of Increment: Understanding that changing factors in a multiplication (a + m)(b + n) produces four distinct sub-areas: the original base ab, two cross-strips an and bm, and a corner overlap mn.
  2. Ancient Indian Computational Mastery: Unlocking Brahmagupta’s (628 CE) khanda-guṇanam (breaking into parts) and ista-guṇana (desired fast multiplication for 11, 101, 1001, 99) alongside Sridharacharya’s (750 CE) technique a² = (a + b)(a - b) + b².
  3. The Big Three Identities & Area Proofs: Visualizing (a + b)², (a - b)², and a² - b² as physical dissections of squares and rectangles, proving why the middle term 2ab emerges or cancels out.
  4. Algebraic Error Diagnosis: Rigorously identifying why students confuse (5m + 6n)² with 25m² + 36n² or attempt to add unlike terms like 5w² + 6w.
  5. Multiple Representations & Invariant Equivalence: Demonstrating that whether you look at a geometric pattern like Tadang, Yusuf, Anusha, Vaishnavi, or Aditya, every sound visual perspective leads to the exact same algebraic formula.

6.1 Some Properties of Multiplication & Increments in Products

In everyday arithmetic, we routinely perform multiplications like 23 × 27 = 621. But what happens if one or both of these factors change slightly? Exploring this question reveals the underlying mechanics of algebra.

1. Analyzing Increments in a Numerical Product (23 × 27)

  • Case A: First number (23) increases by 1:
    The new product is (23 + 1) × 27 = 24 × 27. By the basic meaning of multiplication (24 groups of 27 instead of 23 groups), the product increases by exactly 27 (the value of the second number).
    (23 + 1) × 27 = (23 × 27) + 27 = 621 + 27 = 648.
  • Case B: Second number (27) increases by 1:
    The new product is 23 × (27 + 1) = 23 × 28. By the distributive property, this is 23 × 27 + 23 × 1. The product increases by exactly 23 (the value of the first number).
    23 × 28 = 621 + 23 = 644.
  • Case C: Both numbers increase by 1:
    Students often guess that the product increases by 23 + 27 = 50. But let us calculate carefully:
    (23 + 1) × (27 + 1) = 24 × 28 = 672.
    The actual increase is 672 - 621 = 51! Where did the extra 1 come from?
The Algebraic Explanation of (a + 1)(b + 1)

Let the initial numbers be a and b. Increasing both by 1 yields:
(a + 1)(b + 1) = (a + 1)b + (a + 1) × 1
= ab + b + a + 1 = ab + (a + b + 1).
Therefore, whenever both numbers are increased by 1, the product increases by a + b + 1! The extra 1 represents the new corner unit where the two unit increases intersect.

2. What Happens When One Increases and the Other Decreases?

Suppose a is increased by 1 and b is decreased by 1. The new product is (a + 1)(b - 1):

(a + 1)(b - 1) = (a + 1)b - (a + 1) × 1 = ab + b - a - 1 = ab + (b - a - 1).

The net change in the product is b - a - 1:

  • If b - a - 1 > 0 (i.e., b > a + 1), the product increases. For a = 23, b = 27, change is 27 - 23 - 1 = +3 (from 621 to 624).
  • If b - a - 1 = 0 (i.e., b = a + 1), the product remains unchanged. For a = 4, b = 5, 4 × 5 = 20, and (4 + 1)(5 - 1) = 5 × 4 = 20!
  • If b - a - 1 < 0 (i.e., b < a + 1), the product decreases. For a = 10, b = 3, initial product is 30, but (10 + 1)(3 - 1) = 11 × 2 = 22 (decreased by 8, matching 3 - 10 - 1 = -8).

3. Generalizing to Identity 1: (a + m)(b + n)

If two numbers a and b are changed by amounts m and n respectively (where m and n can be positive or negative integers), the product becomes:

Identity 1: (a + m)(b + n) = ab + an + mb + mn.

This fundamental identity shows that every term of the first binomial (a + m) must be multiplied with every term of the second binomial (b + n). The net change from the original product ab is:

Net Change = an + bm + mn.

Handling Negative Numbers with a Single Identity

Because integer multiplication respects sign rules, Identity 1 handles subtractions automatically without memorizing separate formulas:

  • One decreased, one increased: (a - 2)(b + 3) = ab + 3a - 2b - 6 (setting m = -2, n = +3).
  • Both decreased: (a - 3)(b - 4) = ab - 4a - 3b + 12 (setting m = -3, n = -4). Note that (-3) × (-4) = +12!

4. The Critical Concept of "Like Terms"

In an expression like (3/2)a² - (3/2)ab + (3/10)a, can we add (3/2)a² and (3/10)a? Absolutely not!

  • a² represents a × a (a square area).
  • a represents a linear length.
  • Because their variable parts differ in exponent, they are unlike terms and cannot be combined into a single term. Only like terms (such as ab + ab = 2ab or a²b + 2a²b = 3a²b) can be combined by adding their numerical coefficients.

5. A Pinch of History: Brahmagupta and Khanda-guṇanam

The distributive law was used implicitly in ancient civilizations: Euclid expressed it geometrically in ancient Greece, and Āryabhaṭa utilized it in India. However, the first formal, explicit algebraic statement was written in 628 CE by the great Indian mathematician and astronomer Brahmagupta in his magnum opus Brahmasphuṭasiddhānta (Verse 12.55).

Brahmagupta termed this method khanda-guṇanam (multiplication by parts):

"The multiplier is broken up into two or more parts whose sum is equal to it; the multiplicand is then multiplied by each of these and the results added."

In algebraic notation, if a multiplier c is split into (a + b), then: (a + b)c = ac + bc.

Fast Multiplications Using Distributivity (Ista-guṇana)

In Verse 12.56 of the Brahmasphuṭasiddhānta, Brahmagupta introduced ista-guṇana (desired fast multiplication). By decomposing numbers into forms like 10 + 1, 100 + 1, or 100 - 1, large multiplications can be performed mentally in a single line!

1. Multiplying Any Number by 11 in One Line

Consider a 4-digit number dcba (value 1000d + 100c + 10b + a). Multiplying by 11 means multiplying by (10 + 1):

dcba × 11 = dcba × (10 + 1) = dcba0 + dcba.

Aligning the place values: 

     d     c     b     a     0
+          d     c     b     a
-----------------------------------
     d  (c+d) (b+c) (a+b)    a

The One-Line Algorithm for × 11:

  1. Copy the units digit a.
  2. Add adjacent pairs of digits from right to left: (a + b), then (b + c), then (c + d), writing down each sum and carrying over any tens digits.
  3. Finish by writing the leading digit d (plus any carry).

Worked Example: 3874 × 11:
• Step 1: Units = 4.
• Step 2: 4 + 7 = 11 → write 1, carry 1.
• Step 3: 7 + 8 + 1 (carry) = 16 → write 6, carry 1.
• Step 4: 8 + 3 + 1 (carry) = 12 → write 2, carry 1.
• Step 5: Leading digit = 3 + 1 (carry) = 4.
Result: 42,614 in a single mental pass!

2. Multiplying Any Number by 101, 1001, and 99

  • Multiplying by 101: N × 101 = N × (100 + 1) = N00 + N.
    For 3874 × 101: 387400 + 3874 = 391,274.
  • Multiplying by 1001: N × 1001 = N × (1000 + 1) = N000 + N.
    For 265831 × 1001: 265831000 + 265831 = 266,096,831.
  • Multiplying by 99: N × 99 = N × (100 - 1) = N00 - N.
    For 9734 × 99: 973400 - 9734 = 963,666.

6.2 Special Cases of the Distributive Property: The Three Core Identities

When the two binomial factors share terms, the general expansion yields three celebrated formulas known as the Standard Algebraic Identities.

Identity 1A: Square of a Sum: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²

Consider finding the square of 65. Instead of laborious long multiplication, we decompose 65 into 60 + 5:

65² = (60 + 5)² = (60 + 5)(60 + 5) = 60² + 2 × (60 × 5) + 5² = 3600 + 600 + 25 = 4225.

Is (a + b)² Always Greater Than a² + b²?

Since (a + b)² = (a² + b²) + 2ab, the relationship depends entirely on the sign of the cross-term 2ab:

  • Greater: When 2ab > 0 (both a and b have the same sign—either both positive or both negative). For example, (3 + 4)² = 49 > 3² + 4² = 25.
  • Equal: When 2ab = 0 (at least one of a or b is 0). For example, (5 + 0)² = 25 = 5² + 0².
  • Lesser: When 2ab < 0 (one is positive and the other is negative). For example, if a = 5, b = -2: (5 + (-2))² = 3² = 9, whereas 5² + (-2)² = 25 + 4 = 29! Here 9 < 29!

Identity 1B: Square of a Difference: (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b²

To find 55² = (60 - 5)² geometrically:

  1. Start with a large square of side a = 60 (Area = a² = 3600).
  2. We want the area of the inner square of side a - b = 55.
  3. Subtract the top horizontal rectangle of area a × b = 60 × 5 = 300.
  4. Subtract the right vertical rectangle of area b × a = 5 × 60 = 300.
  5. The Inclusion-Exclusion Trap: Look at the corner square of side b = 5. It was covered by BOTH the horizontal and vertical rectangles! Therefore, it was subtracted twice!
  6. To obtain the true area, we must add back b² once: (a - b)² = a² - ab - ab + b² = a² - 2ab + b².

55² = (60 - 5)² = 3600 - 300 - 300 + 25 = 3025.

Figure 2.3: Visualizing why + b² must be added back in (a - b)² to compensate for the doubly-subtracted corner.

Identity 1C: Difference of Two Squares: (a + b)(a - b) = a² - b²

Multiplying (a + b) by (a - b):

(a + b)(a - b) = a(a - b) + b(a - b) = a² - ab + ba - b² = a² - b².

Notice that the two middle cross-terms -ab and +ba are additive opposites and completely cancel to 0!

Sridharacharya’s Lightning Squaring Method (750 CE)

Rewriting Identity 1C by isolating a² gives:

a² = (a + b)(a - b) + b².

The great 8th-century Indian mathematician Sridharacharya used this formula to square numbers mentally by choosing b such that either (a + b) or (a - b) becomes a multiple of 10 or 100:

  • Find 31²: Choose b = 1 (so 31 - 1 = 30 is a round number):
    31² = (31 + 1)(31 - 1) + 1² = 32 × 30 + 1 = 960 + 1 = 961.
  • Find 197²: Choose b = 3 (so 197 + 3 = 200 is a round number):
    197² = (197 + 3)(197 - 3) + 3² = 200 × 194 + 9 = 38,800 + 9 = 38,809.

6.4 This Way or That Way, All Ways Lead to the Bay

Mathematics is not a rigid set of fixed steps; it is a creative discipline where multiple distinct perspectives converge on the exact same algebraic truth.

The Concentric Circle Sequence: Four Distinct Visualizations

Consider the dot/circle sequence where Step 1 has 3 circles, Step 2 has 8, Step 3 has 15, Step 4 has 24:

  • Method 1 (Complete Square minus Missing Corner): Visualizing an enclosing square of side (k + 1) with 1 circle missing: (k + 1)² - 1 = k² + 2k + 1 - 1 = k² + 2k.
  • Method 2 (Central Square plus Two Border Arms): A central square of k × k plus two side arms of length k: k² + 2k.
  • Method 3 (Horizontal Strips plus Bottom Extension): k rows of length (k + 1) plus a single row of k: k(k + 1) + k = k² + k + k = k² + 2k.
  • Method 4 (Complete Rectangle): An array of k rows by (k + 2) columns: k(k + 2) = k² + 2k.

Every single method, when expanded using distributivity, simplifies to the unique invariant formula: k² + 2k.

Step 1: Given & Objective Base product: a × b = 23 × 27 = 621. We need to find the incremental change in the product under three distinct modifications.

Section 6.3 Complete Diagnostic Solutions: Problems 1 through 12
Problem 1: -3p(-5p + 2q) Flawed Text: -3p(-5p + 2q) = -3p + 5p - 2q = p - 2q.
Error Explanation: The student treated the operation as addition rather than multiplication and botched the signs.
Correct Expression: (-3p) × (-5p) + (-3p) × (2q) = 15p² - 6pq.
Problem 2: 2(x - 1) + 3(x + 4) Flawed Text: 2(x - 1) + 3(x + 4) = 2x - 1 + 3x + 4 = 5x + 3.
Error Explanation: The multipliers 2 and 3 were only distributed to the first term in each bracket, ignoring -1 and +4.
Correct Expression: 2x - 2 + 3x + 12 = (2x + 3x) + (-2 + 12) = 5x + 10.
Problem 3: y + 2(y + 2) Flawed Text: y + 2(y + 2) = (y + 2)² = y² + 4y + 4.
Error Explanation: The student erroneously combined the outside term y and multiplier 2 into (y + 2), violating BODMAS order of operations.
Correct Expression: y + 2y + 4 = 3y + 4.
Problem 4: (5m + 6n)² Flawed Text: (5m + 6n)² = 25m² + 36n².
Error Explanation: The student forgot the middle cross-term 2ab = 2(5m)(6n) = 60mn.
Correct Expression: (5m)² + 2(5m)(6n) + (6n)² = 25m² + 60mn + 36n².
Problem 5: (-q + 2)² Flawed Text: (-q + 2)² = q² - 4q + 4.
Error Explanation: This expression is actually CORRECT!
Proof: (-q + 2)² = (2 - q)² = 2² - 2(2)(q) + q² = 4 - 4q + q² = q² - 4q + 4.
Problem 6: 3a(2b × 3c) Flawed Text: 3a(2b × 3c) = 6ab × 9ac = 54a²bc.
Error Explanation: Distributivity applies over addition, NOT over multiplication! Distributing 3a across factors inside a product multiplies by 3a twice.
Correct Expression: 3a × (6bc) = 18abc.
Problem 7: (1/2)(10s - 6) + 3 Flawed Text: (1/2)(10s - 6) + 3 = 5s - 3 + 3 = 5s.
Error Explanation: This simplification is completely CORRECT!
(1/2) × 10s = 5s, (1/2) × (-6) = -3, and -3 + 3 = 0, yielding 5s.
Problem 8: 5w² + 6w Flawed Text: 5w² + 6w = 11w².
Error Explanation: The student added coefficients of unlike terms having different powers.
Correct Expression: 5w² + 6w cannot be combined into a single term! (At most, it can be factored as w(5w + 6)).
Problem 9: 2a³ + 3a³ + 6a²b + 6ab² Flawed Text: 2a³ + 3a³ + 6a²b + 6ab² = 5a³ + 12a²b².
Error Explanation: 2a³ + 3a³ = 5a³ is correct, but 6a²b and 6ab² are unlike terms and cannot be added into 12a²b²!
Correct Expression: 5a³ + 6a²b + 6ab² (or 5a³ + 6ab(a + b)).
Problem 10: (a + 2)(b + 4) Flawed Text: (a + 2)(b + 4) = ab + 8.
Error Explanation: Multiplied only the first and last terms; missed the two cross-products 4a and 2b.
Correct Expression: a(b + 4) + 2(b + 4) = ab + 4a + 2b + 8.
Problem 11: ab² + a²b + a²b² Flawed Text: ab² + a²b + a²b² = ab(a + b + ab).
Error Explanation: Look closely at the factoring: ab² ÷ ab = b (not a), and a²b ÷ ab = a (not b). While the sum inside parentheses has the right terms, their order was swapped without explanation.
Correct Expression: ab(b + a + ab) = ab(a + b + ab).
Problem 12: (x + 2)(x + 5) Flawed Text: (x + 2)(x + 5) = (x + 2)x + (x + 2)5 = x² + 2x + 5x + 10 = x² + 7x + 10.
Error Explanation: This expansion is 100% CORRECT! It serves as a model textbook example of rigorous step-by-step distribution.

End-of-Chapter Q4 (p. 155): The Calendar 2 × 2 Square Diagonal Invariant

[ a        a + 1 ]
[ a + 7    a + 8 ]
Step 3: Algebraic Proof of Invariance Diagonal Product 1 (Top-Right × Bottom-Left):
P₁ = (a + 1)(a + 7) = a² + 7a + a + 7 = a² + 8a + 7.
Diagonal Product 2 (Top-Left × Bottom-Right):
P₂ = a(a + 8) = a² + 8a.
Now subtract P₂ from P₁:
P₁ - P₂ = (a² + 8a + 7) - (a² + 8a) = 7.

End-of-Chapter Q5 (p. 155): Rigorous Verification of Four Mathematical Claims

Question: Verify which of the following statements are true or false, providing complete algebraic proofs:
(i) (k + 1)(k + 2) - (k + 3) is always 2.
(ii) (2q + 1)(2q - 3) is a multiple of 4.
(iii) Squares of even numbers are multiples of 4, and squares of odd numbers are 1 more than multiples of 8.
(iv) (6n + 2)² - (4n + 3)² is 5 less than a square number.

Statement (i): FALSE Expand: (k + 1)(k + 2) - (k + 3) = (k² + 3k + 2) - k - 3 = k² + 2k - 1.
For k = 1: 1² + 2(1) - 1 = 2 (holds here).
For k = 2: 2² + 2(2) - 1 = 4 + 4 - 1 = 7 ≠ 2!
Since it depends on k, it is NOT always 2. Statement is FALSE.
Statement (ii): FALSE Expand: (2q + 1)(2q - 3) = 4q² - 6q + 2q - 3 = 4q² - 4q - 3 = 4(q² - q) - 3.
Dividing by 4 leaves a remainder of -3 ≡ 1 (mod 4). It is an odd number, so it can NEVER be a multiple of 4! Statement is FALSE.
Statement (iii): TRUE (Exhaustive Proof) • Even Numbers: Any even number is 2k. Its square is (2k)² = 4k², which is an exact multiple of 4.
• Odd Numbers: Any odd number is 2k + 1. Its square is:
(2k + 1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 4k(k + 1) + 1.
Notice the factor k(k + 1): this is the product of two consecutive integers, so one of them MUST be even! Therefore, k(k + 1) = 2m for some integer m.
Substituting: 4(2m) + 1 = 8m + 1.
Thus, every odd square is 1 more than a multiple of 8! Statement is TRUE.
Statement (iv): FALSE Expand (6n + 2)² - (4n + 3)²:
= (36n² + 24n + 4) - (16n² + 24n + 9) = 20n² - 5 = 5(4n² - 1).
For n = 1: 20(1) - 5 = 15 (which is 16 - 1 = 4² - 1, 1 less than a square, not 5 less).
For n = 2: 20(4) - 5 = 75 (not 5 less than any square). Statement is FALSE.
Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway The proof in (iii) that (2k + 1)² = 8m + 1 is a classic number theory theorem frequently tested in competitive math!

The 5 Deadliest Student Blunders in Chapter 6

Trap 1: The Omitted Middle Cross-Term in Binomial Squaring
The Flawed Student Way

Writing: (3x + 4y)² = 9x² + 16y².

Why it fails: The student distributed the exponent 2 across addition as if it were multiplication, completely forgetting the two cross-rectangles of area 3x × 4y!

The 100% CBSE Topper Way

Writing: (3x + 4y)² = (3x)² + 2(3x)(4y) + (4y)² = 9x² + 24xy + 16y².

Rule: The square of a binomial always expands into a trinomial having the middle term 2ab.

Trap 2: Adding Unlike Algebraic Terms (Adding Exponents)
The Flawed Student Way

Writing: 5w² + 6w = 11w³ or 11w².

Why it fails: The student added the coefficients and either added the exponents (confusing multiplication with addition) or ignored the different degrees.

The 100% CBSE Topper Way

Writing: 5w² + 6w cannot be added! It can only be factored as w(5w + 6).

Rule: w² (area) and w (length) are unlike terms. Never add unlike terms!

Trap 3: Distributing Over Multiplication Instead of Addition
The Flawed Student Way

Writing: 2(3a × 4b) = 6a × 8b = 48ab.

Why it fails: The student distributed the factor 2 to both 3a and 4b, effectively multiplying the product by 4 instead of 2!

The 100% CBSE Topper Way

Writing: 2(3a × 4b) = 2 × (12ab) = 24ab.

Rule: Distributivity applies exclusively over addition or subtraction: a(b + c), NOT a(b × c).

Trap 4: Misapplying Signs in (a - b)² (Writing -b² at the end)
The Flawed Student Way

Writing: (a - b)² = a² - 2ab - b².

Why it fails: The student assumed that since the binomial has a minus sign, all following terms should be negative.

The 100% CBSE Topper Way

Writing: (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b².

Rule: (-b) × (-b) = +b². The square of any real number is always non-negative.

Trap 5: Forgetting to Square Numerical Coefficients
The Flawed Student Way

Writing: (5x)² = 5x².

Why it fails: The student attached the power 2 only to the variable x, ignoring the coefficient 5.

The 100% CBSE Topper Way

Writing: (5x)² = 5² × x² = 25x².

Rule: The power on a product applies to every factor: (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ.

15 High-Yield Rapid Revision Points

  1. The Core Meaning: The distributive property a(b + c) = ab + ac connects multiplication with addition, enabling us to break complex multiplications into manageable sub-parts.
  2. Product Increments: Increasing both factors of ab by 1 increases the product by a + b + 1, where the extra 1 represents the new corner unit.
  3. Identity Definition: An algebraic identity is an equation that is true for ALL numerical values of its variables (e.g., (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²).
  4. Identity 1: (a + m)(b + n) = ab + an + bm + mn. The total change in product is an + bm + mn.
  5. Negative Numbers in Identities: Because integers respect signs, Identity 1 handles subtractions automatically: (a - 2)(b + 3) = ab + 3a - 2b - 6.
  6. Like Terms Rule: Terms can only be added or subtracted if they share the exact same variables with the exact same exponents (e.g., ba + ab = 2ab, but a² + a cannot be combined).
  7. Historical Origin (Khanda-guṇanam): Brahmagupta (628 CE) in Brahmasphuṭasiddhānta (Verse 12.55) gave the first explicit algebraic definition of distributivity as "multiplication by parts".
  8. Mental Shortcuts (Ista-guṇana): Brahmagupta's ista-guṇana simplifies multiplications with 11, 101, 1001, and 99 using base expansions like (10 + 1) and (100 - 1).
  9. One-Line × 11 Algorithm: Copy the units digit, sum consecutive adjacent pairs with carries, and end with the leading digit plus carry.
  10. Identity 1A: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b². Geometrically, it decomposes a square into two smaller squares and two identical rectangles of area ab.
  11. Identity 1B & Inclusion-Exclusion: (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b². We must add back +b² because subtracting two strips of ab removed the corner square twice!
  12. Identity 1C (Difference of Squares): (a + b)(a - b) = a² - b². The middle terms +ab and -ab cancel out to 0. Geometrically, an L-shaped region rearranges into a rectangle.
  13. Sridharacharya’s Squaring Trick: a² = (a + b)(a - b) + b² allows lightning-fast mental squaring by picking b to round a to a multiple of 10 or 100.
  14. Calendar Invariant: In any 2 × 2 box on a calendar, (a + 1)(a + 7) - a(a + 8) = 7. The difference between diagonal products is universally 7.
  15. Multi-Method Equivalence: Whether analyzing dot sequences (k² + 2k) or shaded park regions (8w(g + w)), all correct geometric viewpoints simplify to the exact same algebraic formula!
