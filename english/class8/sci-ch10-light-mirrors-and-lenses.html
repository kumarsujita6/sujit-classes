Conceptual Architecture of Chapter 10

The entire NCERT Class 8 curriculum for Chapter 10 ("Light, Mirrors, and Lenses") is built around four interconnected pillars:

The Four Fundamental Optical Pillars
  • Pillar 1: Rectilinear Propagation & Laws of Reflection: Light travels along straight-line paths (rays). When it bounces off a smooth boundary, it obeys two universal laws: (1) The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r), and (2) The incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray all lie in the same geometric plane. When light strikes perpendicularly (normal incidence), ∠i = 0° and ∠r = 0°, causing the ray to retrace its exact path.
  • Pillar 2: Spherical Mirrors (Curved Reflectors): Formed from sections of imaginary hollow glass spheres. Concave mirrors (reflecting surface curved inwards) act as converging mirrors that can produce real, inverted images (diminished, same-size, or enlarged) or virtual, erect, magnified images when objects are held close. Convex mirrors (reflecting surface bulging outwards) act as diverging mirrors that always form virtual, erect, and diminished images with an expanded panoramic field of view.
  • Pillar 3: Refraction & Lenses (Curved Transmitters): Unlike mirrors which reflect light backwards, lenses are transparent media that allow light to pass through while bending its direction. Convex lenses (thicker in the center) are converging lenses capable of forming magnified erect images or inverted real images. Concave lenses (thicker at the rim) are diverging lenses that always produce virtual, erect, and diminished views.
  • Pillar 4: Applied Optical Technology & Heritage: From everyday safety (rear-view vehicle mirrors, dental probes, torch beams) and vision correction (spectacles, human eye accommodation) to solar thermal concentrators and Bhāskara II's 12th-century water-mirror astronomy in India.

Study Tip: The "Symmetry Rule" of Mirrors and Lenses

Notice the beautiful scientific symmetry between mirrors and lenses! A concave mirror and a convex lens are both converging instruments that can form both real/inverted and virtual/magnified images. Conversely, a convex mirror and a concave lens are both diverging instruments that can only form virtual, erect, and diminished images. Memorizing this symmetry cuts your exam revision effort in half!

10.0 The Mystery at the Science Centre

During the summer holidays, a curious school student named Meena visited a regional science centre with her family. While exploring interactive exhibits on solar energy and water conservation, Meena and her younger brother stumbled into an intriguing room filled with strange, curved mirrors—often called a Hall of Mirrors.

When Meena stepped in front of the first mirror, she laughed aloud: her face appeared gigantic and stretched! But when her brother stood just a few steps farther away from the very same mirror, he appeared completely upside-down (inverted)! Walking to a second mirror, Meena noticed that she looked like a miniature doll—her entire body was tiny, neat, and upright.

Meena was thoroughly puzzled. In Grade 7 (Chapter: Light: Shadows and Reflections), she had learned that looking into a standard flat bathroom mirror (a plane mirror) always produces an image that is erect (upright), identical in size to the object, and positioned at the exact same distance behind the mirror. Why did these mirrors behave so strangely?

A smiling science centre guide approached and explained: "These are not plane mirrors, Meena! They are spherical mirrors. Because their surfaces are curved inwards or bulged outwards, they bend and steer reflected light rays in dramatic new ways!"

Probe and Ponder: Everyday Curiosities
  • Can we make mirrors that give enlarged or diminished images? Yes! Curved reflecting surfaces alter the paths of light rays, allowing us to magnify tiny details or shrink entire panoramas into a compact glass surface.
  • Why do side-view mirrors on cars say "Objects in mirror are closer than they appear"? Convex side-view mirrors produce diminished (smaller) images to give the driver a wide view of the road. Because our human brain associates smaller size with greater distance, we instinctively assume the trailing car is far away, when in reality it is alarmingly close!
  • Why is there a thin curved line across the lower half of some reading glasses? Those are bifocal spectacles. The upper part contains a lens for distant vision, while the curved lower segment contains a converging lens designed to focus clearly on reading print held close to the eyes.

10.1 What Are Spherical Mirrors?

To understand curved mirrors, you do not need an expensive optical laboratory. You can start right inside your kitchen with a shiny, clean stainless-steel spoon!

The Shiny Metallic Spoon Experiment (Activity 10.1 Walkthrough)

Hold a polished metal spoon close to your face. It has two distinct reflecting sides:

  • The Inner Curved Surface (The Bowl): This surface is hollowed and curves inward. When you look into it from a few centimeters away, your face looks erect and enlarged. But as you slowly pull the spoon backward away from your eyes, your image suddenly flips completely upside down (inverted)! At first, the inverted image is large, but as you move it to arm's length, it becomes smaller and smaller.
  • The Outer Curved Surface (The Bulging Back): This surface bulges outward towards you. When you view your face in it, your image is always erect (upright) and distinctly smaller (diminished) than your actual face. Even as you pull the spoon far away, the image remains upright and shrinks only slightly.
Scientific Definition: Spherical Mirror

A spherical mirror is a curved mirror whose reflecting surface forms part of an imaginary hollow sphere of glass.

Depending on whether the reflecting surface curves inwards or bulges outwards, spherical mirrors are classified into two primary categories:

  1. Concave Mirror (ଅବତଳ ଦର୍ପଣ): A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface is curved inwards (resembling the mouth of a cave). The outer bulging surface is coated with a non-reflecting protective paint over silver/aluminium.
  2. Convex Mirror (ଉତ୍ତଳ ଦର୍ପଣ): A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface bulges outwards towards the light source. The inner hollowed surface is silvered and coated.

Key Geometric Parameters of Spherical Mirrors

To analyse how spherical mirrors reflect light, physicists define several essential reference points:

Geometric Parameter Symbol Scientific Definition & Simple Explanation
Pole P The geometric center of the reflecting surface of the spherical mirror. It lies directly on the mirror surface.
Center of Curvature C The center of the imaginary hollow glass sphere of which the spherical mirror is a part. (For a concave mirror, C lies in front of the reflecting surface; for a convex mirror, C lies behind the reflecting surface).
Radius of Curvature R The radius of the imaginary hollow sphere of which the mirror forms a part. It is the linear distance from the Pole (P) to the Center of Curvature (C), i.e., distance PC.
Principal Axis — The straight imaginary reference line passing through the Pole (P) and the Center of Curvature (C) of the spherical mirror. It is normal (perpendicular) to the mirror at the Pole.
Principal Focus F The point on the principal axis where all rays traveling parallel to the principal axis actually converge (concave mirror) or appear to diverge from (convex mirror) after reflection.
Focal Length f The distance between the Pole (P) and the Principal Focus (F) along the principal axis. For spherical mirrors of small aperture, the focal length is exactly half the radius of curvature: f = R / 2.

A Step Further: How Are Spherical Mirrors Actually Made?

In textbooks, spherical mirrors are conceptually introduced as parts sliced from a hollow glass sphere. However, in modern optical manufacturing, they are never made by slicing hollow glass spheres! Slicing thin hollow glass would produce warped, brittle pieces with uneven optical power.

Instead, highly skilled opticians take a thick, flat circular piece of high-grade optical glass and grind it against a precision curved metal tool using abrasive powders (like carborundum). Once ground to the exact spherical curvature, the glass is polished until optically smooth. Then, in a vacuum chamber, a microscopic, reflective layer of aluminium or silver is vaporized onto the surface:

  • If the reflective metallic coating is applied on the outer bulging surface, light reflects from the inside hollow cavity, creating a concave mirror.
  • If the reflective coating is applied on the inner hollowed surface, light reflects from the front bulging surface, creating a convex mirror.

Distinguishing Between Concave and Convex Mirrors (Activity 10.2)

Suppose your teacher places two unlabeled curved mirrors on a desk with their reflecting surfaces facing upwards. How can you tell which is which without touching their delicate silvered surfaces?

The Side-Profile Inspection Method: Bend down so your eye is level with the table surface, looking directly across the edge of the mirror:

  • In a concave mirror, the center of the mirror dips downward into a shallow depression, while its outer circular rim rises higher. The reflecting surface is curved inwards.
  • In a convex mirror, the center of the mirror bulges upward like a shallow dome, higher than its outer circular rim. The reflecting surface is curved outwards.

10.2 Image Characteristics Formed by Spherical Mirrors

Before analysing how images behave, let us clearly define the fundamental optical terminology:

Optical Foundations: Real vs Virtual, Erect vs Inverted
  • Real Image (ବାସ୍ତବ ପ୍ରତିବିମ୍ବ): An image formed when light rays actually meet (converge) at a point after reflection or refraction. A real image can always be captured on a screen (such as paper or a cinema screen). Real images are always inverted (upside down) relative to the object.
  • Virtual Image (ଅବାସ୍ତବ / କାଳ୍ପନିକ ପ୍ରତିବିମ୍ବ): An image formed when light rays do not actually meet, but appear to diverge from a point behind the mirror or lens. A virtual image can never be captured on a screen; it can only be viewed by looking into the optical device. Virtual images are always erect (upright).
  • Erect Image: An image that has the same vertical orientation as the object (heads up, feet down).
  • Inverted Image: An image that is flipped upside-down relative to the object.
  • Magnified / Enlarged: Image size is larger than object size.
  • Diminished: Image size is smaller than object size.
  • Lateral Inversion: The optical reversal of left and right sides. If you raise your right hand, your mirror image appears to raise its left hand. Lateral inversion is exhibited by all mirrors: plane, concave, and convex!

Everyday Real-World Applications of Spherical Mirrors

Why do engineers and doctors select specific mirrors for distinct tasks?

Applications of Concave Mirrors (Converging Mirrors)
  1. Dentist and ENT Specialist Inspection Mirrors: When a dentist examines a decayed tooth, they hold a tiny concave mirror very close to the tooth (inside its focal point). This produces an erect, virtual, and highly magnified image of the tooth, allowing the doctor to spot microscopic cavities easily.
  2. Torches, Searchlights, and Vehicle Headlights: The bulb is placed precisely at the focus of a curved concave reflector. The light rays originating from the bulb hit the concave surface and reflect outward as an intensely bright, parallel beam of light that travels vast distances into the dark night.
  3. Shaving and Makeup Mirrors: When held close to the face, the user sees an erect, enlarged view of their skin pores and beard hairs, ensuring a clean shave or precise makeup application.
  4. Solar Cookers and Concentrators: Large concave mirrors reflect and converge parallel incoming rays of sunlight onto a single focal cooking pot, producing extreme heat capable of boiling water and cooking food without gas or electricity.
Applications of Convex Mirrors (Diverging Mirrors)
  1. Rear-View and Side-View Mirrors in Vehicles: A driver needs to see traffic behind without turning around. A flat plane mirror would give a true-size image, but its field of view is very narrow. A convex mirror, curving outward, reflects rays from a vast panoramic angle. It forms an erect, diminished image, fitting multiple lanes of traffic, trucks, and motorcycles into a tiny compact mirror.
  2. Decoding the Warning: "Objects in mirror are closer than they appear": Because the convex mirror shrinks the image of trailing vehicles (diminished size), the human brain naturally judges them to be farther away than they actually are. The manufacturer prints this warning to remind drivers to maintain a safe braking buffer!
  3. Sharp Hairpin Bends & Blind Intersections: Large convex mirrors are mounted on hill roads, parking lots, and sharp T-junctions. They allow drivers coming from both blind directions to view each other around the corner, preventing head-on collisions.
  4. Supermarket and Store Anti-Theft Surveillance: Mounted high up in the ceiling corners of department stores, wide-angle convex mirrors give security guards a sweeping view of multiple shopping aisles simultaneously.

10.3 What Are the Laws of Reflection?

In daily life, we see light traveling in straight paths—a phenomenon known as the rectilinear propagation of light. In optical diagrams, light is represented by straight lines equipped with arrowheads, termed rays. The arrowhead indicates the direction of energy propagation. A group of several adjacent rays traveling together is called a beam of light.

Experimental Setup & Fundamental Terminology (Activity 10.4)

To discover the exact geometric rules governing reflection, scientists use a simple apparatus:

  • Take a comb and cover all its openings with black tape except for one single, narrow slit in the middle.
  • Place a plane mirror vertically on a clean sheet of white paper pinned to a table.
  • Direct a bright torch beam through the comb slit so that a razor-thin beam of light sweeps across the paper and strikes the mirror at an angle.
  • Trace the incoming and outgoing light paths on the paper with a pencil.
Fundamental Reflection Terminology
  • Incident Ray (ଆପତିତ ରଶ୍ମି): The ray of light that approaches and strikes the reflecting surface.
  • Point of Incidence (O) (ଆପତନ ବିନ୍ଦୁ): The exact point on the reflecting surface where the incident ray strikes.
  • Reflected Ray (ପ୍ରତିଫଳିତ ରଶ୍ମି): The ray of light that bounces off the mirror and travels back through the same medium.
  • Normal (ଅଭିଲମ୍ବ): An imaginary straight line drawn at right angles (perpendicular, exactly 90°) to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence (O).
  • Angle of Incidence (∠i) (ଆପତନ କୋଣ): The angle enclosed between the incident ray and the normal.
  • Angle of Reflection (∠r) (ପ୍ରତିଫଳନ କୋଣ): The angle enclosed between the reflected ray and the normal.

The First Law of Reflection (∠i = ∠r)

When you measure the angles in Activity 10.4 using a protractor across multiple trials, you obtain the following remarkable result:

Trial Number Angle of Incidence (∠i) Angle of Reflection (∠r) Observation & Invariant Relationship
Trial 1 30° 30° ∠i = ∠r (Angles are identical)
Trial 2 45° 45° ∠i = ∠r (Angles are identical)
Trial 3 60° 60° ∠i = ∠r (Angles are identical)

The First Law of Reflection

First Law: The angle of incidence is always strictly equal to the angle of reflection:
∠i = ∠r

Critical Exam Trap: What Happens During Normal Incidence?

If a ray of light strikes a mirror along the normal (perpendicular to the mirror surface), what are ∠i and ∠r?

Common Student Blunder: Many students write ∠i = 90° because the ray is perpendicular to the mirror!

The Correct Physics: Remember that ∠i is measured from the normal, not from the mirror surface! Because the incident ray coincides with the normal, the angle between them is zero degrees (∠i = 0°). By the first law, ∠r = 0°. Consequently, the ray reflects directly backward along its original incoming trajectory—it retraces its own path!

The Second Law of Reflection & The Bent Paper Experiment (Activity 10.5)

In Activity 10.5, a stiff sheet of chart paper is laid flat on the table, extending slightly beyond the table's front edge. When a torch beam strikes the mirror, the reflected beam is clearly visible tracing across the extended flat paper.

Now, carefully bend the extended portion of the paper downward along the table edge. What happens? The reflected beam instantly vanishes from the bent section! When you bend the paper, you have forced that portion of the paper into a completely different geometric plane. Because the reflected ray continues traveling in the original flat plane of the table, it shoots straight through the air above the downward-bent sheet and cannot leave a visible track on it. As soon as you flatten the paper back out, the glowing beam reappears.

The Second Law of Reflection

Second Law: The incident ray, the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray all lie in the same geometric plane (they are strictly coplanar).

Convergence vs. Divergence: What Happens to Parallel Beams? (Activity 10.6)

Do the laws of reflection apply to curved spherical mirrors? Yes, absolutely! At every single infinitesimal point on a curved mirror, light reflects such that its angle of incidence equals its angle of reflection with respect to the normal at that specific point. Because the mirror's surface is curved, the normals at different points point in different directions!

In Activity 10.6, multiple parallel slits are left open on the comb to project a bundle of parallel rays onto three different mirrors:

  • Plane Mirror: When a bundle of parallel rays strikes a flat plane mirror, all the normals are parallel to each other. Consequently, all reflected rays bounce off parallel to each other. The beam neither spreads nor narrows.
  • Concave Mirror (The Converging Mirror): Because the surface curves inward, the normals at the outer edges slant inward towards the center. When parallel rays hit this curved surface, their reflected paths bend inward towards each other. They converge to intersect at a single, intense common point on the principal axis: the Principal Focus (F). For this reason, a concave mirror is scientifically known as a converging mirror.
  • Convex Mirror (The Diverging Mirror): Because the surface bulges outward, the normals slant outward away from the center. When parallel rays hit the convex surface, they bounce outward, spreading farther and farther apart. They diverge! If you trace these spreading rays backward behind the mirror with dotted lines, they appear to emanate from a virtual focal point behind the glass. For this reason, a convex mirror is known as a diverging mirror.

Harvesting Solar Energy: Concave Mirrors as Solar Concentrators (Activity 10.7)

Because the Sun is located nearly 150 million kilometers away, the rays of sunlight reaching our planet are virtually parallel.

In Activity 10.7, a concave mirror is pointed directly at the blazing midday Sun. The reflected sunlight is directed onto a dry sheet of paper or newsprint. By carefully shifting the distance between the paper and the mirror, you can collapse the reflected light into an intensely bright, tiny circular point: the real solar image formed at the mirror's principal focus.

If you hold the mirror and paper motionless for 2 to 3 minutes, a wisp of smoke curls up, followed by a charred black hole, and the paper bursts into flame! Why? The concave mirror gathers light (and thermal radiation) across its entire large curved surface area and compresses it into a microscopic focal spot. This super-concentrates thermal energy, elevating the temperature far beyond paper's ignition point (~233°C).

A Step Further: Concentrated Solar Power (CSP)

Devices that focus vast areas of sunlight into a small absorption zone are called solar concentrators. In modern green energy projects across Rajasthan and Gujarat in India, giant parabolic concave mirrors track the sun across the sky. They concentrate solar heat onto central pipes containing synthetic oil or molten salt, heating them to over 400°C. This fluid generates high-pressure steam that drives turbines to produce clean, zero-emission electricity day and night!

10.4 What Is a Lens?

Up to this point, we have explored mirrors, which have opaque, reflective metallic backings that bounce light backward. Now imagine looking through a flat, transparent window glass: objects on the other side look normal in shape and size. What happens if the surface of the transparent medium is curved?

The Water-Drop Magnifier Experiment (Activity 10.8)

Take a clear plastic ruler and rub a tiny smear of oil or candle wax onto its surface. Using a dropper, place a small drop of clean water on the oiled spot. Because of surface tension, the water beads up into a smooth, curved outward dome. Now place the ruler over small printed textbook words and look down through the droplet. The letters underneath appear magnified and giant! The water drop, curved outward on top and flat on the bottom, acts as a tiny liquid magnifying lens!

Scientific Definition & Types of Lenses

A lens is a piece of transparent optical material (glass or plastic) bounded by two spherical surfaces, or by one spherical and one flat surface, which refracts (bends) light rays traveling through it.

  1. Convex Lens (ଉତ୍ତଳ ଲେନ୍ସ): A lens that is thicker in the middle and thinner at the edges. It bulges outward in the center.
  2. Concave Lens (ଅବତଳ ଲେନ୍ସ): A lens that is thicker at the outer edges and thinner in the middle. It hollows inward in the center.

Image Formation Through Lenses (Activity 10.9)

When you look at an object through lenses placed at varying distances, you observe distinct optical behaviors:

  • Convex Lens:
    • When held close to the object (inside focal length): The object appears erect (upright) and enlarged (magnified). This is why a convex lens is universally used as a handheld magnifying glass!
    • When moved farther away (beyond focal length): The image flips and appears inverted (upside-down) and real! As you pull the lens still farther away, the inverted image gradually becomes smaller (diminished).
  • Concave Lens:
    • At all distances: An object viewed through a concave lens always appears erect and diminished (smaller than its true size). It never forms an inverted image, and it can never magnify an object!

Refraction of Parallel Light Beams Through Lenses (Activity 10.10)

Set up multiple parallel beams of light from a multi-slit comb and pass them through three transparent media:

  1. Thin Flat Glass Plate: Light passes straight through without altering the beam geometry. The rays remain parallel.
  2. Convex Lens (The Converging Lens): When parallel rays pass through a convex lens, the curved glass refracts (bends) the outer rays inward towards the central principal axis. All rays intersect at a single focal point on the other side. Hence, a convex lens is called a converging lens.
  3. Concave Lens (The Diverging Lens): When parallel rays pass through a concave lens, the thicker edges bend the rays outward away from the principal axis. The rays fan out in a widening cone. Hence, a concave lens is called a diverging lens.

Where Are Lenses Used in Modern Life?

Lenses are indispensable cornerstones of modern technology and human health:

Modern Optical Instruments
  • Corrective Eyeglasses (Spectacles): Human vision defects occur when the eye's natural focusing power is too weak or too strong. Nearsightedness (Myopia) is corrected using concave lenses to diverge light onto the retina, while farsightedness (Hypermetropia) is corrected using convex lenses to converge light earlier.
  • Handheld Magnifying Glasses: A single convex lens that produces an enlarged, upright virtual image when held close to tiny objects (stamps, insects, map details).
  • Smartphone and DSLR Cameras: Precise combinations of several convex and concave glass elements that focus incoming light onto sensitive digital CMOS sensors to capture sharp photos.
  • Compound Microscopes: Utilize two convex lenses (the objective lens and the eyepiece lens) to magnify invisible bacteria, plant cells, and blood corpuscles up to 2,000 times!
  • Astronomical Telescopes: Huge lenses or combinations of curved mirrors gather faint light from distant galaxies, nebulae, and Saturn's rings.
  • The Living Human Eye: Inside every human eye sits a flexible, transparent convex lens made of living proteins. Tiny ciliary muscles alter its curvature continuously (a process called accommodation), enabling us to effortlessly switch focus from a book 25 cm away to stars millions of light-years away!

10.5 Refraction Wonders & Indian Scientific Heritage

The Mystery of the Bent Pencil in Water (Exercise 12 Explained)

When you place a straight wooden pencil into a transparent glass tumbler half-filled with water and view it from the side:

  1. The submerged portion of the pencil appears displaced and bent at the water's surface.
  2. The submerged pencil also appears distinctly thicker and magnified than the portion in the air!

Scientific Explanation: This dramatic illusion is caused by the refraction of light. Light travels slower in water than in air. When light rays reflecting from the underwater pencil emerge through the water surface into air, they bend away from the normal. When these bent rays reach our eyes, our brain traces them back in straight lines, causing the submerged pencil to appear shifted upwards. Furthermore, the round cylindrical glass tumbler filled with water acts just like a curved convex lens, magnifying the width of the submerged pencil!

Our Scientific Heritage: Bhāskara II and Medieval Indian Astronomical Optics

More than 800 years ago in 12th-century India, the legendary mathematician and astronomer Bhāskara II (Bhāskarāchārya) and his contemporaries developed brilliant observational techniques to map the sky without glass telescopes.

To eliminate physical neck strain and stabilize nighttime observations, astronomers placed large, shallow bowls filled with perfectly still water or mercury on the ground. The perfectly flat, smooth liquid surface functioned as a flawless liquid plane mirror, reflecting crisp images of stars, constellations, and planets. By sighting these reflected images through calibrated wooden sighting tubes mounted on graduated angular quadrants, Indian astronomers calculated the exact angular positions, celestial latitudes, and orbits of planets with astonishing accuracy!

While formal mathematical theorems of reflection were recorded later in Western texts, these historical instruments prove that ancient Indian scientists thoroughly mastered and applied the laws of reflection in advanced scientific practice!

Activity 10.1 Investigating Curved Reflections with a Shiny Spoon
Step 1: Aim & Apparatus

Aim: To observe and compare images formed by the inner (concave) and outer (convex) curved surfaces of a polished metallic spoon at various distances.
Apparatus: A clean, highly polished stainless-steel spoon.

Step 2: Step-by-Step Procedure
  1. Hold the inner curved bowl of the spoon close (3–5 cm) to your face and inspect your reflection.
  2. Slowly pull the spoon backward away from your eyes to arm's length while closely watching the image orientation and size.
  3. Flip the spoon over so the outer bulging surface faces you, and repeat the observation from close up to arm's length.
Step 3: Precise Observations
  • Inner surface (Close): The image of the face is erect (upright) and distinctly enlarged (magnified).
  • Inner surface (Moved away): As the spoon passes a certain distance, the image suddenly flips upside-down (inverted)! At first it is large and inverted; at arm's length, it is small and inverted.
  • Outer surface (Close & Far): The image is always erect (upright) and distinctly diminished (smaller than the face). Moving the spoon farther shrinks the image slightly, but it never turns upside-down.
Step 4: Scientific Reasoning & Analysis

The inner hollow surface acts as a concave mirror. When an object is within its focal length, it creates an erect, magnified virtual image. Beyond the focus, reflected rays physically intersect in front of the spoon, producing an inverted real image. The outer bulging surface acts as a convex mirror, which diverges reflected rays at all object distances, forming only erect, diminished virtual images.

Step 5: Conclusion & Precautions

Conclusion: A concave surface can produce both erect magnified and inverted real images depending on distance, whereas a convex surface exclusively forms erect diminished images.
Precaution: Use an unblemished, scratch-free spoon under bright, even illumination.

Activity 10.3 Image Characteristics of a Toy in Spherical Mirrors

Aim: To investigate how image size, orientation (erect vs inverted), and lateral inversion change as an object is moved in front of concave and convex mirrors.

In a concave mirror, an object inside the focus produces diverging reflected rays that form a virtual, erect, magnified image. Activity 10.5 Proving the Coplanar Nature of Reflection (Second Law)
Step 1: Aim & Apparatus

Aim: To prove that the incident ray, normal, and reflected ray all lie within the same geometric plane.
Apparatus: Setup from Activity 10.4, stiff chart paper extending beyond the table edge.

Step 2: Step-by-Step Procedure
  1. Position the chart paper so that roughly 8–10 cm overhangs the edge of the table.
  2. Shine the light beam onto the mirror so the reflected ray is clearly visible across the flat overhanging portion.
  3. Carefully bend the overhanging portion downward along the table edge and observe whether the reflected ray is still visible on the bent sheet.
  4. Return the paper to its flat horizontal position and observe.
Step 3: Precise Observations

The moment the paper is bent downwards, the reflected light ray disappears from the bent surface! When the paper is brought back to the flat horizontal plane, the reflected beam instantly reappears.

Step 4: Scientific Reasoning & Analysis

The incident ray and the normal define a unique geometric plane (the plane of the table). The reflected ray travels exclusively within this same plane. Bending the paper creates a separate geometric plane. Because the reflected ray cannot bend in mid-air to follow the paper, it travels over the crease into empty space and is no longer intercepted by the paper surface.

Step 5: Conclusion & Precautions

Conclusion: The Second Law of Reflection is verified: the incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray all lie in the same plane.
Precaution: Make a clean, sharp bend exactly along the table edge without shifting the mirror base.

Activity 10.6 Parallel Ray Reflections: Plane vs Concave vs Convex
Step 1: Aim & Apparatus

Aim: To investigate how multiple parallel light rays reflect from plane, concave, and convex mirrors.
Apparatus: Multi-slit comb, bright torch, plane mirror, concave mirror, convex mirror, mirror stands, large white drawing paper.

Step 2: Step-by-Step Procedure
  1. Leave several slits of the comb open to produce a bundle of parallel rays.
  2. Direct the parallel beam sequentially onto: (a) a plane mirror, (b) a concave mirror, and (c) a convex mirror.
  3. Observe and trace the behavior of the reflected beams on the paper in each case.
Step 3: Precise Observations
  • Plane Mirror: The reflected rays remain strictly parallel to one another.
  • Concave Mirror: The reflected rays bend inward and converge towards each other, crossing at a single focal point.
  • Convex Mirror: The reflected rays spread outward (diverge), fanning away from one another.
Step 4: Scientific Reasoning & Analysis

Although each individual ray obeys ∠i = ∠r, the normals to a curved surface are not parallel. On a concave mirror, the surface slopes inward, tilting the normals towards the principal axis and causing reflected rays to converge. On a convex mirror, the surface bulges outward, tilting the normals outward and causing reflected rays to diverge.

Step 5: Conclusion & Precautions

Conclusion: Concave mirrors are converging mirrors; convex mirrors are diverging mirrors.
Precaution: Position the comb parallel to the mirror's central axis so incoming rays are truly parallel.

Activity 10.7 Concentrating Solar Energy with a Concave Mirror
Step 1: Aim & Apparatus

Aim: To concentrate parallel sunlight onto a piece of paper using a concave mirror and observe the thermal effects.
Apparatus: Concave mirror (15–20 cm focal length), thin sheet of dry paper / newspaper.

Step 2: Step-by-Step Procedure
  1. Face the concave mirror towards the bright midday Sun.
  2. Hold a sheet of paper in the path of the reflected light beam.
  3. Adjust the paper distance until the reflected sunlight concentrates into the smallest, sharpest, brightest possible circular spot.
  4. Hold both the mirror and paper completely steady for 2–3 minutes.
Step 3: Precise Observations

The bright focal spot becomes intensely hot. After about 60–90 seconds, smoke begins to curl from the paper. A small brown/black charred spot develops, and the paper catches fire or burns through.

Step 4: Scientific Reasoning & Analysis

Because the Sun is at optical infinity, its rays arrive parallel to the principal axis. The concave mirror reflects and converges all these rays to its principal focus. This concentrates light and infrared heat energy from a large surface area onto a tiny fraction of a square millimeter, rapidly exceeding paper's ignition temperature (~233°C).

Step 5: Conclusion & Precautions

Conclusion: A concave mirror converges parallel light rays into a real, intensely energetic focal spot. This is the operating principle of solar cookers and concentrators.
Safety Precaution: NEVER look directly at the Sun or into the mirror reflecting the Sun, as it can cause permanent retinal blindness. Do not point the focal spot at skin or clothing.

Activity 10.8 The Water-Drop Magnifying Lens
Step 1: Aim & Apparatus

Aim: To demonstrate that a curved transparent water droplet acts as a magnifying convex lens.
Apparatus: Flat transparent plastic ruler (or glass strip), drop of cooking oil or wax, water, dropper, printed textbook page.

Step 2: Step-by-Step Procedure
  1. Rub a very thin smear of oil or wax onto a transparent plastic ruler.
  2. Using a dropper, place a single neat drop of water onto the oiled area. (The oil causes high surface tension, forming a rounded hemisphere).
  3. Lay the ruler flat over fine printed text on a book and look directly down through the water droplet.
Step 3: Precise Observations

The printed letters viewed through the water droplet appear significantly enlarged, bold, and magnified compared to the surrounding letters viewed through plain flat plastic.

Step 4: Scientific Reasoning & Analysis

Surface tension pulls the water droplet into a dome that is thicker in the middle and thinner at the perimeter. This dome shape is geometrically identical to a plano-convex lens. When held close to an object, a convex lens refracts light rays so they enter the eye at a wider visual angle, forming an erect, magnified virtual image.

Step 5: Conclusion & Precautions

Conclusion: Any transparent medium with an outward-curving boundary can function as a converging, magnifying lens.
Precaution: Use only a tiny drop of oil; an excess makes the surface cloudy and degrades image clarity.

1. Definitions of Spherical Mirrors [1 Mark]

A spherical mirror is a curved mirror whose reflective surface forms part of an imaginary hollow sphere of glass.
• Concave Mirror: Reflecting surface curves inward like a cave; outer bulging surface is coated.
• Convex Mirror: Reflecting surface bulges outward; inner hollow surface is coated.

2. Action on Parallel Rays (Convergence vs Divergence) [1 Mark]

• When parallel rays strike a concave mirror, they reflect inward and converge at a single point called the Principal Focus (F). Hence it is a converging mirror.
• When parallel rays strike a convex mirror, they reflect outward and spread apart (diverge), appearing to originate from a virtual focus behind the mirror. Hence it is a diverging mirror.

3. Image Variations with Distance [1.5 Marks]

• Concave Mirror: When the object is held close (inside focal length), it produces an erect, enlarged, virtual image. As the object is moved farther beyond the focus, the image becomes real, inverted, initially enlarged, then of equal size at C, and finally diminished at large distances.
• Convex Mirror: At all distances, it strictly produces erect, diminished, virtual images. As distance increases, the image remains erect but shrinks slightly.

4. Practical Applications & Justifications [1.5 Marks]

• Concave Mirror Applications:
  (a) Dentist Mirror: Held close to teeth to produce a magnified, erect virtual image of tiny cavities.
  (b) Torch / Headlight Reflector: Bulb at focus produces a powerful parallel beam.
• Convex Mirror Applications:
  (a) Vehicle Side-View Mirror: Forms an erect, diminished image giving a wide panoramic field of view of trailing traffic.
  (b) Blind Turn / Intersection Mirror: Allows drivers on hairpin curves to see oncoming vehicles around the corner.

Question: Describe the experimental investigation of solar concentrators. Explain why a concave mirror ignites paper while a plane mirror cannot, outline the complete engineering design and working principle of a domestic parabolic solar cooker, and discuss its ecological benefits for Indian villages.

1. The Principle of Solar Concentration [1.5 Marks]

Sunlight travels across space as virtually parallel rays. When these parallel rays hit a plane mirror, they bounce off parallel to each other, distributing their heat over an area equal to the mirror itself without raising temperature significantly.
In contrast, when parallel sunrays strike a concave mirror, they converge to a single focal point (Principal Focus). All the solar radiant energy collected over the mirror's entire curved area is compressed into a microscopic spot, generating temperatures above 250°C and causing dry paper to ignite.

2. Engineering Design of a Domestic Solar Cooker [2 Marks]

• Reflector: A curved parabolic concave dish lined with high-reflectivity mirror film or polished aluminium facets.
• Focal Cooking Stand: A rigid insulated frame positioning a matte-black stainless-steel cooking vessel precisely at the dish's principal focus. The black coating maximizes thermal absorption.
• Sun-Tracking Swivel Mount: A mechanical hinge that allows the user to rotate the dish every 30 minutes to follow the Sun across the sky.

3. Ecological & Socio-Economic Impact [1.5 Marks]

• Eliminates dependency on expensive LPG cylinders and kerosene in remote rural regions.
• Prevents deforestation by reducing the consumption of firewood for cooking.
• Zero carbon emissions, combating global climate change and reducing indoor air pollution from smoky cooking fires.

Marking Scheme (5 Marks Total): Physics of concentration vs plane mirror (1.5M) + Parabolic cooker design & components (2M) + Rural ecological benefits (1.5M).
LAQ 4 (5M) Advanced Geometric Ray Optics & Angle Problems

Question: Solve the following three geometric optics problems step-by-step:
(a) A light ray strikes a plane mirror making an angle of 35° with the mirror surface. Calculate: (i) Angle of incidence, (ii) Angle of reflection, (iii) Angle of deviation δ.
(b) A ray of light falls on a mirror along the normal. State the values of ∠i and ∠r and describe its subsequent path.
(c) If a plane mirror is rotated by an angle of 15°, through what angle does the reflected ray rotate?

Part (a) Glancing Angle Problem [2 Marks]

• Given: Angle with mirror surface (glancing angle θg) = 35°.
• The normal makes 90° with the mirror surface.
  ∠i = 90° - 35° = 55°. [0.5M]
• By First Law of Reflection: ∠r = ∠i = 55°. [0.5M]
• Angle of deviation: δ = 180° - 2i = 180° - 2(55°) = 180° - 110° = 70°. [1M]

Part (b) Normal Incidence [1.5 Marks]

• When a ray strikes along the normal, it coincides with the normal: ∠i = 0°. [0.5M]
• By First Law of Reflection: ∠r = ∠i = 0°. [0.5M]
• The ray bounces straight backward, retracing its exact incoming path. [0.5M]

Part (c) Mirror Rotation Theorem [1.5 Marks]

• When a plane mirror is rotated by an angle α while keeping the incident ray direction fixed, the normal rotates by α.
• The angle of incidence becomes (i + α), and the reflected ray shifts by:
  Δθ = 2α = 2 × 15° = 30°.
• Therefore, the reflected ray sweeps through an angle of 30°. [1.5M]

Marking Scheme (5 Marks Total): Part (a) calculations with deviation (2M) + Part (b) normal incidence angles and path description (1.5M) + Part (c) rotation proof and calculation (1.5M).

Case Study 1 The Science of Mountain Road Safety: Hairpin Bend Mirrors

Case Passage:

"During a school excursion to the Eastern Ghats in Koraput, Odisha, Class 8 students noticed large, round, dome-shaped mirrors installed on metal poles at every sharp hairpin turn along the ghat road. Rohit wondered why flat bathroom mirrors were not used instead. The science teacher explained that sharp mountain curves create 'blind spots' where drivers ascending and descending cannot see oncoming buses. These specially engineered curved mirrors curve outward, gathering light rays from extreme angles to form upright images of oncoming vehicles."

Sub-Question 1.1: Identify the type of mirror installed at these hairpin turns. [1 Mark]

Answer: Convex Mirrors (Diverging spherical mirrors with reflecting surfaces bulged outwards).

Sub-Question 1.2: Why would a flat plane mirror be dangerous in this situation? [1 Mark]

Answer: A plane mirror has a narrow field of view. A driver would only see a small patch of rock wall directly opposite, remaining blind to oncoming vehicles approaching from around the bend.

Sub-Question 1.3: What are the two key image characteristics formed by these mirrors? [1 Mark]

Answer: The images formed are always virtual and erect (upright) and diminished (smaller than actual size).

Sub-Question 1.4: State one other commercial location where this exact mirror is used for safety. [1 Mark]

Answer: In large supermarkets and department stores, mounted on ceiling corners for anti-theft surveillance over multiple shopping aisles.

Case Study 2 Vision Defects and Corrective Lenses in Schoolchildren

Case Passage:

"Aarav sits in the back row of his classroom. He can easily read his science textbook held in his hands, but he struggles to read the blackboard clearly; the chalk letters appear fuzzy. His ophthalmologist informs him that his eye lens is focusing light rays from the distant blackboard slightly in front of the retina rather than directly on it—a condition called myopia (nearsightedness). The doctor prescribes eyeglasses made of curved transparent glass."

Sub-Question 2.1: Which type of corrective lens is prescribed to Aarav? [1 Mark]

Answer: A Concave Lens (Diverging lens, thicker at the rims and thinner in the middle).

Sub-Question 2.2: How does this lens correct Aarav's vision? [1 Mark]

Answer: The concave lens diverges incoming parallel light rays slightly before they enter the eye, shifting the focal point backward so the sharp image forms exactly on the retina.

Sub-Question 2.3: What natural optical element exists inside Aarav's eye? [1 Mark]

Answer: A natural, flexible convex lens composed of transparent living protein fibers.

Sub-Question 2.4: If Aarav holds his eyeglasses close to fine book print, how will the letters look? [1 Mark]

Answer: The letters will appear erect and diminished (smaller) because concave lenses always form diminished virtual images.

PYQ 1 [CBSE 2023] Angle of Reflection and Angle of Deviation

Question: A ray of light strikes a smooth reflective surface such that the glancing angle with the surface is 40°. Find: (a) Angle of incidence, (b) Angle of reflection, and (c) Total angle of deviation.

Step-by-Step Model Solution

(a) Glancing angle θg = 40°.
  ∠i = 90° - θg = 90° - 40° = 50°.
(b) By First Law of Reflection:
  ∠r = ∠i = 50°.
(c) Total angle of deviation δ:
  δ = 180° - 2i = 180° - 2(50°) = 180° - 100° = 80°.

PYQ 2 [CBSE 2022] Dentist Mirror Selection and Distance Trap

Question: Why does a dentist prefer a concave mirror over a plane mirror or a convex mirror? What will happen if the dentist mistakenly holds the concave mirror far away from the tooth?

Step-by-Step Model Solution

• Why Concave Mirror: When held close to the tooth (inside its focal length), a concave mirror forms an erect, virtual, and highly magnified image, allowing the dentist to clearly spot microscopic dental decay. A plane mirror gives no magnification, and a convex mirror diminishes the view.
• If Held Far Away: If held beyond the focal point, the image will suddenly flip upside-down (inverted) and become real and blurred, making examination completely impossible!

PYQ 3 [State Board 2021] Concave Mirror Image Nature at 10 cm with Focal Length 15 cm

Question: An object is placed 10 cm in front of a concave mirror having a focal length of 15 cm. Describe the nature, orientation, and relative size of the image formed. Name one practical device that utilizes this condition.

Step-by-Step Model Solution

• Position Analysis: The object distance (u = 10 cm) is strictly less than the focal length (f = 15 cm). The object is between the Pole and the Principal Focus.
• Nature of Image: Virtual (cannot be captured on a screen; formed behind the mirror).
• Orientation: Erect (upright).
• Size: Enlarged (Magnified).
• Practical Device: Shaving mirror or dentist's inspection mirror.

PYQ 4 [CBSE Exemplar] Can Concave Lenses Magnify or Convex Mirrors Form Real Images?

Question: Answer with scientific reasoning:
(a) Can a concave lens ever form an enlarged image?
(b) Can a convex mirror ever form a real image of a real object?

Step-by-Step Model Solution

(a) No. A concave lens diverges light rays in all positions. The refracted rays always appear to diverge from a virtual focal point, permanently forming an erect, virtual image that is strictly diminished (smaller) than the object.
(b) No. A convex mirror diverges reflected rays for all real object positions. Because the reflected rays spread outwards, they never actually intersect in front of the mirror; they only appear to intersect behind the mirror, forming exclusively virtual images.

PYQ 5 [High-Probability Model] The Submerged Pencil Refraction Phenomenon

Question: Explain with scientific reasoning why a straight pencil partially dipped in a glass tumbler of water appears bent at the water surface and thicker when viewed from the side.

Step-by-Step Model Solution

1. Why it appears bent: Light travels slower in water than in air. Rays of light reflecting from the submerged part of the pencil bend (refract) away from the normal as they exit the water into the air. Our eyes trace these bent rays in straight lines, causing the submerged pencil to appear shifted upward relative to the portion in air.
2. Why it appears thicker: The round cylindrical glass tumbler filled with water acts like a convex cylindrical lens. Because the pencil is located within its focal distance, the curved liquid column magnifies the pencil's width, making it appear substantially thicker.

5 Common Student Blunders & How to Avoid Them

Trap 1: Measuring Angle of Incidence from the Mirror Surface

The Blunder: A problem states: "A light ray makes an angle of 30° with the mirror surface. Find the angle of reflection." Many students rush and write: ∠r = 30°.

Why It Loses Marks: The angle of incidence is defined strictly with respect to the normal, NOT the mirror surface! The given 30° is the glancing angle.

The Topper Fix: Always subtract from 90° first: ∠i = 90° - 30° = 60°. Then by the First Law: ∠r = ∠i = 60°!

Trap 2: Believing Normal Incidence Means ∠i = 90°

The Blunder: A question asks: "What is the angle of incidence when a ray strikes perpendicular to a mirror?" Students think "perpendicular = 90°", so they write ∠i = 90°.

Why It Loses Marks: The normal itself is perpendicular to the mirror surface. When a ray strikes along the normal, there is zero angular separation between the ray and the normal!

The Topper Fix: At normal incidence, ∠i = 0° and ∠r = 0°. The ray retraces its exact path straight back.

Trap 3: Confusing a Concave Mirror with a Concave Lens

The Blunder: Students mix up the convergence and divergence of mirrors and lenses because they share the same prefixes ("concave" and "convex").

Why It Loses Marks: Writing that a concave lens converges light or that a concave mirror is used as a diverging peephole will cost you full marks!

The Topper Fix: Memorize the Criss-Cross Rule:
• Concave Mirror = Convex Lens = CONVERGING
• Convex Mirror = Concave Lens = DIVERGING

Trap 4: Assuming Convex Mirrors Magnify Images

The Blunder: Students reason: "A convex mirror is installed on cars to help the driver see, so it must make things bigger!"

Why It Loses Marks: A convex mirror never magnifies an object! It shrinks objects (diminished size) so that a huge, wide panorama fits onto the small glass surface.

The Topper Fix: Convex mirrors provide a wider field of view precisely by making individual images smaller (diminished) and erect.

Trap 5: Thinking Virtual Images Are Only Formed by Plane Mirrors

The Blunder: Students assume that spherical mirrors and lenses only form real images because plane mirrors form virtual ones.

Why It Loses Marks: Both concave mirrors and convex lenses form virtual images when an object is placed inside their focal length (which is how dentists examine teeth and how magnifying glasses work!).

The Topper Fix: Remember that a convex mirror and a concave lens form virtual images for every single object distance!

15 High-Yield Rapid Revision Points

  1. Rectilinear Propagation: Light travels along straight-line paths represented geometrically by rays with directional arrowheads.
  2. First Law of Reflection: The angle of incidence is strictly equal to the angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r).
  3. Second Law of Reflection: The incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray are all coplanar (lie in the same geometric plane).
  4. Normal Incidence: When light strikes along the normal, ∠i = 0° and ∠r = 0°; the ray retraces its path straight back.
  5. Universal Validity: The laws of reflection apply equally to all reflecting surfaces: plane, concave, convex, smooth, or irregular.
  6. Concave Mirror (Converging): Reflecting surface curves inwards. Parallel incident rays converge to a real Principal Focus (F).
  7. Convex Mirror (Diverging): Reflecting surface bulges outwards. Parallel incident rays diverge as if emanating from a virtual focus behind the mirror.
  8. Focal Relation: For spherical mirrors of small aperture, the focal length is half the radius of curvature: f = R / 2.
  9. Real vs Virtual Images: Real images are formed by actual ray intersection, are always inverted, and can be captured on a screen. Virtual images are formed by apparent ray divergence, are always erect, and cannot be captured on a screen.
  10. Lateral Inversion: The left-right reversal of an object's image; present in all mirrors (plane, concave, and convex).
  11. Convex Mirror Applications: Vehicle side-view mirrors and blind mountain corners because they always form erect, diminished images with an expanded field of view.
  12. Concave Mirror Applications: Torches and car headlights (bulb at focus creates parallel beam) and dentist inspection mirrors (erect, magnified image when held close).
  13. Convex Lens (Converging): Thicker in the middle, converges parallel rays to a real focus; acts as a magnifying glass when held within focal distance.
  14. Concave Lens (Diverging): Thicker at the outer edges, diverges parallel rays outward; always forms erect, diminished virtual images.
  15. Indian Scientific Heritage: Bhāskara II and 12th-century Indian astronomers utilized shallow bowls of still water as liquid plane mirrors and calibrated sighting tubes to track celestial coordinates with extreme precision.
