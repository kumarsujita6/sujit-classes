7.1 What Is Matter Composed Of?

Look around you right now. You can see your study desk, feel your water bottle, write with a pencil, and breathe in fresh air. All these things take up space and have weight (mass). Anything that occupies space and has mass is called matter.

Have you ever wondered what would happen if you kept breaking a piece of chalk into smaller and smaller pieces?

  • First, you break a large chalk stick into two small pieces.
  • Next, you crush those small pieces into fine chalk powder.
  • Now imagine grinding that powder even further using an ultra-powerful microscopic grinder. Eventually, you reach tiny, fundamental units that can no longer be broken down by ordinary means.

These tiny invisible building blocks are called particles! Matter is not a single continuous solid block like a sheet of glass; it is made of trillions of tiny, separate particles packed together.

Teacher's Definition • Matter & Particles:
Matter: Anything that occupies space (volume) and has mass.
Particulate Nature of Matter: The principle that all matter is made up of extremely tiny, distinct particles (atoms or molecules) that are constantly moving and attracting one another.
Did You Know? Indian Philosophy on Matter (Maharishi Kanada):
Thousands of years ago, the ancient Indian sage Maharishi Kanada proposed that if we divide matter (Padartha) continuously, we will ultimately reach the smallest indivisible particle, which he named Parmanu (meaning the ultimate tiny particle). Today, modern science calls these atoms!

7.2 The Four Super-Characteristics of Particles

No matter what state matter is in—whether it is a hard steel rod, a glass of orange juice, or the air inside a balloon—its particles always follow four golden rules:

Particles are Extremely Small

Particles are so tiny that you cannot see them with your naked eye, nor with an ordinary classroom light microscope! Just a single tiny drop of water contains more than 1,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 (1021) water molecules.

Particles Have Empty Spaces Between Them (Interparticle Spaces)

Particles are not fused seamlessly together. There are tiny gaps or spaces between them called interparticle spaces (or intermolecular spaces). Solids have the smallest spaces, liquids have slightly larger spaces, and gases have huge empty spaces between them.

Particles are in Constant, Continuous Motion

Particles are never completely still! They possess thermal energy (kinetic energy of motion). In solids, they vibrate rapidly around their fixed spots. In liquids and gases, they travel and collide in random directions. When you heat a substance, its particles absorb heat energy and move even faster!

Particles Attract Each Other (Interparticle Forces)

Particles experience an attractive pull towards one another called interparticle force of attraction. This force acts like invisible magnetic glue holding matter together. It is strongest in solids, moderate in liquids, and very weak (almost negligible) in gases.

7.3 What Decides Different States of Matter?

Why is an iron nail rock solid, water a flowing liquid, and oxygen an invisible gas? The state of any matter depends on a tug-of-war between two main factors:

  1. Interparticle Forces of Attraction: Tries to pull the particles tightly together into a fixed place.
  2. Thermal Energy (Heat Motion): Tries to make particles vibrate, shake loose, and fly apart.
Property Solid State (e.g., Ice, Iron) Liquid State (e.g., Water, Milk) Gaseous State (e.g., Steam, Air)
Particle Packing Tightly and orderly packed in a rigid grid. Loosely packed with disorderly arrangement. Very far apart with vast empty spaces.
Interparticle Attraction Strongest: Binds particles firmly to fixed positions. Moderate: Strong enough to hold them together, weak enough to slide. Weakest / Negligible: Cannot hold particles together.
Interparticle Space Minimum (very small gaps). Moderate (slightly larger than solids). Maximum (huge empty voids).
Particle Movement Only vibrate and oscillate about fixed positions. Slide, roll, and glide over each other freely. Move at high speed randomly in all directions.
Shape & Volume Fixed shape and fixed volume. No fixed shape (takes container shape); fixed volume. No fixed shape; no fixed volume (fills entire container).
Compressibility Almost incompressible (cannot squeeze closer). Almost incompressible (particles already close). Highly compressible (huge gaps can be squeezed).

7.4 Space vs Settlement: The Sugar vs Sand Experiment

NCERT Activity 7.2 and 7.7 ask a brilliant question that reveals the secret spaces inside liquids:

Activity 7.2 & 7.7 • The Mystery of Dissolving Sugar:
Take a glass beaker filled halfway with 100 mL of water and mark the water level A. Add two spoonfuls of sugar or common salt and stir thoroughly until completely dissolved.
What do you observe? The water level does not visibly rise! Where did the sugar go?
Teacher's Explanation: When sugar dissolves, it breaks down into individual microscopic sugar particles. These sugar particles quietly slip into the existing empty interparticle spaces between the water particles!
What Happens If You Add Sand Instead of Sugar?
Sand does not dissolve in water. Sand particles cannot break down into individual molecules to fit into the microscopic spaces of water. Instead, sand grains settle at the bottom, displace water, and force the water level to rise noticeably!

7.5 Testing Compressibility: The Syringe Investigation

In Activity 7.5, we test how easily solids, liquids, and gases can be squeezed by using three 50 mL plastic syringes (with their nozzle tips closed tightly with a rubber cork):

  • Syringe 1 (Filled with Chalk Powder / Wood): When you push the piston down, it will not budge at all. Solids cannot be compressed because their particles are already touching tightly.
  • Syringe 2 (Filled with Water): When you push the piston, it barely moves. Liquids are practically incompressible because their particles are also very close together.
  • Syringe 3 (Filled with Air): When you push the piston, it slides inward easily with little effort! Why? Because gas particles are spaced very far apart with massive empty spaces between them, so pressing the piston forces them closer together.

7.6 Thermal Energy, Temperature, and Diffusion

Why do you smell hot food from another room, while cold food requires you to lean close? The answer lies in diffusion and thermal energy.

Teacher's Definition • Diffusion:
Diffusion: The spontaneous intermixing of particles of two or more substances on their own, from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, due to their continuous random motion.
Gases diffuse the fastest, liquids diffuse moderately, and solids diffuse negligibly.

How Temperature Affects Diffusion (Activity 7.8):
If you drop a small crystal of potassium permanganate (or blue food coloring) into a glass of ice-cold water and another into a glass of hot water without stirring:

  • In cold water, pink/purple streaks spread out very slowly.
  • In hot water, the intense purple color spreads throughout the entire beaker within seconds!

Why? Heating adds thermal energy. As temperature increases, the particles gain kinetic energy, vibrate faster, travel at higher speeds, and collide more vigorously, causing diffusion to speed up dramatically!

7.7 Changes of State & The Surprising Ice Anomaly

When you heat a solid (like ice), you supply thermal energy. The particles vibrate with greater and greater energy until they overcome the rigid attractive forces holding them in fixed spots. The solid melts into a liquid at its melting point.

Important NCERT Insight • Why Does Ice Float on Water?
In almost all substances, the solid state is more densely packed than the liquid state. But water is a magical exception! When liquid water freezes into ice, hydrogen bonds arrange the water molecules into an open, hexagonal cage-like crystalline lattice with lots of empty spaces.
Because of these empty spaces, particles in ice are farther apart than in liquid water! Hence, ice has a lower density than liquid water and floats safely on top, protecting aquatic life during harsh winters.
Section 3 • Terminology & Concept Bank

Key Vocabulary & Exam Definitions

Quick-reference memory cards for full marks in definitions and one-mark viva questions.

1. Matter

Anything that has mass and takes up space (volume). Made of microscopic particles.

2. Interparticle Spaces

The empty voids or spaces present between constituent particles of matter. Solids < Liquids < Gases.

3. Interparticle Force of Attraction

The attractive pull that binds neighboring particles together. Gases < Liquids < Solids.

4. Thermal Energy

The energy that particles possess due to their temperature and motion. Heating increases thermal energy and particle velocity.

5. Diffusion

The natural process by which particles of two different substances mix freely with each other due to continuous random movement.

6. Melting Point

The minimum temperature at which a solid transforms into a liquid at standard atmospheric pressure (0 °C or 273 K for pure ice).

7. Sublimation

The direct phase change of a substance from solid state to gaseous state without passing through the intermediate liquid state upon heating (e.g., Camphor, Dry Ice, Ammonium Chloride).

'Keep the Curiosity Alive' Exercises (Fully Solved)

Q1. Choose the correct option: The primary difference between solids and liquids is that the constituent particles are... NCERT MCQ

Correct Answer: (iv) closely packed in solids and move past each other in liquids.

Teacher's Explanation:

  • In solids, strong interparticle forces lock particles into fixed positions where they can only vibrate.
  • In liquids, interparticle forces are slightly weaker, allowing particles to slide, glide, and move past each other while maintaining a fixed total volume.
Q2. Which of the following statements are true? Correct the false statements. True / False

(i) Melting ice into water is an example of the transformation of a solid into a liquid.
TRUE — Ice absorbs thermal energy from the surroundings, breaking down its rigid lattice into flowing liquid water.

(ii) Melting process involves a decrease in interparticle attractions during the transformation.
TRUE — The added heat gives particles enough kinetic energy to partially overcome and weaken the rigid interparticle attractive forces.

(iii) Solids have a fixed shape and a fixed volume.
TRUE — Because particles are tightly locked in fixed positions with minimum spacing, solids retain both their shape and volume.

(iv) The interparticle interactions in solids are very strong, and the interparticle spaces are very small.
TRUE — Strong attractive forces pull particles closely together, leaving minimal empty voids.

(v) When we heat camphor in one corner of a room, the fragrance reaches all corners of the room.
TRUE — Camphor sublimes into gas particles that rapidly diffuse through the air to all corners.

(vi) On heating, we are adding energy to the camphor, and the energy is released as a smell.
FALSE — Correction: Energy itself is not released as a smell! Smell is matter. Heating adds thermal energy to the solid camphor, causing its particles to vaporize into camphor gas. These gaseous camphor particles travel through the air, enter our nose, and trigger our sense of smell.

Q3. If we could remove all the constituent particles from a chair, what would happen? (Choose with justification) Conceptual

Correct Option: (iii) Nothing of the chair will remain.

Justification:

  • Matter is not an empty hollow shell; every single property of the chair (its mass, volume, wood, color, and hardness) is made entirely of its constituent particles (atoms and molecules).
  • If every single particle is removed, there is no matter left. Therefore, absolutely nothing of the chair will remain.
Q4. Why do gases mix easily, while solids do not? Reasoning

Answer:

  1. Huge Interparticle Spaces: Gas particles have very large empty spaces between them, providing plenty of room for particles of another gas to enter and mix freely. In solids, particles are tightly packed with minimal space.
  2. High Speed of Movement: Gas particles have very high thermal energy and move randomly at high speeds in all directions, causing rapid diffusion. In solids, particles are bound to fixed positions and cannot travel to intermix.
Q5. When spilled on the table, milk in a glass tumbler flows and spreads out, but the glass tumbler stays in the same shape. Justify this statement. Application

Justification:

  • Milk is a liquid: Its particles are held together by moderate interparticle forces and can easily slide over one another. When spilled, gravity causes the particles to flow and spread out across the table surface.
  • The glass tumbler is a solid: Its constituent particles are bound tightly by extremely strong interparticle forces in a rigid arrangement. The particles cannot slide past each other, so the tumbler maintains its definite rigid shape and does not flow.
Q6. Represent diagrammatically the changes in the arrangement of particles as ice melts and transforms into water vapour. Diagram

Step-by-Step Transition:

  • Ice (Solid): Rigid crystalline cage arrangement → particles vibrate at fixed points.
  • Melting (+ Heat): Thermal energy disrupts the lattice → particles move closer into a flowing liquid arrangement.
  • Water (Liquid): Particles are close but slide and roll past each other freely.
  • Vaporization (+ Heat): Thermal energy overcomes liquid attractions → particles break away entirely.
  • Water Vapour (Gas): High-speed independent particles zooming far apart with huge gaps.
Q7. Draw a picture representing particles present in: (i) Aluminium foil, (ii) Glycerin, (iii) Methane gas. Drawing / States

Classification & Particle Models:

  • (i) Aluminium foil (Solid): Draw a neat, tightly packed 2D grid of identical circles touching each other with minimal space between them, showing a regular crystal pattern.
  • (ii) Glycerin (Viscous Liquid): Draw circles grouped together irregularly at the bottom of the container, slightly touching but not in regular rows, with small gaps allowing them to slide.
  • (iii) Methane gas (Gas): Draw 4 to 5 widely spaced circles scattered across the entire container, each with an arrow pointing in random directions to indicate rapid straight-line motion.
Q8. Observe the candle just extinguished after burning. Identify the different states of wax and match with particle arrangements. NCERT Fig 7.16

Three States of Wax in a Candle:

  1. Solid Wax (Candle Body): The outer cylindrical pillar of wax is hard and rigid. Its particles are tightly packed in an orderly pattern with strong attractions (Matches the Solid model).
  2. Liquid Wax (Molten Pool near Wick): The heat from the flame melts wax into a warm pool. Its particles are loosely packed and slide past each other (Matches the Liquid model).
  3. Gaseous Wax (White Smoke / Wax Vapour): When extinguished, a stream of white smoke rises from the wick. This consists of vaporized wax particles moving rapidly far apart (Matches the Gas model). If you bring a matchstick near this white smoke, the flame jumps back to the wick because wax vapor is flammable!
Q9. Why does ocean water taste salty, even though the salt is not visible? Explain. Teacher's Explanation

Teacher's Step-by-Step Explanation:

  • When salt dissolves in seawater, the salt crystals break apart into individual microscopic particles (sodium and chloride ions).
  • These particles are so infinitesimally tiny (less than 1 nanometer) that they cannot be seen with the human eye or even a light microscope.
  • They completely occupy the empty interparticle spaces between the water molecules and spread evenly throughout the ocean.
  • When you taste the water, these dissolved salt particles interact with the taste buds on your tongue, giving a salty taste despite being invisible!
Q10. Grains of rice and rice flour take the shape of the container when placed in different jars. Are they solids or liquids? Explain. Common Trap

Answer: Grains of rice and rice flour are SOLIDS, not liquids!

Reasoning:

  • To classify a substance, we must examine the property of each individual grain or particle, not the bulk heap.
  • Each individual grain of rice (and each tiny granule of rice flour) has a definite shape and a fixed volume that does not change whether it is on the table, in your hand, or inside a round jar.
  • They appear to take the container's shape only because millions of tiny solid particles tumble and pile over one another under the pull of gravity, forming a granular heap.

'Discover, Design, and Debate' Projects

Project 1: Fixing a balloon over the neck of an empty bottle and placing it in hot water. What happens? Experiment

Observation: The deflated balloon slowly inflates and stands upright when the bottle is placed in a tub of hot water!

Scientific Reason: The hot water warms the air trapped inside the glass bottle. The air particles absorb thermal energy, vibrate more vigorously, and move farther apart. Because the volume of the heated air expands (thermal expansion), it rushes upward into the flexible balloon, inflating it.

Project 2 (Debate): 'Gases can spread and fill all available space.' Is this property beneficial or harmful? Debate Summary

Teacher's Balanced Verdict: It has both vital benefits and potential hazards!

  • Beneficial Aspects:
    • Allows life-giving oxygen to spread evenly throughout the atmosphere so every living organism can breathe.
    • Distributes pleasant aromas of cooking food, blooming flowers, and essential pollination scents for bees.
    • Enables uniform dispersion of greenhouse gases that keep our planet warm and hospitable.
  • Harmful Aspects:
    • Toxic industrial leaks (e.g., Bhopal gas leak) or poisonous gases diffuse rapidly across entire neighborhoods, endangering lives.
    • Vehicular exhaust, particulate smoke, and pollutants spread everywhere, causing smog and respiratory illnesses.
    • Unpleasant odors from rotting waste spread easily if not properly sealed.

Section 6 • Exam Traps & Key Takeaways

Board Exam Traps, Common Misconceptions & Memory Tricks

Essential exam alerts so you never lose silly marks in your science exams.

Exam Trap #1: 'Grains of Sugar and Sand flow, so are they liquids?'
Mistake: Students often write: "Yes, because they take the shape of the bowl."
Full-Marks Correction: No! They are solids. You must look at the single grain, not the pile. An individual grain of sugar retains its sharp crystalline angles and fixed volume no matter where it is placed.
Exam Trap #2: 'Do particles expand when heated?'
Mistake: Writing that "the particles themselves get fatter/bigger."
Full-Marks Correction: The particles do not grow in size! What increases is their speed of motion and the distance between them. The empty space between them expands, not the particle itself.
Teacher's Memory Hack • S-P-A-M Rule for Particles:
To easily remember the 4 characteristics of particles in exams:
S → Size: Incredibly small.
P → Power of Attraction: Interparticle forces pull them together.
A → Always Moving: Continuous kinetic / thermal motion.
M → Microscopic Spaces: Gaps between them (Solids < Liquids < Gases).
