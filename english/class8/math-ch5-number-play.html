2.1 The Wonder of Consecutive Numbers & Parity Invariants

Numbers that follow each other in order without leaving any gaps are called consecutive numbers. For instance, 3, 4, 5, 6 are consecutive natural numbers, while 14, 16, 18 are consecutive even numbers.

Definition: Consecutive Numbers & Parity

• Consecutive Integers: Integers with a common difference of 1: n, n + 1, n + 2, n + 3, ...
• Parity: The property of an integer being either even (divisible by 2, written as 2k) or odd (not divisible by 2, written as 2k + 1).
• In any sequence of consecutive natural numbers, the parity alternates strictly: Even, Odd, Even, Odd, or vice versa.

2.2 Sums of Consecutive Natural Numbers & The Powers-of-2 Theorem

Anshu began exploring how numbers can be written as the sum of consecutive natural numbers. He noticed fascinating patterns:

  • 7 = 3 + 4 (Sum of 2 consecutive numbers)
  • 10 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 (Sum of 4 consecutive numbers)
  • 12 = 3 + 4 + 5 (Sum of 3 consecutive numbers)
  • 15 = 7 + 8 = 4 + 5 + 6 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 (Can be written in 3 different ways!)
Fundamental Rule: Odd Numbers as Sum of Two Consecutive Numbers

Every single odd number can always be written as the sum of two consecutive integers! If the odd number is 2k + 1, we simply split it into k and k + 1. For example:
• 15 = 7 + 8 (where k = 7)
• 49 = 24 + 25 (where k = 24)
• 101 = 50 + 51 (where k = 50)

The Powers-of-2 Impossibility Theorem

Can EVERY natural number be written as the sum of two or more consecutive natural numbers?
NO! The powers of 2 (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128...) can never be written as the sum of two or more consecutive natural numbers.

Rigorous Algebraic Proof:
Let a number N be the sum of k consecutive natural numbers starting from a:
N = a + (a + 1) + (a + 2) + ... + (a + k − 1)
Using the arithmetic series formula, N = k × a + [1 + 2 + ... + (k − 1)] = k × a + [k(k − 1) / 2]
Multiply both sides by 2: 2N = k × (2a + k − 1).
Notice that the two factors on the right side are k and (2a + k − 1). Their sum is k + (2a + k − 1) = 2a + 2k − 1, which is strictly ODD! Whenever the sum of two integers is odd, exactly one of the factors must be ODD and the other must be EVEN.
Therefore, for any consecutive sum to exist with k ≥ 2, the number 2N (and hence N itself) must have at least one ODD FACTOR greater than 1!
Since powers of 2 (2m) have prime factorizations containing ONLY 2s (no odd factors at all), powers of 2 cannot be represented as consecutive natural sums!

2.3 Expressing Zero and Negative Numbers as Consecutive Sums

Anshu asked: "Can I write 0 as a sum of consecutive numbers? Maybe I should use negative numbers."

Yes, absolutely! If we expand our number set from natural numbers to the set of integers (ℤ), we can represent 0 in infinitely many ways by choosing an odd number of consecutive integers centered symmetrically around 0:

  • −1 + 0 + 1 = 0 (3 consecutive integers)
  • −2 + −1 + 0 + 1 + 2 = 0 (5 consecutive integers)
  • −3 + −2 + −1 + 0 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 0 (7 consecutive integers)
  • In general, for any positive integer m: (−m) + ... + (−1) + 0 + 1 + ... + m = 0 (2m + 1 consecutive integers).

2.4 The 8-Sign Problem & Three Rigorous Proofs of Constant Parity

Take any 4 consecutive numbers, for example 3, 4, 5, and 6. Place '+' and '−' signs between them. Since there are 3 positions between the 4 numbers and each position has 2 choices, there are exactly 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 possible expressions.

When evaluated, every single one of the 8 expressions produces an EVEN NUMBER! Why does this happen? The textbook presents three brilliant pedagogical explanations:

Explanation 1: The Algebraic Sign-Switch Invariant (2b Rule)

Consider any expression formed by 4 numbers: E1 = a + b − c − d.
Now switch the sign in front of b from positive to negative: E2 = a − b − c − d.
By how much has the value changed? Calculate the difference:
E1 − E2 = (a + b − c − d) − (a − b − c − d)
= a + b − c − d − a + b + c + d = 2b.
Since 2b is always a multiple of 2, the difference between the two expressions is ALWAYS EVEN!
• If you subtract an even number from an even number, you get an even number.
• If you subtract an even number from an odd number, you get an odd number.
Therefore, switching any sign NEVER changes the parity of the expression! Since all 8 expressions can be reached from the all-plus expression (a + b + c + d) by switching signs one by one, and since a + b + c + d = 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = 18 is even, all 8 expressions MUST be even!

Explanation 2: Parity Arithmetic Rules

Recall the fundamental laws of parity addition and subtraction:
• Odd ± Odd = Even
• Even ± Even = Even
• Odd ± Even = Odd
• Even ± Odd = Odd
Notice that whether you add or subtract, the parity outcome is identical! (e.g., Odd + Odd = Even, and Odd − Odd = Even). Therefore, replacing any '+' with a '−' does not alter the parity outcome of the combination.

Explanation 3: Bilateral Positive-Negative Token Model

Imagine positive numbers as green (+1) tokens and negative numbers as red (−1) tokens. Whenever a green token pairs with a red token, they cancel out to zero (0).
When you change a number b from +b to −b, you remove b green tokens and add b red tokens. The net change in the balance of tokens is 2b tokens, which is an even quantity. Thus, whether the remaining tokens are paired up in twos is completely unchanged!

2.5 Breaking Even: Remainder Classes Modulo 4

We know how to identify even numbers (numbers divisible by 2). But when will the sum of two even numbers be divisible by 4?

Every even number falls into one of two categories when divided by 4:

  • Category A (Multiples of 4): Leaves remainder 0 when divided by 4. General form: 4k (e.g., 4, 8, 12, 16, 20...).
  • Category B (Even numbers leaving remainder 2): Leaves remainder 2 when divided by 4. General form: 4m + 2 (e.g., 2, 6, 10, 14, 18, 22...).

Now examine the sum of two even numbers:

  • Case 1: (Multiple of 4) + (Multiple of 4)
    4k + 4p = 4(k + p) ⇒ Divisible by 4! (e.g., 8 + 16 = 24).
  • Case 2: (Leaves remainder 2) + (Leaves remainder 2)
    (4m + 2) + (4n + 2) = 4m + 4n + 4 = 4(m + n + 1) ⇒ Divisible by 4! (e.g., 6 + 10 = 16, 2 + 6 = 8, 14 + 18 = 32). The two remainders of 2 combine to form 4, creating an exact extra group of 4!
  • Case 3: (Multiple of 4) + (Leaves remainder 2)
    4k + (4m + 2) = 4(k + m) + 2 ⇒ NOT divisible by 4! Leaves remainder 2. (e.g., 4 + 6 = 10, 8 + 14 = 22).

2.6 Fundamental Divisibility Theorems

Divisibility is the backbone of number theory. Let a, b, c, M, N be integers:

Core Divisibility Theorems

1. Linear Combination Law: If a divides M and a divides N, then a divides (M + N) and a divides (M − N).
Proof: M = ap and N = aq ⇒ M ± N = a(p ± q). Since (p ± q) is an integer, a divides M ± N.

2. Transitivity / Multiple Law: If A is divisible by k, then every multiple of A is divisible by k.
Proof: A = kq ⇒ c × A = c × (kq) = k × (cq).

3. Factor of Divisor Law: If a number A is divisible by b, then A is divisible by every factor of b.
Proof: Let f be a factor of b, so b = f × m. Since A is divisible by b, A = b × q = (f × m) × q = f × (mq).

4. Coprime Divisibility Theorem: If a number A is divisible by b and also divisible by c, then A is divisible by the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of b and c. If b and c are coprime (gcd(b, c) = 1), then LCM(b, c) = b × c, so A is divisible by b × c!

2.7 The Division Algorithm & Modular Remainder Arithmetic

When an integer N is divided by a positive integer d, there exist unique integers q (quotient) and r (remainder) such that:

Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder   ⇒   N = d × q + r,   where 0 ≤ r < d.

This allows us to classify all numbers by their remainder properties:

  • Numbers leaving remainder 3 when divided by 5: Written as 5k + 3 (or 5k − 2). Examples: 3, 8, 13, 18, 23...
  • Tathagat's Sum Claim: If you take three numbers that each leave a remainder of 2 when divided by 6, their sum is: (6a + 2) + (6b + 2) + (6c + 2) = 6(a + b + c) + 6 = 6(a + b + c + 1), which is strictly a multiple of 6!
  • Remainder Arithmetic: If A leaves remainder r1 mod d and B leaves remainder r2 mod d, then (A + B) leaves remainder (r1 + r2) mod d, and (A − B) leaves remainder (r1 − r2) mod d.
  • The Ancient Pebble Riddle: A quantity of pebbles < 100 leaves remainder 1 when grouped in 2s, 3s, and 5s, but remainder 0 when grouped in 7s. Since LCM(2, 3, 5) = 30, the number must be 30k + 1. For k = 3, 30(3) + 1 = 91 pebbles, and 91 = 7 × 13 (divisible by 7)!
  • The Common Deficit (LCM − 1) Problem: Find the smallest number leaving remainder 2 mod 3, 3 mod 4, and 4 mod 5. Since each remainder is 1 less than the divisor (3−2 = 4−3 = 5−4 = 1), (N + 1) is a common multiple of 3, 4, 5. LCM(3, 4, 5) = 60 ⇒ N = 60 − 1 = 59.

2.8 Base-10 Generalized Place Value Representation

In the decimal (base-10) system, the position of each digit indicates the power of 10 associated with it. For example, a 4-digit number whose digits are a, b, c, d is formally written as:

N = abcd = 1000a + 100b + 10c + d = a × 103 + b × 102 + c × 101 + d × 100

Here, a ≠ 0 (the leading digit of an n-digit number is never zero), and each letter represents a single digit from 0 to 9.

2.9 Algebraic Proofs of Divisibility: End Digits (10, 5, 2)

Why do we only look at the last digit to check divisibility by 10, 5, or 2? Algebra makes it crystal clear:

Let N = ... + 1000d + 100c + 10b + a = 10 × (... + 100d + 10c + b) + a.

  • Divisibility by 10: Since 10 × Q is always divisible by 10, N is divisible by 10 if and only if the units digit a = 0.
  • Divisibility by 5: Since 10 × Q is a multiple of 5 (because 10 = 5 × 2), N is divisible by 5 if and only if the units digit a is divisible by 5 ⇒ a = 0 or 5.
  • Divisibility by 2: Since 10 × Q is a multiple of 2 (because 10 = 2 × 5), N is divisible by 2 if and only if the units digit a is divisible by 2 ⇒ a = 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8.

2.10 Algebraic Proofs of Divisibility: Terminal Block Tests (4 and 8)

Why do we check the last 2 digits for 4, and the last 3 digits for 8?

  • Divisibility by 4: Notice that 100 = 4 × 25. Therefore, any power of 10 from 100 upwards is already divisible by 4!
    N = ... + 1000d + 100c + 10b + a = 100 × (... + 10d + c) + (10b + a) = 4 × [25 × (... + 10d + c)] + (10b + a).
    The first part is always divisible by 4. Thus, N is divisible by 4 if and only if the number formed by its last two digits (10b + a) is divisible by 4!
  • Divisibility by 8: Notice that 1000 = 8 × 125. Therefore, all powers of 10 from 1000 upwards are divisible by 8!
    N = ... + 10000e + (1000d + 100c + 10b + a) = 8 × [1250e + ...] + (100d + 10c + a).
    Hence, N is divisible by 8 if and only if the number formed by its last three digits (100d + 10c + a) is divisible by 8!

2.11 Algebraic Proofs of Divisibility: Sum of Digits (9 and 3)

The divisibility rules for 9 and 3 depend entirely on the remarkable algebraic fact that every power of 10 is 1 more than a multiple of 9:

  • 10 = 9 + 1
  • 100 = 99 + 1
  • 1000 = 999 + 1
  • 10000 = 9999 + 1, and in general, 10k = (99...9) + 1.

Now expand any 4-digit number N = abcd:

N = 1000a + 100b + 10c + d
  = (999 + 1)a + (99 + 1)b + (9 + 1)c + d
  = [999a + 99b + 9c] + [a + b + c + d]
  = 9 × (111a + 11b + c) + (a + b + c + d)

Look at that formula! The first term, 9 × (111a + 11b + c), is always divisible by 9 (and therefore always divisible by 3). The second term is simply (a + b + c + d), which is the sum of the digits!
Two Golden Consequences:
1. A number is divisible by 9 (or 3) if and only if its digit sum is divisible by 9 (or 3).
2. The remainder when any number is divided by 9 is strictly identical to the remainder obtained when the sum of its digits is divided by 9!

2.12 Algebraic Proof of Divisibility by 11: The Alternating Sum Rule

While powers of 10 are always 1 more than a multiple of 9, look at what happens when powers of 10 are divided by 11:

  • 100 = 1 = 0(11) + 1 ⇒ 1 more (+1 excess)
  • 101 = 10 = 1(11) − 1 ⇒ 1 less (−1 shortage)
  • 102 = 100 = 9(11) + 1 = 99 + 1 ⇒ 1 more (+1 excess)
  • 103 = 1000 = 91(11) − 1 = 1001 − 1 ⇒ 1 less (−1 shortage)
  • 104 = 10000 = 909(11) + 1 = 9999 + 1 ⇒ 1 more (+1 excess)
  • 105 = 100000 = 9091(11) − 1 = 100001 − 1 ⇒ 1 less (−1 shortage)

The place values alternate strictly between (+1) and (−1) modulo 11! Expand a 6-digit number fedcba:

N = 100000f + 10000e + 1000d + 100c + 10b + a
  = (100001 − 1)f + (9999 + 1)e + (1001 − 1)d + (99 + 1)c + (11 − 1)b + a
  = [100001f + 9999e + 1001d + 99c + 11b] + [(a + c + e) − (b + d + f)]
  = 11 × Q + [(Sum of digits at odd places from right) − (Sum of digits at even places from right)]

Therefore, a number is divisible by 11 if and only if the difference between the sum of its odd-placed digits and even-placed digits is 0 or a multiple of 11!

2.13 Composite Divisibility Rules & The Mandatory Coprime Condition

To test whether a number is divisible by a composite number C, we can factor C into two smaller numbers: C = a × b. But beware! This rule works ONLY IF a and b are COPRIME (gcd(a, b) = 1)!

The Famous 24 Pitfall (Testing 4 and 6 vs 3 and 8)

Can we test divisibility by 24 by checking divisibility by 4 and 6?
ABSOLUTELY NOT! 4 and 6 are NOT coprime (gcd(4, 6) = 2 ≠ 1).
Counterexample: The number 12 is divisible by 4 (12 = 4 × 3) and divisible by 6 (12 = 6 × 2). But 12 is NOT divisible by 24!
Likewise, 36 is divisible by 4 and 6, but 36 ÷ 24 = 1.5 (not divisible).
The Correct Coprime Test for 24: Since 24 = 3 × 8 and gcd(3, 8) = 1, a number is divisible by 24 if and only if it is divisible by BOTH 3 and 8!

Standard Valid Coprime Tests for Composite Divisors:

  • Divisible by 6: Divisible by 2 and 3 (gcd(2, 3) = 1).
  • Divisible by 12: Divisible by 3 and 4 (gcd(3, 4) = 1).
  • Divisible by 15: Divisible by 3 and 5 (gcd(3, 5) = 1).
  • Divisible by 18: Divisible by 2 and 9 (gcd(2, 9) = 1).
  • Divisible by 36: Divisible by 4 and 9 (gcd(4, 9) = 1).
  • Divisible by 44: Divisible by 4 and 11 (gcd(4, 11) = 1).
  • Divisible by 72: Divisible by 8 and 9 (gcd(8, 9) = 1).

2.14 Digital Roots (Beejank) & Aryabhata II's Mahasiddhanta

The digital root (traditionally known in Indian mathematics as Beejank, ବୀଜାଙ୍କ) of a natural number is obtained by adding all its digits repeatedly until a single digit between 1 and 9 is reached.

For example, for 489710:
Step 1: 4 + 8 + 9 + 7 + 1 + 0 = 29
Step 2: 2 + 9 = 11
Step 3: 1 + 1 = 2 ⇒ Digital root is 2!

Historical Significance: Aryabhata II (c. 950 CE)

In his seminal mathematical treatise Mah&amacron;siddh&amacron;nta (Chapter 15), the great Indian astronomer and mathematician Aryabhata II detailed the method of computing digital roots to verify arithmetic calculations. This method became known internationally as "Casting Out Nines" (navasesha).

Fundamental Mathematical Properties of Digital Roots:

  • Modulo 9 Equivalence: The digital root of N is congruent to N mod 9 (with digital root 9 representing 0 mod 9, or an exact multiple of 9).
  • Multiples of 9: The digital root of EVERY multiple of 9 is always strictly 9!
  • Multiplication Law: DigitalRoot(A × B) = DigitalRoot( DigitalRoot(A) × DigitalRoot(B) ).
  • Addition Law: DigitalRoot(A + B) = DigitalRoot( DigitalRoot(A) + DigitalRoot(B) ).
  • The Repunit Riddle (NCERT Page 19): A number made of 9 identical smallest odd digits (all 1s) with digit sum 9 and digital root 9 is 11,11,11,111 (Eleven crore eleven lakh eleven thousand one hundred eleven).

2.15 Cryptarithms (Alfametics): Rules & Solving Strategies

A cryptarithm (or alphametic) is a mathematical puzzle where letters or symbols disguise digits. To solve them, we must follow three inviolable rules:

The 3 Inviolable Rules of Cryptarithms

1. Unique Mapping: Each letter represents one and only one digit (0 to 9). Two different letters can NEVER represent the same digit.
2. No Leading Zero: The leading (first) letter of any multi-digit number can NEVER be 0 (e.g., in SEND, S ≠ 0).
3. Base-10 Arithmetic: The puzzle strictly obeys standard decimal base-10 addition, subtraction, or multiplication rules including carry-overs.

Key Deductive Strategies:

  • Addition Column Carry: When two 1-digit numbers add up, their maximum sum is 9 + 9 = 18 (or 9 + 9 + 1 carry = 19). Therefore, the carry from the addition of two digits is ALWAYS at most 1! If adding two 2-digit numbers produces a 3-digit number (e.g., AB + CD = EFG), the leading digit E must be 1.
  • Units Digit Multiples: If X × k ends in X, investigate digits whose multiples end in themselves. For example, 5 × odd ends in 5; 6 × even ends in that even digit (6 × 2 = 12, 6 × 4 = 24, 6 × 6 = 36, 6 × 8 = 48); 0 × k = 0; 1 × k = k.
  • Size Bounds: In PQ × 8 = RS, since RS is a 2-digit number (< 100), PQ must be strictly less than 100 / 8 = 12.5. Hence PQ ≤ 12. Since P ≠ Q and P ≠ 0, testing 10, 11, 12 quickly yields PQ = 12 (12 × 8 = 96)!

2.16 Ancient Indian Ethnomathematics: Navakankari

Mathematics is deeply interwoven with traditional Indian culture and strategic board games. Navakankari (known in Karnataka as S&amacron;lu Mane &A形象;ta, in Gujarat as Navkakri, in Rajasthan as Ch&amacron;r-P&amacron;r, and in the West as Nine Men's Morris) is a classic two-player alignment game dating back thousands of years.

  • The Board Topology: 3 concentric nested squares with lines connecting their corresponding midpoints, generating 24 intersection nodes and 16 collinear triples (lines of 3).
  • Game Flow: Each player has 9 pawns. Players alternately place pawns on vacant intersections. Once all 18 pawns are placed, players take turns sliding a pawn along lines to an adjacent vacant intersection.
  • Forming a "Mill": Whenever a player aligns 3 of their pawns in a straight horizontal or vertical line, they form a "mill" and earn the right to remove one opponent pawn (that is not currently inside an opponent's mill).
  • Winning Condition: A player wins when the opponent is reduced to fewer than 3 pawns (making it impossible to ever form a line of 3) or when all opponent pawns are completely blocked from moving.

In-Text Page 112: Anshu's Consecutive Numbers Exploration & 4 Fundamental Questions

Textbook Prompt: Anshu explores sums of consecutive numbers: 7 = 3 + 4; 10 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4; 12 = 3 + 4 + 5; 15 = 7 + 8 = 4 + 5 + 6 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5. He wonders: (1) Can I write every natural number as a sum of consecutive numbers? (2) Which numbers can I write in more than one way? (3) Can we write all even numbers as consecutive sums? (4) Can I write 0 as a sum of consecutive numbers using negative integers?

Step 1: Identifying Core Number Properties

We analyze the algebraic expression for the sum of k consecutive natural numbers starting from integer a: S = a + (a+1) + ... + (a+k−1) = k × a + [k(k−1)/2]. Thus 2S = k(2a + k − 1).

Step 2: Addressing Question 1 & 3 (Can every natural / even number be written?)

NO. Powers of 2 (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32...) can never be written as a sum of two or more consecutive natural numbers. Because k and (2a + k − 1) have opposite parity (one is odd, one is even), 2S must contain at least one odd prime factor > 1. Since powers of 2 have NO odd prime factors, even numbers like 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 cannot be expressed as consecutive natural sums!

Step 3: Addressing Question 2 (Which numbers have multiple representations?)

Numbers with multiple odd prime factors can be represented in multiple ways. The number of ways to write N as a sum of consecutive natural numbers is exactly equal to the number of odd factors of N > 1. For example, 15 has odd factors 3, 5, and 15 ⇒ exactly 3 ways: (7+8), (4+5+6), and (1+2+3+4+5).

Step 4: Addressing Question 4 (Writing 0 using negative integers)

YES. By expanding to the integers (ℤ), 0 can be written as the sum of any odd number of consecutive integers centered symmetrically at 0:
• −1 + 0 + 1 = 0 (3 integers)
• −2 + −1 + 0 + 1 + 2 = 0 (5 integers)
• In general: (−m) + ... + (−1) + 0 + 1 + ... + m = 0 for any natural number m.

Step 5: Concluding Pedagogical Summary

Every natural number EXCEPT powers of 2 can be written as consecutive natural sums. All odd numbers can always be written as a sum of 2 consecutive numbers ((2k+1) = k + (k+1)), and 0 can be represented in infinitely many ways using balanced negative-positive integer sets.

LAQ 3 (5 Marks): Complete Characterization of Numbers as Consecutive Sums

Question: Prove that a natural number N can be expressed as the sum of two or more consecutive natural numbers if and only if N is not a power of 2. Furthermore, demonstrate how to construct the consecutive sum for any given odd factor.

Step 1: Formula for Consecutive Sum (1 Mark)

Let N be the sum of k consecutive integers starting at a ≥ 1 (with k ≥ 2):
N = ∑i=0k−1 (a + i) = ka + k(k−1)/2 = k(2a + k − 1) / 2.
Hence: 2N = k × (2a + k − 1).

Step 2: Parity Difference of Factors (1.5 Marks)

Observe the sum of the two factors on the right-hand side:
k + (2a + k − 1) = 2a + 2k − 1 = 2(a + k) − 1 = ODD.
Since their sum is odd, one factor must be odd and the other must be even. Therefore, 2N MUST possess an ODD FACTOR > 1.
Since any odd factor of 2N is also an odd factor of N, N must have at least one odd prime factor.

Step 3: Powers of 2 Impossibility (1 Mark)

If N = 2m, then 2N = 2m+1. The only factors of 2N are powers of 2 (all even, except 1). Thus, 2N has NO odd factor > 1.
Hence, no valid integers a ≥ 1 and k ≥ 2 can ever satisfy the equation! Powers of 2 can NEVER be written as consecutive natural sums.

Step 4: Construction Algorithm for Any Odd Factor m (1 Mark)

Suppose N has an odd factor m > 1. Let N = m × q.
We can represent N as the sum of m consecutive integers centered at q:
S = (q − (m−1)/2) + ... + q + ... + (q + (m−1)/2).
Example: 35 = 5 × 7. Take m = 5, center q = 7 ⇒ (7−2) + (7−1) + 7 + (7+1) + (7+2) = 5 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 9 = 35!

LAQ 4 (5 Marks): Casting Out Nines Method & Arithmetic Error Checking

Question: Describe the method of "Casting Out Nines" (Beejank check) formulated by Aryabhata II. Prove its mathematical validity using modular congruence. Illustrate by verifying whether 487 × 356 = 173,372 is correct or erroneous.

Step 1: Description of Casting Out Nines (1 Mark)

Casting Out Nines is a rapid arithmetic check where every number is replaced by its digital root (Beejank, remainder mod 9). If an arithmetic operation (addition, subtraction, multiplication) is executed correctly, the digital root of the result MUST match the digital root of the combined digital roots of the operands.

Step 2: Congruence Modulo 9 Proof (1.5 Marks)

Let A = 9Q1 + r1 and B = 9Q2 + r2, where r1, r2 are the digital roots (mod 9).
For multiplication: A × B = (9Q1 + r1)(9Q2 + r2) = 81Q1Q2 + 9Q1r2 + 9Q2r1 + r1r2 = 9 × Q' + (r1 × r2).
Taking modulo 9 on both sides: (A × B) mod 9 ≡ (r1 × r2) mod 9.
Thus, the digital root of the product must equal the digital root of the product of their digital roots.

Step 3: Verification of 487 × 356 = 173,372 (1.5 Marks)

• Digital root of 487: 4 + 8 + 7 = 19 ⇒ 1 + 9 = 10 ⇒ 1 + 0 = 1.
• Digital root of 356: 3 + 5 + 6 = 14 ⇒ 1 + 4 = 5.
• Expected digital root of product = DigitalRoot(1 × 5) = 5.
• Digital root of given answer 173,372: 1 + 7 + 3 + 3 + 7 + 2 = 23 ⇒ 2 + 3 = 5.
Since 5 = 5, the calculation passes the casting out nines test!

Step 4: Limitations of the Test (0.5 Mark)

Casting out nines can detect calculation errors, but it CANNOT detect transposed digits (e.g., writing 173,327 instead of 173,372) or errors that differ by exact multiples of 9.

Case Study 2: Ethnomathematics & Combinatorics in Navakankari

During an inter-school cultural exhibition in Cuttack, Class 8 students organized a tournament of the traditional Indian alignment game Navakankari (Nine Men's Morris). Two students, Rohan and Priya, analyze the game mathematically:

Question 1 (1 Mark): How many total distinct "mills" (lines of 3) exist on the board?

Solution: The board consists of 3 concentric squares. Each square has 4 sides (4 lines of 3) ⇒ 3 × 4 = 12 square-edge mills. The 4 cross-links each form 1 line of 3 connecting the squares ⇒ 4 cross-mills. Total mills = 12 + 4 = 16 lines of three.

Question 2 (2 Marks): Explain why midpoints are tactically superior to corners using graph vertex degree

Solution: The 8 corner vertices connect to only 2 adjacent intersections within their square, giving them degree 2 (or 3 including diagonals if present; on standard Navakankari board, corners connect to 2 lines, so degree 2). The 8 edge-midpoints connect to 2 points along the square plus the cross-link line, giving them degree 4. Nodes of degree 4 offer twice the branching mobility, allowing players to threaten two mills simultaneously (the "double mill" strategy).

Question 3 (2 Marks): What is the minimum number of pawns required to keep a player alive in the game?

Solution: A player must have at least 3 pawns. If a player is reduced to 2 pawns, it is mathematically impossible to form a line of three (which requires 3 collinear pawns), so the game terminates immediately with a loss for that player.

Five Common Exam Hall Blunders (Side-by-Side Analysis)

Blunder 1: Testing Composite Divisibility with Non-Coprime Factors (The 24 Trap)

Students often split 24 as 4 × 6 and conclude that any number divisible by 4 and 6 must be divisible by 24.

The Fatal Mistake:
"12 is divisible by 4 (12 ÷ 4 = 3) and 12 is divisible by 6 (12 ÷ 6 = 2). Therefore, 12 must be divisible by 24!"
Result: Completely wrong. 12 ÷ 24 = 0.5 (Not divisible).
Topper's Correct Approach:
4 and 6 share common factor 2 (gcd(4, 6) = 2 ≠ 1). Their LCM is only 12, not 24!
To test 24, factor into COPRIME pair 3 and 8 (gcd(3, 8) = 1). A number is divisible by 24 if and only if it is divisible by BOTH 3 and 8!
Blunder 2: Assuming Divisibility by 3 Implies Divisibility by 9

Students assume the converse of divisibility: since all multiples of 9 are divisible by 3, they assume all multiples of 3 are divisible by 9.

The Fatal Mistake:
"The sum of digits of 75 is 7 + 5 = 12. Since 12 is divisible by 3, 75 must be divisible by 9."
Result: False! 75 ÷ 9 = 8.33...
Topper's Correct Approach:
Divisibility flows only from multiples to factors: Multiples of 9 ⊂ Multiples of 3. But multiples of 3 are NOT necessarily multiples of 9! For divisibility by 9, the digit sum MUST be a multiple of 9 itself (9, 18, 27...).
Blunder 3: Misaligning Odd and Even Positions for Divisibility by 11

Counting positions from the left instead of from the units digit (right), or counting place-value rank rather than alternate indices, causing sign inversion in remainder checks.

The Fatal Mistake:
In number 328105, student starts from left: "+3 − 2 + 8 − 1 + 0 − 5 = +3". They claim remainder is 3.
Topper's Correct Approach:
Place values alternate starting from the UNITS digit (100 = +1 excess):
Δ = 5 − 0 + 1 − 8 + 2 − 3 = −3.
A result of −3 means 3 short of 11, giving remainder 8 (328105 = 11 × 29827 + 8).
Blunder 4: Assigning Zero to Leading Letters or Reusing Digits in Cryptarithms

Setting a leading letter to 0 or assigning the same digit to two different letters to make an equation work.

The Fatal Mistake:
In PQ × 8 = RS, student writes 10 × 8 = 80, setting Q = 0 and S = 0. Or in SEND + MORE, student sets S = 0.
Topper's Correct Approach:
Strictly memorize the two primary cryptarithm laws:
1. Leading letter ≠ 0 (S ≠ 0, P ≠ 0).
2. Different letters = Different digits (If Q=0, no other letter can be 0).
Blunder 5: Believing Every Natural Number is a Consecutive Sum

Assuming any natural number can be decomposed into consecutive natural numbers, forgetting the Powers-of-2 Theorem.

The Fatal Mistake:
"Every number has some consecutive representation. For 8, let's keep guessing numbers until we find a match."
Topper's Correct Approach:
8 = 23 is a power of 2. Powers of 2 (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64...) possess ZERO odd factors > 1. Since 2S = k(2a + k − 1) strictly requires an odd factor, no power of 2 can EVER be a consecutive natural sum!
