1. Visual Mind Map & Chapter Overview

How did a band of hardy mountain warriors from the rugged Sahyadri ranges of Maharashtra rise to dismantle the mighty Mughal Empire and become the dominant rulers of India before the British? Under the inspiring leadership of Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, the Marathas forged a revolutionary state founded on Hindavi Swarajya (self-rule for all), peerless mountain warfare, an impregnable fort network, and India's first modern indigenous navy!

Fig 3.1: Conceptual Flow of Maratha Emergence, Statecraft, and Expansion.

What was 'Hindavi Swarajya'?

Hindavi Swarajya was the visionary concept formulated by Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj. It meant independent self-rule rooted in the welfare of the native people of the land, free from foreign imperial domination, where justice was delivered equally to citizens of all castes and faiths without religious persecution.

2. Exhaustive Concept Explanations

1. Who Were the Marathas? Cultural Foundations

The Marathas are people native to the rugged Deccan plateau and coastal Konkan region of western India, speaking the ancient language Marathi. In the 13th century, Maharashtra was ruled by the Yadava dynasty from Devagiri (Daulatabad), which was later overthrown by the Delhi Sultanate.

Despite political subjugation, Maharashtra's cultural backbone was strengthened by the great Bhakti movement. Spiritual saints of the Warkari tradition democratised knowledge by composing soul-stirring hymns (abhangas) in simple Marathi:

  • Sant Dnyaneshwar (13th century): Penned the Dnyaneshwari, translating the Bhagavad Gītā into Marathi so common peasants and weavers could understand philosophical wisdom.
  • Sant Namdev & Sant Eknath: Preached social equality and condemned untouchability and empty priestly rituals.
  • Sant Tukaram (17th century): A farmer-merchant poet who taught that true religion lies in compassion for the suffering and oppressed.
  • Samarth Ramdas: Urged youth to build strong physical bodies, organize community councils, and defend dharma with courage.

This spiritual awakening fostered deep social cohesion and a shared regional consciousness, providing the moral foundation upon which Shivaji built his political empire.

2. Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj: The Architect of Swarajya (1630 – 1680 CE)

Shivaji was born in 1630 at Shivneri Fort near Pune. His father, Shahaji Bhonsle, was a military commander in the Deccan sultanates, while his mother, Jijabai, was a woman of extraordinary wisdom and courage who shaped young Shivaji with stories of the Ramayana and Mahabharata, instilling in him a burning desire for freedom.

A. Master of Geography: 'Ganimi Kawa' (Guerrilla Warfare)

Shivaji recognized that his small forces could never defeat the massive, cannon-heavy armies of the Bijapur Sultanate or the Mughal Empire in open-field battles. Instead, he made the rugged Western Ghats (Sahyadris) his greatest weapon:

  • Ganimi Kawa: Sudden ambush tactics utilizing narrow mountain passes, dense mist, and ravines.
  • Maratha light cavalry rode swift, sure-footed Deccan ponies (Bhimthadi horses), struck enemy supply convoys by surprise, and vanished into impenetrable mountain fortresses before enemy artillery could deploy.
Over 300 Forts: Raigad, Pratapgad, Sinhagad. • Triple Wall (Peta): Bastions covering every cliff. • Water Cisterns: Carved rock tanks storing rain. • Secret Escape Routes (Chor Darwaja): Sally ports. • Granaries: Food grain stored for year-long sieges. Ramachandrapant Amatya: 'Forts are the real wealth!'

B. Father of the Indian Navy

Shivaji was one of the very few Indian rulers who recognized that European powers (Portuguese, British, Dutch) were dominating trade because of their naval strength. As noted in the Ādnyāpatra (1715): "The hat-wearers are fully intent on entering this land... Navy and gunpowder are their chief weapons."

  • Shivaji established a royal battle fleet consisting of hundreds of war vessels, including Gurabs (three-masted gunboats) and Galbats (nimble rowing warships).
  • He constructed formidable sea forts right inside the roaring Arabian Sea, including Sindhudurg at Malvan, Vijaydurg, and Padmadurg, with stone foundations laid directly into molten lead on submerged ocean bedrock.

C. Administration: The Ashta Pradhan Council

Shivaji created a well-structured administrative council of eight ministers (Ashta Pradhan):

  1. Peshwa (Mukhya Pradhan): Prime Minister, general administration and royal seal.
  2. Amatya (Majumdar): Finance and accounts minister.
  3. Senapati (Sar-i-Naubat): Commander-in-chief of the military.
  4. Sachiv (Surnis): Keeper of royal correspondence and edicts.
  5. Mantri (Waqianavis): Home minister, intelligence and court records.
  6. Sumant (Dabir): Foreign affairs and external diplomacy.
  7. Nyayadhish: Chief Justice of law and judicial disputes.
  8. Panditrao: Minister of religious affairs, charities, and learning.
Shivaji's Humane Military Ethics

Even contemporary hostile historians (like the Mughal chronicler Khafi Khan) acknowledged Shivaji's exemplary code of conduct:

  • Strict royal orders forbade his soldiers from burning mosques, looting sacred places, or harming women, children, and prisoners of war.
  • If a copy of the holy Quran fell into Maratha hands during a campaign, soldiers were commanded to handle it with reverence and deliver it safely to a Muslim cleric.
  • Shivaji's army and navy included trusted Muslim officers, such as naval admiral Daryasarang and artillery commander Ibrahim Khan!

3. The 27-Year War of Defiance (1681 – 1707 CE)

When Shivaji passed away in 1680, Emperor Aurangzeb believed the Maratha kingdom was finished. Aurangzeb marched into the Deccan with his entire imperial army (over 4,00,000 soldiers, camels, and heavy cannons):

  • Chhatrapati Sambhaji Maharaj (1680–1689): Shivaji's eldest son fought off the Mughals fiercely for nine years without losing a single fort. Captured through betrayal in 1689, he chose horrific torture and martyrdom rather than surrendering his faith and sovereignty.
  • Chhatrapati Rajaram (1689–1700): Moved the Maratha operational headquarters south to the impregnable hill fort of Gingee in Tamil Nadu, tying down the Mughal army in an eight-year siege.
  • Rani Tarabai (The Heroic Queen): Following Rajaram's death in 1700, his brave widow, Tarabai, took charge of the Maratha nation. She personally led cavalry raids, appointed brilliant commanders (like Santaji Ghorpade and Dhanaji Jadhav), and carried the war directly into Mughal provinces (Malwa and Gujarat).

Aurangzeb was worn down by endless guerrilla warfare. In 1707, the 89-year-old Emperor died in Ahmednagar as a broken, defeated man, while the Maratha flag flew high across the Deccan!

4. The Peshwa Era & The Pan-Indian Confederacy

In the 18th century, under Chhatrapati Shahu (Shivaji's grandson), real executive leadership passed to the hereditary Peshwas (Prime Ministers) based in Pune.

A. Peshwa Bajirao I (1720–1740 CE)

The son of Balaji Vishwanath, Bajirao I was a military genius who fought 41 major battles and was never defeated once in his life! He declared: "Let us strike at the trunk of the withered Mughal tree, and the branches will fall of themselves!"

  • At the Battle of Palkhed (1728), Bajirao outmaneuvered the Nizam of Hyderabad through rapid cavalry encirclement without firing a heavy cannon.
  • In 1737, he led a lightning cavalry dash to the outskirts of Delhi, creating panic in the Mughal royal court.
  • He institutionalized two major tax levies across outer territories:
    • Chauth: One-fourth (25%) of land revenue, paid in exchange for military protection against foreign looters.
    • Sardeshmukhi: An additional 10% levy claimed by the Maratha crown as the hereditary chief head (Sardeshmukh) of the Deccan.

B. The Great Maratha Confederacy

As the empire expanded from Tamil Nadu in the south to the Indus River (Attock) in the northwest, power was decentralized among four prominent ruling houses:

  • Scindias (Shindes) of Gwalior: Led by Mahadji Shinde, who modernised the Maratha army with European-drilled infantry and artillery, and became the protector of the Mughal Emperor at Delhi.
  • Holkars of Indore: Led initially by Malhar Rao Holkar and later by the saintly queen Ahilyabai Holkar.
  • Gaekwads of Baroda: Rulers of prosperous agricultural and trading regions in Gujarat.
  • Bhonsles of Nagpur: Expanded Maratha territory eastwards to Odisha and Bengal.
Punyashlok Ahilyabai Holkar: A Model Ruler

Rani Ahilyabai Holkar ruled Malwa from Maheshwar for nearly 30 years (1767–1795). She is remembered as one of India's most enlightened rulers:

  • She held open public darbars every day, listening patiently to the grievances of the poorest citizens.
  • She rebuilt hundreds of desecrated temples and constructed pilgrim ghats, wells, and rest houses (dharmashalas) across the entire length and breadth of India—from Kashi Vishwanath in Varanasi and Somnath in Gujarat to Kedarnath in the Himalayas and Jagannath Puri in Odisha!

C. Admiral Kanhoji Angre & The Maratha Coast

Known as Sarkhel (Grand Admiral), Kanhoji Angre commanded the Maratha Navy from 1698 to 1729. Based out of naval bastions like Kolaba (Alibaug) and Vijaydurg, Angre defeated repeated joint naval assaults by British, Portuguese, and Dutch warships. For over three decades, European merchant ships were forced to buy transit permits (Dastaks) from the Marathas to sail safely in the Arabian Sea.

4. Complete NCERT In-Text Activities & Exercise Solutions

End-of-Chapter Textbook Exercises

Q1. Analyse how geography (particularly mountains and coastlines) guided Maratha military strategy and state formation.

Answer:

1. Mountain Topography (Sahyadris / Western Ghats):

  • The steep vertical cliffs and narrow passes of the Sahyadri mountains made it impossible for bulky Mughal siege cannons and heavy cavalry to manoeuvre.
  • Shivaji constructed over 300 hill forts on isolated peaks. These forts possessed natural water cisterns and sheer drop-offs, making them nearly impossible to starve out or conquer.
  • The mountains enabled Ganimi Kawa (guerrilla warfare)—allowing fast Maratha cavalry to launch night ambushes and melt away into misty mountain ravines.

2. Konkan Coastline:

  • The coastline provided access to maritime trade routes and custom duties. Recognizing that European powers were using sea power to enter India, Shivaji established an indigenous navy and built sea forts (Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg) on rocky islands to protect local coastal trade and prevent colonial expansion.
Q2. Imagine you are creating a short biography of a Maratha leader for younger students. Choose one personality (Kanhoji Angre, Bajirao I, Mahadji Shinde, Ahilyabai Holkar, or Tarabai) and write 3–4 paragraphs highlighting what makes them inspirational.

Selected Personality: Punyashlok Ahilyabai Holkar (1725–1795)

Ahilyabai Holkar was the courageous and benevolent queen of the Malwa kingdom in central India. After losing her husband Khanderao in battle and later her son and father-in-law, she took over the reins of government at Indore with quiet determination, breaking traditional barriers of her era.

What makes Ahilyabai truly inspirational is her motherly devotion to her people. Rather than staying hidden behind palace curtains, she held open daily courts where any farmer, merchant, or widow could approach her directly. She built wide tree-lined roads, dug village irrigation wells, and promoted the famous handloom weaving tradition of Maheshwari sarees, providing dignified livelihood to thousands of craftswomen.

Overcoming the tragedy of personal loss and military threats from greedy rivals, Ahilyabai used royal wealth not for luxury, but to rebuild sacred pilgrimage sites all over India—including the Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi, Somnath in Gujarat, and Jagannath Puri in Odisha. She proved that the highest greatness of a ruler lies in selfless service, simplicity, and cultural preservation.

Q3. If you could visit one Maratha fort today (such as Raigad, Sindhudurg, Gingee, or Pratapgad), which would you choose and why? Research its history, architecture, and strategic importance.

Choice: Sindhudurg Sea Fort (Malvan, Maharashtra)

  • Why Chosen: Sindhudurg is an engineering wonder built directly in the rough waves of the Arabian Sea on the rocky Kurte island, demonstrating Shivaji's visionary maritime genius.
  • History: Constructed between 1664 and 1667 under the supervision of master builder Hiroji Indulkar to keep foreign naval forces (Portuguese in Goa and British in Mumbai) in check.
  • Architectural Ingenuity: Over 4,000 mounds of iron and molten lead were used to bind giant basalt rocks to the underwater seabed. The fort's zigzagging perimeter walls stretch over 3 kilometres with 42 bastions, designed so cleverly that the main entrance (Maha Darwaja) is completely invisible from the sea to confuse attacking enemy ships.
  • Strategic Importance: It served as the premier dockyard and fortress base for the Maratha Navy, enabling them to control Konkan shipping lanes and resist naval invasions.
Q4. The chapter states, “The British took India from the Marathas more than from the Mughals or any other power.” What do you think this means? What evidence supports this idea?

Answer:

  • Mughals were only Figureheads: By the mid-18th century, the Mughal Empire had shrunk to a tiny zone around Delhi. In fact, Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II was a virtual pensioner living under the direct military protection of the Maratha general Mahadji Shinde.
  • Marathas were the True Pan-Indian Sovereign Power: The Maratha Confederacy collected taxes (Chauth) across central India, Gujarat, the Deccan, Rajasthan, and parts of Bengal and Odisha. They possessed the largest armies, cavalry, and artillery networks in the subcontinent.
  • The Decisive Anglo-Maratha Wars: The British East India Company could not conquer India simply by defeating local Nawabs in Bengal. It required three massive, bloody Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818) for the British to finally defeat the Peshwa, Scindias, and Holkars. Only after the fall of the Maratha Empire in 1818 did British colonial rule become supreme across India.
Q5. Compare how Chhatrapati Shivaji and later Marathas treated religious places and people of different faiths. What evidence shows their approach to religious diversity?

Answer:

  • Strict Royal Protective Decrees: Shivaji issued strict standing orders to his troops forbidding desecration of mosques, shrines, and tombs. If a holy Quran was captured during a battle, it had to be handed over respectfully to a local Muslim cleric.
  • Muslim Officers in High Command: Shivaji's administration and military were thoroughly merit-based. His supreme artillery commander was Ibrahim Khan, and his naval admirals included Daryasarang and Daulat Khan.
  • Religious Freedom for Subjects: While Shivaji and later Marathas took immense pride in their Hindu cultural roots and revived Sanskrit state terms (Rajyavyavahara Kosha), they never forced religious conversion, maintained grants to Sufi dargahs, and protected people of all faiths.
Q6. The chapter describes how forts were 'the core of the state' for Marathas. Why were they so important? How did they help the Marathas survive against larger enemies?

Answer:

  • Centres of Government: Maratha forts were not mere military outposts; they were self-contained cities housing the royal treasury, mints, granaries, weapon armouries, and administrative offices.
  • Survival during Massive Invasions: Even when huge enemy armies looted open towns in the valleys, Marathas retreated into their hill forts. The sheer vertical rocks prevented enemy siege machines from reaching the gates.
  • Decentralized Resilience: Shivaji built a network of over 300 interconnected forts. If the enemy captured one fort after months of struggle, the Maratha garrison secretly slipped out through hidden escape sally ports (Chor Darwaja) and relocated to the next fort. As a result, an invader could never deliver a single knockout blow to the Maratha state.
Q7. You have been appointed as the chief designer for Maratha coins. Design a coin that represents Maratha achievements and values. Explain the symbols you chose.

Maratha Gold 'Hon' / Silver Rupee Coin Design:

  • Front Side (Obverse):
    • Central Inscription: Written in ancient Devanagari script: "Śrī Rājā Śivachhatrapati" (Honourable King Shiva, Supreme Sovereign).
    • Symbol of Bhagwa Dhwaj: A swallow-tailed saffron banner representing selfless service, dharma, and renunciation.
  • Back Side (Reverse):
    • Hill Fort Silhouette (Raigad): Symbolising indomitable courage, sovereignty, and unyielding defense of the motherland.
    • Crossed Sword & Bow: Depicting martial valour and preparedness to protect the weak.
    • Surrounding Ocean Waves: Honoring the Maratha Navy and coastal mastery.
Q8. What do you think was the Marathas' most important contribution to Indian history? Write a paragraph supporting your opinion with examples.

Answer:

The most important contribution of the Marathas to Indian history was the revival of indigenous political self-confidence and cultural renaissance after centuries of foreign imperial domination. By founding Hindavi Swarajya, Chhatrapati Shivaji proved that common farmers, artisans, and mountain folk could unite, build their own navy and fort network, defeat mighty empires, and establish an enlightened, merit-based administration that protected religious diversity. Furthermore, through leaders like Ahilyabai Holkar and Mahadji Shinde, the Marathas rejuvenated pilgrimage corridors and shared national heritage, passing on an unbroken legacy of freedom that directly inspired India's modern freedom fighters like Bal Gangadhar Tilak!

6. Exam Hall Traps & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

3 Crucial Exam Hall Traps
  1. Confusing Chauth and Sardeshmukhi: Chauth was 1/4th (25%) of revenue collected for protection; Sardeshmukhi was an additional 1/10th (10%) claimed by right of hereditary headship. Do not swap their percentages!
  2. Confusing Rani Tarabai and Rani Ahilyabai: Tarabai was Shivaji’s daughter-in-law who defeated Aurangzeb's forces (1700–1707). Ahilyabai Holkar was the later 18th-century queen of Indore famous for temple rebuilding and benevolent civil governance.
  3. Believing Shivaji only Fought Muslims: Shivaji fought for freedom against whoever threatened Swarajya (including Bijapur, Mughals, Portuguese, and even rebellious Maratha Deshmukhs). He employed respected Muslim generals and protected Islamic holy sites.
