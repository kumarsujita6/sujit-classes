The history of medieval and early modern India is fundamentally transformed by the rise of the Marathas. Rather than being merely a military uprising, the Maratha movement was a profound socio-cultural and political revolution rooted in the fertile soil of the Bhakti movement, catalyzed by the visionary genius of Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, and sustained by a unique confederacy of warrior-statesmen and brilliant women leaders.

From Ahilyabai Holkar's national temple restoration circuit and handloom patronage to the Bhonsles of Nagpur reviving the uninterrupted worship of Lord Jagannath in Puri (Odisha), the Marathas proved that Indians could organize, govern, and defend a sovereign civilization.

2.1 Who Were the Marathas? Deccan Roots & the Bhakti Cultural Renaissance

The Marathas are a vibrant ethno-linguistic community native to the rugged Deccan plateau, predominantly inhabiting the modern Indian state of Maharashtra. Their identity is inseparable from the Marathi language, which possesses an illustrious, unbroken literary heritage dating back to at least the 12th century.

During the 13th century, central and western Maharashtra was ruled by the wealthy and culturally refined Yadava dynasty from their impregnable fortress capital of Devagiri (later renamed Daulatabad). In the early 14th century, Alauddin Khilji and subsequent Delhi Sultanate armies overthrew the Yadavas. While imperial dynasties rose and fell in quick succession, the real bedrock of Maratha resilience was forged not on battlefields, but in the devotional hearts of ordinary people through the Bhakti movement.

Textbook Concept: What was the Bhakti Movement in Maharashtra?

Between the 7th and 17th centuries, spiritual seekers rejected empty ritualism and severe caste hierarchies. Instead, they championed Bhakti (pure, loving personal devotion to the Divine). In Maharashtra, saints composed deeply emotional devotional songs called Abhangas in the everyday spoken language of the masses, allowing their message of equality, moral purity, and divine love to spread to every rural household.

Sant Dnyaneshwar (13th Century)

At just 15 years of age, he broke the monopolistic barrier of classical Sanskrit by composing the Dnyaneshwari (or Bhavartha Deepika)—a masterly commentary on the Bhagavad Gita written in lucid Marathi. He also rendered profound Upanishadic philosophy accessible to common farmers and artisans.

Sant Namdev (1270–1350)

A close companion of Dnyaneshwar from the tailor community, Namdev travelled across northern India preaching divine unity and moral righteousness. His devotional hymns were so universally revered that dozens of his sacred verses are enshrined in the Guru Granth Sahib of the Sikhs.

Sant Tukaram (1608–1650)

A contemporary of Shivaji, Tukaram composed thousands of immortal Abhangas praising Lord Vitthala (Vithoba) of Pandharpur. He fearlessly criticized social hypocrisy and caste oppression, emphasizing that true religion lies in compassion toward suffering humanity.

Samarth Ramdas (1608–1681)

Ramdas infused spiritual devotion with physical fitness, civic duty, and political organization (analogous to the Sikh Gurus in Punjab). His magnum opus, Dasbodh, instructed youths to combine practical worldly wisdom, unity, and valor in the defense of righteousness.

Teacher's Memory Trick: The Four Pillars of Maharashtra Dharma

Remember the mnemonic "D-N-T-R": Dnyaneshwar (Intellectual foundation & Gita translation), Namdev (All-India devotional bridge), Tukaram (Heart of the masses & compassion), and Ramdas (Active organization & political consciousness). Together, they created a unified society ready to rally under Shivaji's flag.

2.2 Foundation of Maratha Power: Birth, Mentorship & the Dream of Swarajya

In the early 17th century, several prominent Maratha chieftains served as military nobles (sardars) under the rival Deccan Sultanates (Ahmadnagar's Nizam Shahi, Bijapur's Adil Shahi, and Golconda's Qutb Shahi). However, these chieftains fought endlessly against one another for foreign masters, causing devastating famines and distress among the peasantry.

In 1630, a revolutionary leader was born who would end this era of servitude: Shivaji Bhonsle, born at the hill fort of Shivneri near Junnar to Shahji Bhonsle and Jijabai.

Textbook Concept: What was a Jāgīr?

A jāgīr was an assignment of agricultural land or revenue granted by a sultan or monarch to an aristocrat or general in return for military or administrative service. The jagirdar collected taxes from the cultivators to maintain a specified contingent of troops. Shahji held a jagir centered around Pune, Supe, and Chakan.

Because Shahji was heavily engaged in military campaigns across Karnataka and southern India on behalf of Bijapur, the young Shivaji grew up at Pune under the extraordinary moral and intellectual guidance of his mother, Rajmata Jijabai, alongside his loyal guardian and administrator Dadoji Konddeo.

  • Jijabai's Mentorship: A woman of towering intellect, indomitable courage, and deep spiritual piety, Jijabai instilled in young Shivaji the heroic epics of the Ramayana and Mahabharata, teaching him that power exists solely to protect the weak, uphold justice (Dharma), and liberate one's homeland from foreign tyranny.
  • The Maval Companions: Instead of seeking royal court luxuries, Shivaji spent his youth exploring the steep ravines, misty crests, and thick forests of the Maval region in the Western Ghats (Sahyadris). He befriended the hardy, loyal hill-folk (the Mavalas—such as Tanaji Malusare, Yesaji Kank, and Baji Prabhu Deshpande) who became his lifelong commandos.
  • The Oath of Swarajya: When he was merely 16 years of age (1646), Shivaji took a solemn vow to establish Hindavi Swarajya (indigenous, sovereign self-rule). He boldly captured the neglected hill fort of Torna without bloodshed, followed by Chakan, Kondhana (Sinhagad), and Purandar, systematically securing the Pune district.

2.3 Military Masterstrokes: Guerrilla Warfare (Ganimi Kava) & the Slaying of Afzal Khan

Facing empires that commanded hundreds of thousands of heavy cavalry, musketeers, and siege cannons, Shivaji knew that meeting them in conventional pitched field battles would result in annihilation. Instead, he perfected an ancient indigenous method of combat into an art form known as Ganimi Kava (Guerrilla Warfare).

Textbook Concept: What is Guerrilla Warfare (Ganimi Kava)?

Guerrilla warfare is a tactical combat doctrine where small, highly mobile, lightly equipped units exploit their intimate knowledge of rugged terrain, thick forests, and weather conditions. Rather than defending static lines, they use speed, deception, and surprise to ambush enemy supply columns, cut off rear communications, strike vulnerable flanks, and vanish back into mountain shelters before the enemy's heavy forces can deploy.

Alarmed by Shivaji's rapid capture of hill fortresses and expanding influence, the Adil Shahi Sultanate of Bijapur dispatched its most terrifying veteran general, Afzal Khan, in 1659 with an army of over 10,000 elite cavalry, heavy artillery, and war elephants, boasting that he would bring the "mountain rat" back in chains.

Strategic Trap at Pratapgad

Afzal Khan attempted to provoke Shivaji into descending into the open plains by desecrating sacred temples at Tuljapur and Pandharpur. Shivaji maintained iron discipline, refusing to leave the dense forests and steep precipices surrounding his new fortress, Pratapgad.

The One-on-One Meeting

Shivaji lured Afzal Khan into agreeing to an unarmed personal summit in a shamiana (tent) at the base of Pratapgad. Suspecting treachery, Shivaji wore chain-mail armor under his white robe, hid a curved dagger (bichuwa) in his sleeve, and concealed a wāgh nakh in his fingers.

The Claws of Justice (Wāgh Nakh)

As they embraced, the towering Afzal Khan gripped Shivaji in a neck-lock and stabbed him in the back with his hidden dagger. The armor saved Shivaji. In a flash, Shivaji disemboweled Khan with his wagh nakh and finished him with the bichuwa.

Total Mountain Rout

At a pre-arranged trumpet signal, hidden Mavala forces under Kanhoji Jedhe and Netaji Palkar ambushed the disoriented Bijapuri army trapped in the forested ravines, capturing thousands of horses, 65 artillery pieces, and the enemy treasury.

Don't Miss Out (NCERT Box): The Wāgh Nakh (Tiger's Claw)

The wāgh nakh is a lethal, concealable hand weapon consisting of four or five curved steel blades riveted to a metal bar with finger rings. Designed to mimic a feline predator's retractile claws, it allowed Shivaji to defend his life when attacked under the guise of diplomatic embrace.

2.4 Defying the Mughal Colossus: The Shaista Khan Midnight Strike & Surat

The slaying of Afzal Khan established Shivaji as a formidable regional power. Recognizing the danger, Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb dispatched his maternal uncle, Shaista Khan, as the Viceroy of the Deccan with a mammoth force of nearly 100,000 troops.

  • Three Years of Occupation: Shaista Khan captured Pune, devastating farmlands and taking up residence in Shivaji's childhood home, the Lal Mahal. Mughal cavalry barred all overt resistance.
  • The Surgical Strike (April 1663): Demonstrating sheer military audacity, Shivaji and 400 handpicked commandos infiltrated Pune during the holy month of Ramadan, disguised as a wedding procession. In the dead of night, they cut through a kitchen wall into the Lal Mahal.
  • The Panic and Flight: In the melee, Shivaji sliced off three of Shaista Khan's fingers as he leaped through a window into the darkness. Khan's son and multiple guards were killed, while the Marathas lit torches tied to the horns of cattle driven towards another mountain to decoy the Mughal cavalry, escaping untouched to Katraj. Utterly humiliated, Aurangzeb transferred the terrified Shaista Khan to Bengal.
Textbook Concept: What is a "Surgical Strike"?

A surgical strike is a lightning, targeted military operation executed with pinpoint precision to destroy an exact high-value target while inflicting minimal collateral damage on surrounding civilians or innocent infrastructure. Shivaji's raid on Lal Mahal is hailed as the medieval textbook archetype of a modern surgical raid.

To avenge the severe three-year destruction of Maharashtra's agricultural economy and refill the Swarajya treasury, Shivaji launched a bold retaliatory strike against Surat (in modern Gujarat) in January 1664.

  • Surat's Economic Status: Surat was the richest maritime trading emporium of the Mughal Empire and the primary embarkation port for the Hajj pilgrimage, generating immense customs revenue.
  • Wealth Acquired: Shivaji recovered over one crore (10 million) rupees in gold, silver, pearls, and diamonds, financing the construction of coastal sea fortresses and modern armaments.
  • Strict Humanitarian Ethics: Despite sacking the imperial storehouses, Shivaji enforced strict orders: no religious buildings (mosques or churches) were harmed, women and children were completely protected, and the residence of Mohandas Parekh, a widely respected merchant renowned for feeding the poor, was granted armed protection by Maratha guards.
  • International Headlines: News of the raid was so sensational that it was published in the London Gazette, England's official government journal, noting that Shivaji demanded tribute from European colonial trading factories or threatened to ruin their trade.

2.5 The Treaty of Purandar (1665) & the Daring Agra Escape (1666)

The sacking of Surat was a devastating blow to imperial Mughal prestige. Aurangzeb responded by dispatching his most capable diplomat-general, Mirza Raja Jai Singh of Amber, along with general Diler Khan and vast resources. Jai Singh applied a dual strategy: encircling Swarajya forts with artillery while bribing local feudatories and the Bijapur Sultanate to attack Shivaji simultaneously.

Textbook Concept: The Treaty of Purandar (June 1665)

Recognizing that prolonged siege warfare against overwhelming odds would exhaust his young nation's manpower, Shivaji demonstrated supreme pragmatism by concluding the Treaty of Purandar:
• Shivaji surrendered 23 out of his 35 forts (yielding an annual revenue of 400,000 hons) to the Mughals, retaining only 12 for himself.
• His nine-year-old son, Sambhaji, was enrolled as a high-ranking Mughal mansabdar of 5,000 horsemen.
• Shivaji agreed to assist the Mughals against Bijapur in the Deccan.

Jai Singh subsequently convinced Shivaji to travel north to the imperial capital of Agra to meet Emperor Aurangzeb in person, guaranteeing his personal safety.

  • The Insult at Agra Court (May 1666): When Shivaji attended Aurangzeb's 50th birthday darbar at Agra, he was made to stand in the rank of panch-hazari (nobles commanding 5,000 troops)—the exact rank assigned to Jaswant Singh, whom Shivaji had defeated in the Deccan. Outraged by this deliberate breach of protocol and royal dignity, Shivaji loudly protested in open court, turned his back on the throne, and stormed out.
  • House Arrest: Aurangzeb placed Shivaji and young Sambhaji under strict armed house arrest surrounded by the imperial guards of Polad Khan, planning to assassinate them or exile them to the desolate frontiers of Kabul.
  • The Stratagem of the Sweet Baskets: Feigning a severe illness, Shivaji announced that he wished to atone by sending massive baskets of fruits, flowers, and sweets as daily charitable gifts to Muslim fakirs, Hindu Brahmins, and imperial nobles. Initially, suspicious guards inspected every basket, but over weeks of monotonous checking, their vigilance relaxed.
  • The Great Escape (August 1666): One evening, Shivaji and Sambhaji climbed into two large sweet hampers and were carried right past the complacent guards. Disguised as wandering sadhus, they undertook an extraordinary circuitous journey through Mathura, Allahabad, Varanasi, Gaya, and Golconda, miraculously arriving back in Raigad months later. Aurangzeb's dream of capturing Shivaji was permanently shattered.

2.6 The Grand Coronation of 1674: Shri Raja Shiva Chhatrapati & Swarajya

By 1670, Shivaji had launched a whirlwind counter-offensive, recapturing nearly all the 23 forts ceded under the Treaty of Purandar (immortalized by Tanaji Malusare's heroic sacrifice at Sinhagad) and sacking Surat a second time to restock his finances.

However, both the Mughal Emperor and the Deccan Sultans still officially dismissed Shivaji as a rebellious "zamindar" or an outlaw chieftain. To establish undisputed sovereign legitimacy, Shivaji resolved to hold a formal royal coronation in accordance with sacred Vedic traditions.

Textbook Concept: The Coronation at Raigad (6 June 1674)

Presided over by Gaga Bhatt, the renowned high scholar of Varanasi, Shivaji was consecrated at the unscalable mountain fortress of Raigad:
• He assumed the formal royal title of 'Shri Raja Shiva Chhatrapati' (the Lord of the Royal Umbrella, meaning sovereign protector of the people).
• He instituted his own sovereign calendar era: the Rājyābhiṣheka Shaka.
• He minted independent gold coins (Hon) and copper coins (Shivrai) bearing Devanagari script inscriptions, asserting sovereign cultural independence.

2.7 Dakshina Digvijaya (Southern Campaign) & the Historic Ban on Slave Trade

In 1677–1678, Chhatrapati Shivaji embarked upon his monumental Dakshina Digvijaya (Conquest of the South). Crossing through the Deccan sultanates via friendly alliances with Golconda, Maratha armies swept into present-day Karnataka and northern Tamil Nadu.

  • Capture of Southern Bastions: Shivaji captured the colossal rock fortress of Gingee (Jinji) and the massive water-moated fort of Vellore, establishing a powerful southern corridor of Maratha rule.
  • Strategic Depth: This southern expansion was a masterstroke of geopolitical foresight. As we will see, when Aurangzeb later invaded Maharashtra and captured the northern capital of Raigad, the Marathas retreated to Gingee, using it as an invincible southern base to wage a 27-year war of resistance.
Don't Miss Out (NCERT Box): Banning the European Colonial Slave Trade

While campaigning in the South along the Coromandel coast, Shivaji discovered that European colonial powers—especially the Dutch East India Company—were routinely abducting and purchasing impoverished local Indian civilians to transport and sell them abroad as slave labor.

While other contemporary Indian rulers ignored this human atrocity, Chhatrapati Shivaji issued strict royal decrees prohibiting the Dutch from purchasing or trading any human beings as slaves within his territories. This humanitarian intervention reflected his core philosophy that a sovereign king exists to protect the basic human dignity of his subjects.

2.8 The Epic 27-Year War of Defiance (1681–1707): Sambhaji, Rajaram & Tarabai

In April 1680, at the age of fifty, Chhatrapati Shivaji succumbed to a severe illness at Raigad. Hearing of his demise, Aurangzeb believed that the young Maratha state would disintegrate overnight.

In 1681, Aurangzeb personally marched southward at the head of the entire imperial grand army—over 500,000 soldiers, massive artillery trains, and inexhaustible imperial wealth—vowing never to return to Delhi until the Marathas were wiped from history. This triggered the titanic 27-Year War of Defiance (1681–1707).

1. Chhatrapati Sambhaji (1680–1689)

Shivaji's eldest son, Sambhaji, proved to be an invincible military commander who fought over 120 battles without suffering a single defeat. For nearly a decade, he held the entire Mughal military machine at bay while simultaneously repelling Portuguese invasions in Goa. In 1689, captured by deceit at Sangameshwar, Sambhaji endured horrific physical torture rather than surrender his religion or kingdom, achieving immortal martyrdom (Dharmaveer).

2. Chhatrapati Rajaram & Gingee (1689–1700)

When Mughals captured Raigad and took Sambhaji's infant son Shahu captive, Shivaji's younger son, Rajaram, shifted the capital 800 kilometers south to Gingee (Jinji) in Tamil Nadu. For eight grueling years, Mughal general Zulfiqar Khan besieged Gingee, while Maratha cavalry generals Santaji Ghorpade and Dhanaji Jadhav severed Mughal supply lines across the Deccan in brilliant guerrilla strikes.

3. Maharani Tarabai (1700–1707)

Following Rajaram's death in 1700, his 25-year-old widow, Maharani Tarabai, took command as regent for her infant son Shivaji II. Displaying extraordinary strategic genius, she unified quarreling sardars and fundamentally transformed the war from a defensive survival struggle into a massive offensive invasion of Mughal northern provinces (Malwa, Gujarat, and Berar).

4. Imperial Collapse of Aurangzeb (1707)

Tarabai's relentless counter-offensives exhausted Mughal financial reserves, broke the morale of imperial troops, and destroyed imperial authority. In 1707, an aged, broken, and defeated Aurangzeb died at Ahmadnagar, without having conquered the Marathas. Swarajya had not only survived—it was poised to dominate India.

2.9 Structural Transformation: The Rise of the Peshwas & Pan-Indian Expansion

Following Aurangzeb's death in 1707, the Mughal court released Sambhaji's son, Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj, from captivity, hoping to trigger a civil war between Shahu and Maharani Tarabai. Although initial friction occurred, Shahu emerged as Chhatrapati with his capital at Satara, while Tarabai established an autonomous royal branch at Kolhapur.

During this era, the Maratha state underwent a fundamental administrative transition from Shivaji's highly centralized monarchy to a decentralized confederacy of semi-autonomous military houses. The office of the Peshwa (originally the Prime Minister in the Ashta Pradhana council) rose to paramount executive leadership.

Textbook Concept: What was the Peshwa?

The term Peshwa comes from the Persian word for "leader" or "foremost minister" (Prime Minister). Under Chhatrapati Shahu, the brilliant Chitpavan Brahmin administrator Balaji Vishwanath was appointed Peshwa in 1713. In 1719, he negotiated the historic Mughal-Maratha treaties in Delhi, securing imperial recognition for Swarajya and the legal right to collect Chauth and Sardeshmukhi across the six Mughal provinces (subahs) of the Deccan. The post subsequently became hereditary.

Peshwa Bajirao I (1720–1740)

Succeeding his father at age twenty, Bajirao I is celebrated as one of the greatest cavalry generals in world history, fighting in over 40 pitched battles without suffering a single defeat. He famously proclaimed to Shahu: "Let us strike at the trunk of the withering tree [the Delhi Mughals], and the branches will fall off themselves. Thus should the Maratha flag fly from the Krishna to the Indus!"

The Five Great Maratha Houses

To expand across India, Bajirao empowered gifted military commanders, creating the Maratha Confederacy:
• Peshwas at Pune (Western India & Imperial Core)
• Shindes (Scindias) at Gwalior (Malwa & North India)
• Holkars at Indore (Central India)
• Gaekwads at Baroda (Gujarat & Saurashtra)
• Bhonsles at Nagpur (Vidarbha, Chhattisgarh & Odisha)

Nanasaheb Peshwa (1740–1761)

Bajirao's son, Balaji Bajirao (Nanasaheb), transformed Pune into a magnificent imperial capital. Under his leadership, Maratha armies advanced to the eastern coasts of Bengal and Odisha and surged across the Punjab plain to plant the saffron flag at the Indus River.

The Flaw of Decentralization

While decentralization enabled rapid continental conquest, regional chiefs often acted as independent despots. In distant provinces, occasional indiscipline, heavy taxation, and raiding damaged the noble moral reputation that Shivaji had established.

2.10 The March to Attock (1758), Panipat (1761) & Mahadji Shinde's Resurgence

In 1754, the Marathas gained decisive political control over Delhi, acting as the protectors and kingmakers of the Mughal Emperor. In 1758, a joint force under Raghunathrao and Malhar Rao Holkar marched through Punjab, liberated Lahore, and unfurled the Maratha flag at Attock and Peshawar on the banks of the Indus (in present-day Pakistan), expelling Timur Shah, son of Afghan king Ahmad Shah Abdali.

The Tragedy of the Third Battle of Panipat (14 January 1761)

Ahmad Shah Abdali returned with a massive coalition of Afghan tribes and Rohilla Afghans led by Najib-ud-Daulah. The Maratha army, commanded by Sadashivrao Bhau and young heir Vishwasrao, accompanied by non-combatant pilgrims, was encircled at Panipat.

Cut off from food supplies for months, the starving Marathas launched a desperate frontal assault. Despite valiant artillery fighting by Ibrahim Khan Gardi, the Marathas suffered a devastating defeat, losing nearly an entire generation of commanders and 75,000 soldiers. A cryptic banker's letter to Pune recorded: "Two pearls have been dissolved [Bhau and Vishwasrao], twenty-seven gold mohurs have been lost, and of the silver and copper the total cannot be counted."

European historians predicted that Panipat would permanently extinguish Maratha power. Instead, the Marathas achieved one of the most astonishing comebacks in world military history under the brilliant young Peshwa Madhavrao I (1761–1772).

  • Ten-Year Recovery: Within a single decade, Madhavrao restored domestic stability, suppressed internal rebellions, and dispatched powerful armies northward under Mahadji Shinde (Scindia).
  • Recapture of Delhi (1771): In 1771, Mahadji Shinde entered Delhi in triumph, broke the Rohilla Afghan power, and reinstated the blinded Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II on the Red Fort throne as a client monarch under Maratha protection. Delhi remained under undisputed Maratha suzerainty until the British captured it in 1803!

2.11 Nana Phadnavis, the First Pan-Indian Anti-British Alliance & the Three Wars

In the late 18th century, the British East India Company realized that their ultimate obstacle to conquering the Indian subcontinent was not the declining Mughals, but the formidable Maratha Empire.

Don't Miss Out (NCERT Box): The First Pan-Indian Anti-British Alliance

Nana Phadnavis (1742–1800), the legendary administrative minister at Pune (dubbed the "Maratha Machiavelli" by Europeans), achieved an unprecedented diplomatic breakthrough during the First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782).

Setting aside decades of intense rivalry, Nana forged the first united pan-Indian grand coalition against the British, bringing together the Marathas, the Nizam of Hyderabad, and Hyder Ali of Mysore. Maratha armies under Mahadji Shinde encircled British forces at Wadgaon, forcing them to surrender and sign the Treaty of Salbai (1782), guaranteeing twenty years of peace.

Tragically, after the deaths of Mahadji Shinde (1794) and Nana Phadnavis (1800), fierce internal factional feuds crippled the Maratha leadership:

  • Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805): The incompetent Peshwa Bajirao II signed the humiliating Subsidiary Alliance (Treaty of Bassein). The Shindes and Bhonsles fought heroically at Assaye and Laswari but were defeated by Arthur Wellesley due to superior British artillery coordination.
  • Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818): In a desperate final attempt, the Peshwa, Appa Sahib of Nagpur, and Malhar Rao Holkar II took up arms. Outnumbered and divided, the Marathas were defeated. The British abolished the office of the Peshwa, exiled Bajirao II to Bithoor near Kanpur, and annexed Maharashtra into the Bombay Presidency.
Exam High-Yield Fact: "The British took India from the Marathas, not the Mughals"

By the late 18th century, the Mughal Emperor was merely a powerless pensioner inside the Red Fort. The Marathas directly controlled or collected taxes from Delhi, Agra, the Ganga-Yamuna Doab, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Central India, Maharashtra, and parts of Karnataka and Odisha. The British had to fight three massive wars against the Maratha confederacy to achieve paramountcy over India.

2.12 Ashta Pradhana Mandala: Shivaji's Eight-Pillar Cabinet & Civilian Rule

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj was not merely an unmatched warrior; he was an administrative genius centuries ahead of his time. Drawing upon classical ancient Indian political texts like Kautilya's Arthashastra, he established a specialized Ashta Pradhana Mandala (Council of Eight Ministers).

Figure 2.2: The Architectural Structure of Chhatrapati Shivaji's Ashta Pradhana Mandala.
Revolutionary Civilian Reforms under Chhatrapati Shivaji:

1. Abolition of Hereditary Jagirs: Shivaji recognized that hereditary fiefdoms bred local despots who oppressed peasants. He abolished land grants for state officials, compensating them strictly with regular cash salaries (rokh) drawn directly from the central treasury.

2. Periodic Administrative Transfers: High officials were routinely transferred between districts and fort commands to prevent them from building personal power bases.

3. Welfare Pensions for Fallen Soldiers: Demonstrating immense empathy, Shivaji instituted state-funded monthly pensions for the widows and orphaned children of soldiers who fell on the battlefield, while offering honorable state employment to their sons when they reached adulthood.

2.14 The Military Machine: Bargirs, Shiledars, Iron Rockets & European Battalions

The armed forces of the Maratha Empire were organized into three specialized wings: the Infantry (Payak), the Cavalry (Paga), and the Royal Navy (Armār).

Bārgīrs (State-Equipped Cavalry)

The elite core of the Maratha cavalry. Their warhorses, weapons, saddlery, armor, and fodder were fully paid for and provided by the state treasury. They maintained iron discipline and obeyed central military officers directly.

Shiledārs (Freelance Cavalry)

Independent aristocratic warrior-troopers who owned their own horses, armor, and weapons. They were contracted by the state for military campaigns and received a campaign allowance, but maintained greater autonomy.

Pioneering Metal Rockets

Contrary to popular myths that rockets in India originated only under Tipu Sultan in Mysore, NCERT records that rockets were actively used in Maratha military campaigns from the days of Chhatrapati Shivaji himself! By 1770, Maratha armories were mass-producing specialized metal tube rockets capable of terrifying enemy cavalry.

European-Style Modern Battalions

In the late 18th century, Mahadji Shinde recruited French military officers (such as Benoit de Boigne) to raise, drill, and discipline elite battalions armed with flintlock muskets and standardized bronze mobile field artillery, making the Gwalior army the most feared military force in Asia.

2.15 "Forts are the Core of the State": Ramachandrapant Amatya's Ādnyāpatra

For the Marathas, forts were not merely royal residences; they were the absolute life-breath and constitutional foundation of sovereign existence. Shivaji directly constructed, captured, or renovated over 300 mountain, land, and coastal fortresses across Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu.

Primary Source Highlight: The Ādnyāpatra (1715) by Ramachandrapant Amatya

"Forts are the core of the state. In their absence, the land gets devastated in the face of an invasion. Therefore, all former kings secured the country by building forts. This kingdom [Hindavi Swarajya] was created by the late great master [Chhatrapati Shivaji] from forts alone... A great enemy like Aurangzeb invaded, conquered great empires like Bijapur and Golconda... but it was due to forts that the Maratha state survived despite decades of onslaught."

2.16 Maritime Supremacy: The Maratha Royal Navy & Admiral Kanhoji Angre

In 1657, Chhatrapati Shivaji took a step that was utterly revolutionary among Indian monarchs: he founded a standing, full-time royal maritime battle fleet—the Maratha Navy (Armār).

While contemporary empires like the Mughals possessed merchant vessels but no battle-ready navy, and Bijapur maintained only transport ships, European powers (Portuguese, British, and Dutch) operated as aggressive maritime bullies. They monopolized the Arabian Sea by forcing all Indian ships to purchase naval travel permits called Cartaz (in Portuguese); any Indian ship caught sailing without a European Cartaz was seized and confiscated.

Founding the Navy (1657)

Recognizing that "he who commands the sea commands the land," Shivaji commissioned the construction of deep-keeled war vessels (Gurabs) and nimble gunboats (Galbats) at Kalyan and Bhivandi, establishing major naval shipyards.

Coastal Fortress Shield

Shivaji built invincible sea fortresses on rocky reefs jutting into the Arabian Sea—such as Sindhudurg (founded with over 72,000 kg of molten lead anchoring its foundational granite blocks), Vijaydurg, and Suvarnadurg—functioning as naval dry-docks and bases.

Admiral Kanhoji Angre (Sarkhel)

Appointed Grand Admiral (Sarkhel) in 1698, Kanhoji Angre became the supreme master of the Konkan coast. Undefeated throughout his lifetime, he crushed Portuguese fleets and shattered multiple joint British-Portuguese naval expeditions sent to annihilate him.

Challenging the European Cartaz

Angre boldly inverted colonial arrogance: the Marathas demanded that European ships pay fees and purchase Maratha naval passes to trade along the Konkan coast! Outraged that an Indian power dared enforce maritime sovereignty, European powers maliciously labeled Angre a "pirate."

2.17 Judicial Moderation, Overseas Trade & Riverine Infrastructure

The judicial administration of the Maratha Empire stood out in the medieval world for its humanitarian moderation and rare use of capital punishment (the death penalty). Mutilation and execution, which were commonplace in contemporary European and Asian monarchies, were avoided wherever possible, with restitution, fines, and community reconciliation preferred.

The Village Panchāyat

At the grassroots level, civil and minor criminal disputes were heard and resolved by the Panchāyat—a traditional community council composed of village headmen (Patil), record-keepers (Kulkarni), and respected village elders. If a citizen felt aggrieved by the decision, they held the legal right to appeal directly to the district Maratha chief or the Royal Court of the Chhatrapati.

The Kotwāl in Urban Hubs

In major metropolitan and commercial centers such as Pune, Satara, Indore, and Gwalior, the Kotwāl served as the municipal police chief and magistrate. The Kotwal's department enforced evening curfews, regulated market weights and measures, checked price-gouging, registered property deeds, and maintained municipal cleanliness.

Active Overseas Commercial Fleets

Shivaji Maharaj personally recognized that political independence required financial autonomy. He and his senior ministers owned merchant vessels that traded directly across the Indian Ocean basin—exporting Indian textiles, spices, and brassware, and importing Arabian horses and gold bullion from ports as far afield as Mocha (Yemen), Muscat (Oman), and Malacca (Malaysia).

River Ferries & Ghat Infrastructure

The Maratha state invested heavily in public works. They paved steep mountain passes (ghat-margs) for pack bullocks, constructed masonry bridges over turbulent streams, and, in provinces like 18th-century Odisha, organized and subsidized a comprehensive network of public river ferries across the Mahanadi, Brahmani, and Baitarani rivers to protect merchants and pilgrims.

2.18 Environmental Ethics in Governance: Shivaji's Tree Conservation Edict

In an era when rulers stripped forests indiscriminately for warfare, Chhatrapati Shivaji issued royal edicts embodying advanced principles of ecological conservation and peasant empathy.

Think About It (NCERT Primary Source): Shivaji's Edict on Preserving Fruit Trees

In a famous royal circular sent to his provincial governors and naval admirals, Shivaji issued the following strict command:

"Wood from big trees like teak is required for the Navy. If needed, secure permission for cutting the trees from the forest and then proceed. Other trees like the mango and jackfruit are also useful, but do not touch them. Because such trees take many years to mature, and the people look after them like their children. If you cut them, will their sorrow ever end? If you accomplish something by oppressing others, it perishes soon, along with the oppressor. There is harm in the absence of such trees as well. Therefore, do not use force in any circumstances."

2.19 Cultural Resurgence: The Sanskrit Seal, Rājya-Vyavahāra-Koṣha & Moḍī Script

Centuries of Delhi Sultanate and Mughal dominance had imposed Persian as the sole language of diplomacy, elite administration, and royal seals across India. Shivaji initiated a conscious cultural renaissance to restore indigenous linguistic dignity without persecuting any community.

The Sanskrit Rājmudrā (Royal Seal)

Breaking completely with the Persian convention, Shivaji had his royal seal cast in classical Sanskrit verse in Devanagari script:
"Pratipachchandra-lekheva vardhiṣṇur-viśvavanditā |
Shāhasūnoḥ Śhivasyaishā mudrā bhadrāya rājate"
Translation: "Ever-growing in glory like the crescent moon on the first day, revered by the entire world, this seal of Shivaji, son of Shahji, shines solely for the welfare of the people."

The Rājya-Vyavahāra-Koṣha

Shivaji commissioned the noted scholar Raghunath Pandit to compile the Rājya-Vyavahāra-Koṣha (1677)—a comprehensive administrative dictionary providing classical Sanskrit and Marathi equivalents for over 1,400 Persian and Arabic administrative loanwords. Within decades, foreign loanwords in Maratha official state correspondence dropped from over 80% to under 14%.

The Moḍī Cursive Script

To facilitate rapid administrative dictation, revenue accounting, and battlefield correspondence without lifting the reed pen from the paper, Maratha scribes perfected the Moḍī script (a fluid, cursive derivative of Devanagari). Millions of historical documents preserved in Pune and Thanjavur archives are written in Moḍī.

The Gaṇapatī-Pantapradhān Rupee

In the 18th century, while adopting the standard Mughal silver weight standard for commercial convenience, Marathas introduced distinctive religious-cultural invocations. The rare Gaṇapatī-Pantapradhān rupee, minted by the Patwardhans, featured bilingual inscriptions: Persian on one side for cross-border commerce, and Devanagari invoking Lord Ganesha and loyalty to the Peshwa on the obverse.

2.20 The Mighty Maratha Women: Maharani Tarabai & Rajmata Ahilyabai Holkar

One of the most glorious and distinctive dimensions of Maratha history was the emergence of extraordinary women rulers and military commanders who shattered medieval patriarchal constraints.

Maharani Tarabai: Savior of Swarajya

The daughter of legendary Maratha commander Hambirao Mohite and queen of Chhatrapati Rajaram. Following her husband's death in 1700, she personally took the field, mounted on warhorses and directing artillery sieges. Recognizing that Aurangzeb had concentrated all imperial forces in Maharashtra, she devised the strategy of sending massive Maratha columns deep into unprotected northern Mughal provinces, ensuring the survival and ultimate victory of Swarajya.

Rajmata Ahilyabai Holkar: The Philosopher Queen

Ruling the extensive kingdom of Malwa from Indore for thirty glorious years (1767–1795) following the death of her husband Khanderao and son Male Rao, Ahilyabai is universally celebrated as a model of enlightened, compassionate governance. She held open daily public audiences to hear the grievances of ordinary citizens, personally led troops against highway dacoits, and protected tribal Bhil and Gond communities.

Ahilyabai Holkar's Pan-Indian Cultural Circuit & Maheshwari Textiles:

1. Rebuilding Sacred Shrines Across India: Ahilyabai utilized her private treasury to rebuild and renovate hundreds of desecrated temples, stone bathing ghats, free food kitchens (annachhatras), and wells from the snowy heights of Kedarnath and Badrinath in the Himalayas to Rameshwaram in the southern ocean.

2. Restoring Kashi Vishwanath & Somnath: Most famously, she reconstructed the grand Kashi Vishwanath Temple in Varanasi (which had been demolished by Aurangzeb in 1669) and the sacred Somnath Temple in Gujarat (repeatedly devastated since the raids of Mahmud of Ghazni).

3. Founding the Maheshwar Handloom Industry: Ahilyabai personally invited master artisan weavers from Surat, Varanasi, and Mandu to her capital at Maheshwar on the banks of the Narmada River, designing and financing the world-famous Maheshwari handloom sari industry, an enduring economic heritage that continues to sustain thousands of traditional weavers today.

2.21 The Marathas in Eastern India: The Bhonsles of Nagpur & Lord Jagannath at Puri

Special Odisha Focus: The Bhonsles of Nagpur and the Revival of Puri Jagannath Temple

In the mid-18th century (1751–1803), the Bhonsles of Nagpur brought the historic region of Odisha under their governance. During the previous century of Mughal governorship, the sacred rituals and annual Rath Yatra of Lord Jagannath at Puri had suffered catastrophic, repeated disruptions; Mughal subahdars had frequently forced temple authorities to hide the deities in remote islands of Chilika Lake to prevent their desecration.

Under Maratha rule, the worship of Lord Jagannath was restored to full, uninterrupted splendor:
• The Maratha Subahdars (such as Sheo Bhatt Sathe and Mudhoji Bhonsle) granted extensive tax-free agricultural lands and revenue villages (Amruta Manohi endowments) to feed thousands of daily pilgrims.
• They renovated sacred structures throughout Puri, rebuilt the stone bridge of Atharanala, constructed broad pilgrim rest houses (dharamshalas), and maintained a secure public ferry system across all major Odishan rivers.
• The Maratha rulers proudly served as humble devotees of the Lord, fostering deep spiritual integration between western and eastern India.

2.22 In Focus: The Thanjavur Marathas & Raja Serfoji II (Tamil Nadu)

In the late 17th century (1676), Shivaji's half-brother, Ekoji I (Venkoji), captured the rich Kaveri delta kingdom of Thanjavur in Tamil Nadu, establishing an illustrious royal dynasty that ruled for nearly two centuries.

The Thanjavur Marathas fostered a dazzling syncretic culture where Marathi, Tamil, and Telugu arts, classical literature, and music blended into an extraordinary renaissance. The rulers were themselves gifted poets, dramatists, and patron scholars.

Thanjavur Painting Style

Under royal Maratha patronage, the world-renowned Thanjavur painting style flourished. Characterized by vibrant mineral pigments, dynamic compositions of Hindu deities, and magnificent 22-karat gold foil relief work adorned with uncut gemstones, it remains a hallmark of Indian fine arts.

Carnatic Music & Bharatanatyam

Thanjavur became the vibrant epicenter of South Indian classical performing arts. The famous "Trinity of Carnatic Music" (Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar, and Syama Sastri) flourished in this environment, while the Tanjore Quartet codified and refined the repertoire of classical Bharatanatyam dance under royal Maratha encouragement.

Textbook Deep-Dive: The Polymath King Raja Serfoji II (1798–1832)

Of all Thanjavur rulers, Raja Serfoji II was the most remarkable enlightened visionary, fluent in Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Sanskrit, English, French, and Latin:

• Geographical Drama (Devendra Kuravanji): Serfoji authored a celebrated Marathi dance-drama titled Devendra Kuravanji, which ingeniously wove the latest scientific knowledge of global world geography and astronomy into traditional classical verse!
• Dhanwantari Mahal: An advanced medical research center and hospital that provided completely free healthcare to the public, integrating traditional Indian Ayurveda, Siddha, and Unani therapies with Western surgical methods.
• First Native Printing Press & Library: Serfoji established the first printing press set up by an Indian ruler, producing books in Devanagari, English, and Tamil, and vastly expanded the world-famous Saraswathi Mahal Library, preserving thousands of priceless ancient palm-leaf manuscripts.
• Brihadishwara Temple Stone Inscription: Serfoji commissioned the recording of the entire history of the Bhonsle royal family on the southern stone enclosure wall of the colossal Brihadishwara Temple in Thanjavur, comprising one of the largest continuous stone inscriptions in the world.

2.23 The Enduring Maratha Legacy: Seeds of India's Freedom Movement

The historical achievement of the Maratha Empire cannot be measured solely in captured territories or military victories. It permanently transformed the psychological consciousness of the Indian people:

  • Broke the Myth of Imperial Invincibility: For over three centuries, it was widely assumed that only dynastic invaders from beyond the Hindu Kush could establish a continental empire in India. Shivaji and his successors decisively proved that indigenous Indian forces could defeat mighty imperial armies and govern a subcontinent-wide empire with justice and efficiency.
  • The Ideal of Hindavi Swarajya: Shivaji's fiery conception of Swarajya (self-rule rooted in justice, equality, and cultural self-confidence) became the spiritual foundation for India's modern freedom struggle. Over two centuries later, nationalist pioneers like Lokmanya Bal Gangadhar Tilak revived public celebrations of Shivaji Maharaj to unite millions of Indians against British colonial rule, famously proclaiming: "Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it!"

In-Text Prompt 1 (Page 61): Ramachandrapant Amatya's Warning on the "Hat-Wearers"
Step 1: Core Concept Breakdown

The excerpt from the Ādnyāpatra (1715) provides an early Indian perspective on European colonial merchants ("hat-wearers"), warning that they were not ordinary commercial traders but territorial imperialists who utilized superior naval technology and gunpowder to conquer land and propagate their faith.

Step 2: Historical Context & Primary Source

Ramachandrapant Amatya, who served as Finance Minister under Shivaji and witnessed the arrival of Portuguese, English, French, and Dutch traders, wrote: "They are not like other merchants... They are obstinate people, and Navy and gunpowder are their chief weapons. Their traffic should be controlled, and they shouldn't be allowed to build strong buildings."

Step 3: Point-wise Explanation
  • Colonial Disguise: European trading companies entered India masquerading as peaceful commercial buyers of spices and cotton textiles.
  • Hidden Political Motives: Under the pretext of defending warehouses, they built fortified stone castles, imported heavy naval cannons, and interfered in local political disputes to seize sovereign territory.
  • Religious Expansion: Amatya explicitly observed that European merchants were fanatically dedicated to converting indigenous populations to their own religion.
  • Amatya's Policy Directive: Amatya advised the Maratha state to strictly forbid Europeans from acquiring permanent inland fortresses or dominating coastal maritime corridors.
Step 4: Real-World Analogy

Imagine a guest who asks to rent a spare room in your home for one week to sell books, but then quietly installs steel locks, brings in armed bodyguards, refuses to leave, and eventually claims ownership of the entire house. This is exactly what European trading companies did across Asia.

Step 5: Exam Topper Tip

Keyword Focus: Highlight that Ramachandrapant Amatya's Ādnyāpatra represents one of the earliest surviving Indian strategic documents warning against European colonial imperialism over half a century before the Battle of Plassey (1757).

In-Text Prompt 13 (Page 78): Don't Miss Out — The Moḍī Cursive Administrative Script
Step 1: Core Concept Breakdown

Understanding the linguistic origins, functional design, and historical role of the Moḍī script in Maratha state administrative correspondence.

Step 2: Historical Context

As the Maratha Empire expanded across India, royal secretaries, revenue clerks, and military messengers had to draft thousands of letters, revenue registers, and battlefield orders every day.

Step 3: Point-wise Explanation
  • Cursive Design: Moḍī (meaning "bent" or "broken") is a fluid cursive variation of the Devanagari script designed so that words could be written continuously without repeatedly lifting the reed pen (borū) from paper.
  • Elimination of Top-Line: Traditional Devanagari requires a continuous horizontal top-line (shiro-rekhā) over each word; Moḍī eliminated or streamlined this, vastly accelerating writing speed.
  • Official State Script: All Maratha administrative correspondence, treasury books, and royal farmans from the 17th to early 20th centuries were penned in Moḍī.
  • Archival Heritage: Massive state archives containing millions of Moḍī documents are preserved at the Peshwa Daftar in Pune and the Saraswathi Mahal Library in Thanjavur.
Step 4: Real-World Analogy

Moḍī served as the medieval Indian equivalent of modern stenography or cursive shorthand, allowing scribes to take down rapid oral dictation during bustling court proceedings.

Step 5: Exam Topper Tip

Keyword Focus: Remember that Moḍī is a script (a writing system), while Marathi is the language. Shivaji's seal and coins used standard Devanagari, but everyday administrative records used Moḍī.

The British had to fight three major Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818) to win paramountcy over India.
Trap 3: Believing the Marathas Had No Navy and Were Exclusively Land Warriors
Why Students Lose Marks: Students associate Shivaji only with mountain hill forts and completely forget his maritime achievements.
Topper's Rule: Shivaji founded the Maratha Royal Navy in 1657. He built world-famous sea fortresses like Sindhudurg and Vijaydurg on ocean reefs, and Admiral Kanhoji Angre remained undefeated against Portuguese, British, and Dutch fleets, challenging the colonial Cartaz pass system.
Trap 4: Claiming that the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) Permanently Ended Maratha Power
Why Students Lose Marks: Students assume that the tragic defeat at Panipat in 1761 meant the extinction of the Maratha Empire.
Topper's Rule: Panipat was a severe psychological blow, but Maratha power rebounded within ten years under Peshwa Madhavrao I! By 1771, Mahadji Shinde recaptured Delhi, reinstated Emperor Shah Alam II, and the Marathas continued to rule North India and Delhi until 1803.
Trap 5: Portraying Shivaji's Movement as Anti-Islamic or Communal
Why Students Lose Marks: Answering that Hindavi Swarajya was founded to persecute other religious communities.
Topper's Rule: Shivaji revived indigenous cultural pride while strictly enforcing religious inclusivity (Sarva Dharma Sambhava). He gave strict orders never to harm mosques, churches, or the Holy Quran; his royal navy admirals (Daryasarang Daulat Khan, Siddi Misri), artillery chief (Nur Khan Beg), personal bodyguard (Madari Mehtar), and Panipat artillery commander (Ibrahim Khan Gardi) were all Muslims who served with fierce loyalty.

15 Rapid Revision Points (The 3-Minute Exam Snapshot)

  1. Geographic Roots: The Marathas are native to the Deccan plateau (Maharashtra), unified by the Marathi language which possesses rich literature dating back to the 12th century.
  2. Bhakti Renaissance: Saints like Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram, and Ramdas preached egalitarian devotion (Abhangas) in spoken Marathi, eroding caste divisions and forging cultural solidarity.
  3. Birth & Swarajya: Born at Shivneri in 1630 to Shahji and Jijabai, Shivaji captured Torna fort at age 16 (1646) and pledged to establish sovereign Hindavi Swarajya.
  4. Guerrilla Warfare (Ganimi Kava): Leveraging the rugged Sahyadri cliffs, Shivaji used speed, surprise, and terrain mastery to rout larger armies, slaying Afzal Khan with the Wāgh Nakh in 1659.
  5. Surgical Strike & Surat: In 1663, Shivaji carried out a midnight surgical strike on Shaista Khan in Pune's Lal Mahal; in 1664 and 1670, he raided Surat for over 1 crore rupees while strictly sparing mosques and charitable citizens.
  6. Treaty of Purandar & Agra Escape: Ceded 23 forts to Mirza Raja Jai Singh in 1665; placed under house arrest in Agra in 1666, he made a legendary escape hidden inside fruit/sweet baskets.
  7. Coronation at Raigad (1674): Consecrated by Gaga Bhatt with Vedic rites as 'Shri Raja Shiva Chhatrapati', founding the Rājyābhiṣheka Shaka calendar and minting gold Hon and copper Shivrai coins.
  8. Dakshina Digvijaya & Anti-Slavery Edict: Conquered Gingee and Vellore in 1677–78, securing strategic depth, and banned the Dutch colonial slave trade in southern ports.
  9. The 27-Year War (1681–1707): After Sambhaji's martyrdom in 1689 and Rajaram's defense of Gingee, Maharani Tarabai launched northern counter-invasions, exhausting Aurangzeb until his death in 1707.
  10. Ashta Pradhana Governance: Eight ministers headed by the Peshwa; abolished hereditary jagirs in favor of direct cash salaries (rokh); provided welfare pensions for war widows.
  11. Dual Taxation: Levied Chauth (25%) for military protection and Sardeshmukhi (additional 10%) as supreme hereditary headman on external provinces.
  12. Maritime Supremacy: Founded Navy in 1657; built offshore sea forts (Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg); Admiral Kanhoji Angre remained undefeated and challenged European Cartaz passes.
  13. Cultural Revival: Cast the Sanskrit Rājmudrā ("Bhadrāya Rājate"), compiled the Rājya-Vyavahāra-Koṣha dictionary, and used the cursive Moḍī script for state administration.
  14. Pan-Indian Women & Cultural Renaissance: Ahilyabai Holkar rebuilt Kashi Vishwanath and Somnath and founded Maheshwari handlooms; the Nagpur Bhonsles revived Lord Jagannath worship in Puri (Odisha); Raja Serfoji II in Thanjavur patronized Bharatanatyam and founded Dhanwantari Mahal.
  15. The British Conquest: By 1771, Mahadji Shinde recaptured Delhi. The British fought three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818) to take India effectively from the Marathas, who seeded the ideals of Indian independence.
