2.1 What is an "Unknown" and Why Do We Need It?

In our daily lives, we constantly encounter situations where some information is readily available, but the key numerical answer is missing:

  • "A taxi driver charges a fixed flag-down fare of ₹800 plus ₹20 for every kilometre driven. If a passenger's total bill is ₹2200, how many kilometres did the taxi travel?"
  • "Jasmine arranges matchsticks in triangular patterns where each step follows the rule 2n + 1. She has exactly 99 matchsticks. Which step in the sequence can she build?"
  • "Two friends Ramesh and Suresh have 60 marbles together. Ramesh has 30 more marbles than Suresh. How many marbles does each boy hold?"

In earlier classes, arithmetic taught us to work forward: starting from known numbers (like 800 + 20 × 70) and calculating the result (2200). But algebra flips the telescope around! In algebra, we start from the final outcome (the bill is ₹2200) and travel backward to reveal the hidden mystery number.

Formal Definition: Variable (Letter-Number)
A variable (or letter-number) is an alphabet letter (such as x, y, n, p, or m) used to represent a numerical quantity whose value is currently unknown or can change. Unlike an ordinary English letter, an algebraic letter-number obeys every law of arithmetic: it can be added, subtracted, multiplied, divided, squared, and compared.

2.2 The Two-Pan Balance Scale: The Heart of Equality

The equal sign (=) is not merely a signal saying "calculate the answer." In mathematics, the equal sign represents a state of perfect physical balance, exactly like a two-pan beam balance used by a vegetable vendor or goldsmith:

  • Left Hand Side (LHS): The contents of the left weighing pan.
  • Central Fulcrum (=): The pivot point upon which the horizontal beam rests. The horizontal alignment indicates that both pans hold identical total weights.
  • Right Hand Side (RHS): The contents of the right weighing pan.

2.3 The Four Fundamental Invariance Axioms of Equality

Because an equation behaves identically to a balanced scale, we can perform four fundamental operations on both sides without disrupting the state of balance:

Axiom Name Physical Scale Meaning Algebraic Statement Class 7 Textbook Example
Addition Property Placing equal physical weights onto both pans keeps the scale level. If a = b, then a + c = b + c x - 4 = 7 ⇒ x - 4 + 4 = 7 + 4 ⇒ x = 11
Subtraction Property Removing identical weights or identical sacks from both pans preserves balance. If a = b, then a - c = b - c 2y + 7 = 21 ⇒ 2y + 7 - 7 = 21 - 7 ⇒ 2y = 14
Multiplication Property Multiplying the contents of both pans by the exact same positive factor keeps them balanced. If a = b, then a × c = b × c u / 15 = 6 ⇒ (u / 15) × 15 = 6 × 15 ⇒ u = 90
Division Property Dividing the contents of both pans into equal fractional piles maintains balance. If a = b, then a ÷ c = b ÷ c (c ≠ 0) 11y = 66 ⇒ 11y ÷ 11 = 66 ÷ 11 ⇒ y = 6

2.5 Why Trial and Error Fails: The Need for Systematic Inversion

When faced with an equation such as 2n + 1 = 99, a student might guess:

Guess n = 10 ⇒ LHS = 2(10) + 1 = 21 ≠ 99 (Much too small!)
Guess n = 40 ⇒ LHS = 2(40) + 1 = 81 ≠ 99 (Closer...)
Guess n = 50 ⇒ LHS = 2(50) + 1 = 101 ≠ 99 (Slightly too high!)
Guess n = 49 ⇒ LHS = 2(49) + 1 = 99 = RHS ✓ (Correct!)

While guessing worked here because 49 is a small whole number, trial and error has fatal limitations in higher mathematics:

  1. Fails on Fractions and Decimals: If the equation is 5x - 4 = 7, the solution is x = 11/5 = 2.2. A student guessing integers (1, 2, 3...) will never hit 2.2 and might wrongly conclude that the equation has no solution!
  2. Fails on Negative Numbers: Puzzles involving debts, temperature drops, or negative coordinates require systematic integer arithmetic.
  3. Gross Inefficiency: In large-scale real-life systems (like calculating satellite trajectories or banking loans), trial and error would require trillions of guesses.

2.6 The Principle of Operational Inversion: The Onion-Peeling Model

How do we solve equations without guessing? By viewing an equation as an encrypted secret wrapped inside layers of arithmetic operations. To reach the unknown variable at the core, we must peel off each operational layer from outside to inside, using the opposite (inverse) operation:

The Onion-Peeling Rule of Algebra
To unwind an equation, reverse the standard order of operations (BODMAS/PEMDAS). During evaluation, we do Brackets ⇒ Multiplication/Division ⇒ Addition/Subtraction. Therefore, during solving (unwinding), we peel off:
1. Outer Additions / Subtractions FIRST (by applying their additive inverse).
2. Outer Multiplications / Divisions SECOND (by applying their multiplicative inverse).
3. Inner Parentheses LAST.

Consider solving 5x - 4 = 7:

Step 1 (Peel outer subtraction): Add 4 to BOTH sides ⇒ 5x - 4 + 4 = 7 + 4 ⇒ 5x = 11
Step 2 (Peel outer multiplication): Divide BOTH sides by 5 ⇒ 5x ÷ 5 = 11 ÷ 5 ⇒ x = 11/5 = 2.2
Check: 5(11/5) - 4 = 11 - 4 = 7 = RHS ✓

2.7 Transposition: The Rigorous Shortcut

In classroom practice, writing "+ 4 on both sides" or "- 7 on both sides" every single time can become lengthy. Mathematicians use an abbreviated technique called Transposition.

What is Transposition?
Transposition means shifting a term from one side of an equation across the equals sign to the opposite side. When a term moves across '=', its mathematical operation inverts:
• A positive added term (+a) becomes a subtracted term (-a).
• A subtracted term (-a) becomes an added term (+a).
• A multiplying factor (×m) becomes a divisor (÷m).
• A dividing denominator (÷d) becomes a multiplier (×d).
Critical Conceptual Guardrail: Transposition is NOT Magic!
Students often develop the misconception that numbers "jump across the equal sign and magically change sign." Always remember: Transposition is merely the fast record of performing an inverse operation on both sides!
When 2y + 7 = 21 becomes 2y = 21 - 7, the '+7' did not jump; we subtracted 7 from both pans, causing +7 - 7 to cancel out to 0 on the left, leaving -7 on the right.

What happens when unknown sacks sit on both pans of the scale, as in Fig. 7.11 (5 sacks = 2 sacks + 21 kg) or Example 6 (6y + 7 = 4y + 21)?

Physical Strategy: Remove sacks from both pans until sacks remain on ONLY ONE pan!
Algebraic Step 1: Subtract the smaller variable term (4y) from BOTH sides:
 6y + 7 - 4y = 4y + 21 - 4y ⇒ 2y + 7 = 21
Algebraic Step 2: Subtract the constant term (+7) from BOTH sides:
 2y + 7 - 7 = 21 - 7 ⇒ 2y = 14
Algebraic Step 3: Divide both sides by the coefficient (2):
 y = 14 ÷ 2 ⇒ y = 7

2.9 Generating Chains of Equivalent Equations

An equation is not a one-way street. We can start from a known fact and build upwards, or start from a complex equation and unwind downward:

Start with solution: y = 5
Multiply both sides by 3 ⇒ 3y = 15
Add 6 to both sides ⇒ 3y + 6 = 21
Divide both sides by 3 ⇒ y + 2 = 7
Subtract 2 from both sides ⇒ y = 5

Because every forward step preserves equality, reversing each step in exact opposite order restores the original solution. This proves that every equation in an equivalent chain shares the identical solution!

2.10 Ancient Roots of Algebra: Bījagaṇita and the Global Heritage

Algebra was not invented in modern Europe. Its deepest foundations were laid in ancient India and classical Islamic scholarship:

  • The Metaphor of Bīja (Seed): In Sanskrit, algebra is called Bījagaṇita (ବୀଜଗଣିତ). Bīja means "seed" and gaṇita means "computation". Just as a giant banyan tree is completely concealed inside a tiny, invisible seed, the exact numerical answer to a complex real-world riddle is concealed inside the unknown letter-number. Solving an equation is like watering the seed step-by-step until the tree emerges in full view!
  • Brahmagupta (628 CE): In Chapter 18 of his magnum opus Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta, the great Indian astronomer and mathematician Brahmagupta formulated the arithmetic of zero, negative numbers, and unknowns. He introduced abbreviations for multiple unknowns based on colors:
    • yā: short for yāvat-tāvat ("as much as needed" — the primary unknown x).
    • kā: short for kālaka (black — a second unknown y).
    • nī: short for nīlaka (blue — a third unknown z).
    • pī: short for pītaka (yellow).
    • lo: short for lohita (red).
    • rū: short for rūpa (definite form — constant numbers).
    • Negative dot: A dot placed above a number (e.g., 8̇) indicated a negative quantity (-8).
    Fields Medalist David Mumford famously remarked: "Brahmagupta is the key person in the creation of algebra as we know it."
  • Brahmagupta's Master Linear Formula: For equations of the form Ax + B = Cx + D, Brahmagupta provided the direct formula:
    x = (D - B) / (A - C)
  • Āryabhaṭa (499 CE): Introduced Viparīta Vidhi (or Viloma Vidhi — the Method of Inversion), demonstrating that equations must be solved by performing operations in reverse sequence.
  • Bakhśhālī Manuscript (c. 300 CE): Written on birch bark in Sharada script, this ancient manuscript found in Bakhshali (near Peshawar) contains practical word riddles including the famous four-person wealth distribution problem.
  • Bhāskarāchārya II (1150 CE): In his famous text Bījagaṇita, Bhāskara poses delightful poetic riddles, including the horse-trading debt puzzle (300 + 6x = 10x - 100).
  • Al-Khwarizmī (825 CE): Working in Baghdad, Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi studied translated Indian Sanskrit texts. He authored Kitāb al-jabr wa-l-muqābala. The word al-jabr means "restoration" (transposing negative terms by adding them to the other side) and al-muqābala means "balancing/cancellation" (reducing equal terms on both sides). The word al-jabr gave rise to the modern word Algebra!

Table 3.4: Brahmagupta's Master Linear Solution Formula (628 CE)

Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta Chapter 18 Theorem
For any linear equation involving a single unknown on both sides of the form:
Ax + B = Cx + D
The universal solution is given by the ratio of the difference of constants to the difference of unknown coefficients:
x = (D − B) / (A − C)
Derivation from First Principles:
1. Subtract Cx from both sides: Ax - Cx + B = D ⇒ (A - C)x + B = D
2. Subtract B from both sides: (A - C)x = D - B
3. Divide both sides by (A - C): x = (D - B) / (A - C)  [Provided A ≠ C]

In-Text Prompt (Page 167): Can Jasmine Make an Arrangement Using Exactly 200 Sticks?
1. What is Given?
The sequence rule is 2n + 1 matchsticks for position n (where n must be a positive integer 1, 2, 3...). Target stick count = 200.
2. What is Required?
Determine whether it is mathematically possible to form an arrangement in this sequence using exactly 200 sticks.

Parity and Integer constraints: 2n is always an EVEN integer for any integer n. Therefore, 2n + 1 is always strictly an ODD integer.

Since 99.5 is NOT a whole number, you would need half a matchstick, which is impossible in this sequence.
Furthermore, 2n + 1 is an ODD number for all whole numbers n, whereas 200 is an EVEN number!
Conclusion: It is strictly IMPOSSIBLE to make an arrangement using exactly 200 sticks.

Position 99 uses 2(99) + 1 = 199 sticks. Position 100 uses 2(100) + 1 = 201 sticks. 200 falls directly in the gap! ✓

Question Statement
(a) Prove from the fundamental balance scale axioms that any linear equation in the form Ax + B = Cx + D (where A ≠ C) has the unique solution x = (D − B) / (A − C).
(b) Apply this formula to solve the financial balance problem: "A shopkeeper has ₹1200 and 15 bags of rice. Another has ₹2800 and 7 bags of rice. If both have identical total assets, find the value of one bag of rice."
Step-by-Step Marking Scheme (5 Marks Total)
Part (a): Formal Proof of Brahmagupta's Formula [2.5 Marks]
Given: Ax + B = Cx + D
Step 1 (Subtraction Property of Equality): Subtract Cx from both sides:
Ax − Cx + B = Cx − Cx + D
(A − C)x + B = D [1.0 Mark]
Step 2 (Subtraction Property of Equality): Subtract B from both sides:
(A − C)x + B − B = D − B
(A − C)x = D − B [0.5 Mark]
Step 3 (Division Property of Equality): Since A ≠ C, (A − C) ≠ 0. Divide both sides by (A − C):
x = (D − B) / (A − C) [Q.E.D. — 1.0 Mark]

Part (b): Application to Rice Assets [2.5 Marks]
Let value of 1 bag of rice = ₹x.
Shopkeeper 1 assets = 15x + 1200
Shopkeeper 2 assets = 7x + 2800 [0.5 Mark]
Equation: 15x + 1200 = 7x + 2800
Here: A = 15, B = 1200, C = 7, D = 2800 [0.5 Mark]
Applying Brahmagupta's formula:
x = (2800 − 1200) / (15 − 7)
x = 1600 / 8
x = ₹200 per bag of rice [1.0 Mark]
Check: Shopkeeper 1 = 15(200)+1200 = ₹4200. Shopkeeper 2 = 7(200)+2800 = ₹4200. Equal! [0.5 Mark]

6.1 The Five Classic Exam Hall Traps & How to Avoid Them

Trap 1: The Dividing-By-Negative Sign Flip Error

When solving an equation like −3x = 12, students often confuse transposition of negative coefficients with addition!

The Common Blunder:
−3x = 12
x = 12 + 3 = 15  [DISASTER!]
or
x = 12 / (+3) = 4  [Flipped sign while dividing!]
The Mathematical Remedy:
In −3x, the number −3 is MULTIPLYING x.
The inverse of multiplying by −3 is DIVIDING by −3:
x = 12 ÷ (−3)
x = −4
Memory Hook: "Dividing carries the sign with it! If you divide by a negative number, keep the negative on the denominator!"
Trap 2: The Half-Hearted Distributive Expansion

When expanding parentheses like 4(x + 6), students multiply the first term but forget the second!

The Common Blunder:
4(x + 6) − 8 = 20
4x + 6 − 8 = 20  [FORGOT 4 × 6!]
4x − 2 = 20 ⇒ x = 5.5
The Mathematical Remedy:
The outside factor 4 belongs to EVERY resident inside the bracket:
4 × x + 4 × 6 − 8 = 20
4x + 24 − 8 = 20
4x + 16 = 20 ⇒ 4x = 4 ⇒ x = 1
Memory Hook: "The factor outside the gate shakes hands with EVERYONE inside the house!"
Trap 3: The "Subtract From" Reversed Order Trap

English word problem syntax often reverses algebraic subtraction order!

The Common Blunder:
"Subtract 7 from three times a number x":
Written as: 7 − 3x  [REVERSED! Completely wrong sign and root!]
The Mathematical Remedy:
Whatever comes AFTER the word "from" is the starting quantity:
Starting quantity = 3x.
Subtract 7 from it ⇒ 3x − 7
Memory Hook: "Start with what comes after 'FROM'! Take away what comes before!"
Trap 4: The Partial Fraction Multiplier Trap

When clearing denominators by multiplying by an LCM, students often multiply the fractions but forget to multiply the solitary whole numbers!

The Common Blunder:
(x / 3) + 5 = 10
Multiplying by 3:
x + 5 = 30  [FORGOT TO MULTIPLY +5 BY 3!]
x = 25
The Mathematical Remedy:
Multiplying both sides means multiplying EVERY term on both pans:
3 × (x / 3) + 3 × 5 = 3 × 10
x + 15 = 30
x = 15
Memory Hook: "No free rides! When multiplying an equation, every single term pays the toll!"
Trap 5: The "Circular Verification" False Security Trap

Checking your answer in an already-simplified intermediate equation that already contains a calculation error!

The Common Blunder:
Original: 2(x + 3) − 5 = 15.
Step 1 blunder: 2x + 3 − 5 = 15 ⇒ 2x − 2 = 15.
Student checks in '2x − 2 = 15'!
Concludes answer is correct even though original bracket expansion was broken!
The Mathematical Remedy:
ALWAYS substitute the calculated root back into the RAW ORIGINAL EQUATION from the question paper:
LHS = 2(7 + 3) − 5 = 2(10) − 5 = 15 = RHS ✓.
Memory Hook: "Verify at the source! Never check your work against your own intermediate steps!"

Essential snapshot summaries for lightning-fast revision before entering the examination hall:

1
Equation Definition: An algebraic equation is a mathematical statement showing that two expressions have equal value, joined by '='.
2

Linear Property: A linear equation in one unknown has the variable appearing only to the first power (exponent 1, never x² or 1/x).

7

9

Ancient Notation: Brahmagupta (628 CE) used yā (yāvat-tāvat) for x, rū (rūpa) for constants, and superposed dots (8̇) for negative numbers.

12
No-Solution Condition: An equation of the form Ax + B = Ax + C where B ≠ C yields a contradiction (B = C) and has NO solution.
13
Distributive Sign Care: When removing parentheses preceded by a negative sign, reverse all signs inside: −a(b − c) = −ab + ac.
14
Clearing Fractions: Multiply EVERY single term on both sides by the LCM of all denominators to eliminate fractional fractions cleanly.
15
