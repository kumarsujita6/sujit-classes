1. Inside a Torchlight: How Everyday Portable Light Works

When you take apart a standard household torchlight, you find five essential parts working in harmony:

  • Cylindrical Electric Cells: Stored inside the plastic body to supply electrical energy.
  • Lamp (Bulb or LED): Placed at the front in front of a curved shiny reflector.
  • Reflector: A shiny parabolic metallic bowl that focuses light rays into a powerful, straight parallel beam.
  • Slide Switch: A plastic slider with a metal strip beneath it that moves back and forth to complete or break the circuit.
  • Metal Spring / Strip: Runs along the casing connecting the base of the battery to the switch.

2. The Electric Cell and the Battery

Key Definition • Electric Cell:
An electric cell is a compact portable source of electrical energy. Inside the cell, chemical energy is converted into electrical energy. An electric cell has two distinct terminals:
• Positive Terminal (+): Marked by a protruding metal cap at the top.
• Negative Terminal (–): Marked by a flat metal (zinc) disc at the base.
Key Definition • Battery:
A combination of two or more electric cells connected together in series is called a battery. In a standard battery, the positive terminal of one cell is connected to the negative terminal of the next cell. This adds up their voltages, giving a stronger electrical push (voltage) to operate appliances!

3. Incandescent Bulb vs. LED: A Technological Revolution

In recent years, traditional glass bulbs with thin coiled wires have been almost completely replaced by LEDs. Let us understand why:

Feature Incandescent Torch Bulb Light Emitting Diode (LED)
Light Producing Element A microscopic coiled wire called tungsten filament that glows red-hot. A tiny semiconductor chip encapsulated inside an epoxy plastic lens.
Polarity Sensitivity No polarity: Glows regardless of which way the battery terminals are connected. Strict polarity: Glows ONLY when the longer leg (+) connects to (+) and shorter leg (–) connects to (–).
Energy Efficiency Low: Wastes 90% of electrical energy as unwanted heat; filament burns out easily. Extremely High: Converts almost all electrical energy directly into light without getting hot.
Durability & Lifespan Fragile glass bulb; if dropped, the delicate filament snaps easily. Solid-state electronic device; highly rugged, shockproof, lasting tens of thousands of hours.

4. What Is an Electrical Circuit? Closed vs. Open Loops

Key Definition • Electrical Circuit:
An electrical circuit is a continuous, unbroken conducting path between the two terminals of an electric cell through which electric current can flow.
• Closed Circuit: When there is no gap anywhere in the circuit, the loop is complete. Electric current flows, and the lamp glows!
• Open Circuit: If there is any break, loose wire, or open switch, the conducting path is broken. Current cannot jump across the gap, and the lamp stays unlit!
Conventional Direction of Electric Current:
By international scientific convention, the direction of electric current in an external circuit is always taken to flow from the Positive (+) terminal to the Negative (–) terminal of the electric cell.

6. Electrical Conductors vs. Insulators

Not all materials allow electricity to flow through them. We can test any material by inserting it into a gap in a simple circuit called a conduction tester:

Scientific Comparison Table

Category Scientific Definition Everyday Examples & Uses
Electrical Conductors Materials that allow electric charges (electrons) to pass through them freely and easily with very low resistance. Examples: Copper, aluminium, iron, brass, gold, silver, graphite (pencil lead), tap water with dissolved salts.
Uses: Electrical wiring, appliance plugs, switch contacts.
Electrical Insulators Materials that offer extremely high resistance and do NOT permit electric current to pass through them under normal conditions. Examples: Rubber, plastic (PVC), dry wood, pure glass, ceramic, dry air, paper.
Uses: Plastic coating over wires, rubber gloves worn by electricians, handles of screwdrivers and pliers.

Must-know definitions, technical terms, and memory shortcuts for Chapter 3.

Term Scientific Concept Exam Memory Key

In-Text Activity Solutions (Activities 3.1 to 3.11)

Activity 3.1 & 3.2: Exploring Torchlight & Observing Electric Cell Terminals Fully Solved

Activity 3.1 Observations: A torchlight consists of an outer casing, 2 or 3 cylindrical cells arranged head-to-tail, an incandescent or LED bulb, a reflective cone, and a slider switch. Sliding the switch presses a metal strip against the bulb base, completing the circuit.

Activity 3.2 Observations: On a standard 1.5 V cell, the metallic protruding cap is labelled with a '+' (positive terminal). The opposite flat circular metal bottom is labelled with a '–' (negative terminal). Chemical reactions inside provide the voltage.

Activity 3.3: Making a Battery of Two and Four Cells Fully Solved

Procedure & Rule:

  • To make a battery, place the positive terminal (+) of the first cell in contact with the negative terminal (–) of the second cell.
  • If two cells are placed side by side in a plastic holder, a metal strip at the back joins the (+) of one cell to the (–) of the adjoining cell.
  • Critical Warning: If two positive terminals (+ to +) or two negative terminals (– to –) are connected facing each other, their voltages oppose and cancel each other, and NO current will flow!
Activity 3.7 & 3.10: Testing LED Polarity and Drawing Circuit Diagrams Fully Solved

LED Behaviour (Activity 3.7):

  • When the longer lead is connected to (+) and the shorter lead to (–), the LED glows brightly.
  • When connections are reversed (longer lead to –, shorter to +), the LED does NOT glow!
  • Why? An LED is a diode—a one-way electrical valve that only allows electric current to flow through it in one forward direction.

End-of-Chapter Exercises (Questions 1 to 11)

Question 1: Choose the incorrect statement regarding an electric switch. Exercise Q1

Options Given:

(i) A switch is the source of electric current in a circuit.
(ii) A switch helps to complete or break the circuit.
(iii) When the switch is open, there is an air gap between its terminals.
(iv) Switches are used in both DC battery circuits and household AC wiring.

Correct Answer: Statement (i) is INCORRECT!

Reason: A switch is NOT a source of electric current. The electric cell or battery is the source of electrical energy. A switch merely acts as a mechanical gatekeeper that closes or opens the conducting path.

Question 2: What is the purpose of using symbols for electrical components? Exercise Q2

Answer (Point-Wise):

  1. Simplicity and Speed: Drawing realistic pictures of complex cells, lamps, switches, and generators is tedious and time-consuming. Symbols allow anyone to sketch a circuit in seconds.
  2. Clarity: Schematic diagrams eliminate visual clutter, clearly revealing electrical connections without confusion.
  3. Universal International Language: Electrical symbols are standardised globally. An engineer or technician in India, Japan, Germany, or the USA can read the exact same circuit schematic without language barriers.
Question 3: In Fig. 3.17 (two lamps connected in series in a single loop), if the filament of one of the lamps is broken, will the other glow? Justify your answer. Exercise Q3

Answer: NO, the other lamp will NOT glow.

Scientific Justification:

  • In a series circuit, all components are connected one after another in a single continuous loop. The same electric current must flow through both lamps to complete the circuit.
  • If the tungsten filament of one lamp breaks (it fuses), a physical gap is created inside that lamp.
  • This turns the entire setup into an open circuit. Because electric current cannot jump across the broken gap, current stops flowing through the entire loop. Therefore, the second lamp also goes off!
Question 4: A student forgot to remove the insulator plastic covering from the ends of connecting wires while assembling a circuit. Will the lamp glow? Exercise Q4

Answer: NO, the lamp will NOT glow.

Scientific Reason:

  • The outer covering of electrical wires is made of PVC plastic or rubber, which is an electrical insulator.
  • Insulators have extremely high electrical resistance and do not allow electric charges to pass through them.
  • Unless the plastic coating is stripped off at both ends to expose the bare metallic copper wire, no electrical contact is established with the cell or bulb terminals, leaving the circuit open.

Question 6: In Fig. 3.18, analysing which lamp(s) will glow with combinations of switches S1 and S2. Exercise Q6

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  • Case (i): S2 is ON, S1 is OFF:
    → Only Lamp L2 will glow. The branch containing L1 is open (S1 is OFF), but the branch with L2 and S2 forms a complete closed loop with the battery.
  • Case (ii): S2 is OFF, S1 is ON:
    → Only Lamp L1 will glow. The branch containing L2 is open, while the branch with L1 forms a closed loop.
  • Case (iii): Both S1 and S2 are ON:
    → Both Lamp L1 and Lamp L2 will glow! Both parallel branches form independent closed circuits.
  • Case (iv): Both S1 and S2 are OFF:
    → Neither lamp will glow. Both conducting paths are completely broken.
Question 7: Vidyut assembled a circuit, but even after closing the switch, the lamp does not glow. List possible reasons and how to troubleshoot. Exercise Q7

Possible Faults & Troubleshooting Guide:

  1. Fused Bulb: The tungsten filament inside the lamp may be snapped. Fix: Inspect against light; replace with a fresh bulb.
  2. Discharged (Dead) Cell: The chemicals inside the cell are exhausted. Fix: Test the cell with a known working torch or voltmeter.
  3. Unstripped Connecting Wires: Plastic insulation was not peeled off at the wire tips. Fix: Strip 1 cm of insulation to expose bare copper wire.
  4. Loose Connections: Wires are loosely touching terminal screws without firm mechanical contact. Fix: Tighten screws and bind wire loops tightly.
  5. Oxidised (Rusted) Terminals: Rust on cell caps or clips acts as an insulator. Fix: Rub terminals with sandpaper to expose shiny metal.
  6. Reversed LED Polarity (if LED is used): Longer wire was mistakenly connected to (–) terminal. Fix: Reverse the orientation of the LED leads.
Question 8: In Fig. 3.20, in which case(s) will the lamp/LED NOT glow when the switch is closed? Exercise Q8

Analysis of Cases:

  • Case with Opposing Cells (+ to + or – to –): The lamp will NOT glow because identical polarities cancel the electrical drive.
  • Case with Reversed LED: If the longer lead of the LED is connected to the (–) terminal, the LED will NOT glow because of unidirectional diode blocking.
  • Case with Broken Circuit Wire: The lamp will NOT glow due to an open circuit.
  • Normal Series Connection: Only the setup where cells are connected in series (+ to –) and LED leads are correctly polarised (+ to longer lead) will glow!
Question 9: Suppose the '+' and '–' markings on a battery have rubbed off. Suggest a foolproof method to identify the two terminals. Exercise Q9

Foolproof Method Using an LED:

  1. Take an LED (whose longer leg is known to be the positive terminal and shorter leg is the negative terminal) and a safety resistor (or low-voltage setup).
  2. Connect one battery terminal to the longer leg of the LED and the other battery terminal to the shorter leg.
  3. Result A: If the LED glows, then the battery terminal connected to the longer leg is the Positive (+) Terminal, and the terminal connected to the shorter leg is the Negative (–) Terminal!
  4. Result B: If the LED does not glow, reverse the connections. The terminal that now connects to the longer leg when it lights up is the Positive (+) terminal!
Question 10: You are given six cells marked A to F. Design an activity to identify which are working and which are exhausted. Exercise Q10

Experimental Protocol:

  1. Items Required: A known working torch bulb (1.5 V) in a bulb holder, two connecting copper wires with stripped ends, and the six test cells (A, B, C, D, E, F).
  2. Procedure:
    • Connect one wire from the base terminal of the bulb holder to the flat negative terminal of Cell A.
    • Touch the second wire from the bulb holder to the positive metal cap of Cell A.
    • Record whether the bulb glows and observe its brightness.
    • Repeat the exact procedure for Cells B, C, D, E, and F one by one.
  3. Inference:
    → Cells that make the bulb glow brightly are fully charged and working.
    → Cells that produce a very dim glow are partially discharged.
    → Cells that produce zero glow are completely dead / exhausted.
Question 11: Tanya made a circuit with two cells in series to light an LED, but connected them improperly. Will it glow? How to correct the wiring? Exercise Q11

Answer:

In Tanya's faulty diagram, either the cells were connected with like poles facing each other (+ to +), or the LED leads were reversed. In that condition, the LED will NOT glow.

Correct Wiring Scheme:

  1. Connect the positive terminal (+) metal cap of Cell 1 to the circuit wire leading to the longer lead (+) of the LED.
  2. Connect the negative terminal (–) flat base of Cell 1 directly to the positive terminal (+) metal cap of Cell 2.
  3. Connect the negative terminal (–) flat base of Cell 2 through the switch to the shorter lead (–) of the LED.
  4. Now the two 1.5 V cells provide 3.0 V in series, and current flows in the forward direction, lighting the LED brilliantly!

Q1. In the symbol for an electric cell, what do the longer thinner line and the shorter thicker line represent?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (a)
In international electrical schematics, the longer, thinner parallel vertical line always denotes the positive terminal (+), while the shorter, thicker vertical bar denotes the negative terminal (–).

Q2. Why is an LED preferred over a traditional incandescent torch bulb?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (b)
Incandescent bulbs waste almost 90% of electrical energy as heat in making the tungsten filament glow. LEDs convert electrical energy directly into light with minimal heat loss and do not suffer from fragile filaments burning out.

Q3. A student inserts a pencil sharpened at both ends into a conduction tester gap. The tester bulb glows! This proves that:

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (c)
Pencil 'lead' is actually a mixture of graphite (an allotrope of carbon) and clay. Graphite is one of the rare non-metals that possesses free delocalised electrons, allowing it to conduct electricity efficiently!

Q4. What is the conventional direction of electric current in an external circuit?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (a)
By universal scientific convention established before electrons were discovered, electric current in an external circuit is always taken to flow from positive (+) to negative (–).

Q5. Why do electricians always wear thick rubber gloves while repairing household electrical switches?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (d)
The human body contains water and electrolytes, making it an electrical conductor. Rubber is an excellent insulator. Wearing rubber gloves breaks any potential electrical circuit between the live mains wire and the ground, shielding the electrician from fatal electric shocks.

Assertion & Reason Drills

Drill 1: Assertion (A): In a torch, two cells must be placed such that the positive terminal of one touches the negative terminal of the other.
Reason (R): Connecting cells in series (+ to –) combines their voltages to provide sufficient electrical push to light the lamp. Assertion-Reason
Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
Connecting cells in series head-to-tail (+ to –) adds their individual 1.5 V potentials (1.5 V + 1.5 V = 3.0 V), supplying the required voltage for the torch bulb.
Drill 2: Assertion (A): Pure distilled water is an excellent conductor of electricity.
Reason (R): Water molecules are made of hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Assertion-Reason
Answer: (d) Assertion (A) is completely FALSE, but Reason (R) is true.
Pure distilled water does not contain free dissolved ions and is actually a poor conductor (insulator)! Tap water conducts electricity only because it contains dissolved mineral salts.

Exam Hall Traps & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Bulletproof your preparation with common circuit diagram traps and a quick revision checklist.

Exam Hall Trap #1 • Reversing Battery Symbols in Schematics:
Trap: Drawing the thick bar as positive and the thin line as negative.
Correction: Always remember: Long & Thin = Positive (+); Short & Thick = Negative (–)!
Exam Hall Trap #2 • Forgetting That LEDs Have Strict Polarity:
Trap: Drawing an LED connected backwards and showing it glowing.
Correction: Unlike incandescent bulbs, an LED will NEVER glow if the negative terminal of the battery is connected to its longer lead!

15 Lightning Revision Points for Quick Exam Recap

  1. Electric Cell: Converts chemical energy into electrical energy; has a (+) metal cap and (–) flat zinc base.
  2. Battery Definition: A combination of two or more cells arranged in series (+ connected to –).
  3. Incandescent Lamp: Produces light when electric current heats a thin tungsten filament until it glows white-hot.
  4. Fused Bulb: Occurs when the filament inside snaps, creating a permanent gap that opens the circuit.
  5. LED Structure: Light Emitting Diode with two leads: longer is positive (anode), shorter is negative (cathode).
  6. Unidirectional Conduction: LEDs permit current to flow in one forward direction only; reversed connection prevents lighting.
  7. Electric Switch: A control device that deliberately closes (completes) or opens (breaks) a conducting path.
  8. Closed Circuit: Unbroken loop from (+) terminal back to (–) terminal; current flows and devices operate.
  9. Open Circuit: Any break or gap stops current flow immediately throughout the entire loop.
  10. Conventional Current Direction: Flows from positive (+) terminal to negative (–) terminal in external circuit wires.
  11. Circuit Diagram: A schematic representation of an electrical network using standardised scientific symbols.
  12. Conductor: A substance allowing electric current to pass through freely (e.g., copper, aluminium, iron, graphite).
  13. Insulator: A substance that strongly resists electric current flow (e.g., rubber, plastic, wood, glass).
  14. Graphite Anomaly: Although carbon/graphite is a non-metal, its free valence electrons allow it to conduct electricity.
  15. Electrical Safety: Electricians use tools with plastic handles and wear rubber gloves because rubber insulates against lethal electric shocks.
