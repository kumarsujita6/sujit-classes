1. The Evolution of Time Measurement

How did our ancestors tell time before the advent of wristwatches, smartphone screens, or digital wall clocks? They relied on periodic events in nature — phenomena that repeat regularly at predictable, identical intervals.

Natural Cycle Astronomical Basis Unit of Time Defined
Solar Day Time interval between one sunrise and the very next sunrise (Earth's rotation on its axis). 1 Solar Day = 24 Hours = 1,440 Minutes = 86,400 Seconds.
Lunar Month Time interval between one new moon and the next new moon (Moon's revolution around Earth). Roughly 29.5 days (approx. 1 Month).
Solar Year Time taken by Earth to complete one full revolution around the Sun (cycle of seasons). 365.25 Days = 1 Year.

Historical Man-Made Timekeeping Instruments

  • Sundial (Chhaya-Yantra): Operates on the principle that the position and length of the shadow cast by an upright vertical rod (called a gnomon) changes progressively as the Sun moves across the sky from east to west.
    Masterwork: The massive sundials at Jantar Mantar (built by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II in Jaipur, Delhi, Ujjain, Varanasi, and Mathura) can measure local solar time accurate to within two seconds!
    Major Drawback: Sundials are completely useless on overcast/cloudy days and cannot indicate time after sunset.
  • Water Clocks (Clepsydra & Ghatika-Yantra): Measured time by the steady, regulated flow of water.
    • Outflow type: Water drips at a constant rate out of a graduated vessel through a pinhole.
    • Inflow / Sinking bowl type (Ghatika-Yantra): Mentioned by ancient Indian mathematician-astronomer Aryabhata in Aryabhatiya. A lightweight copper bowl with a calibrated orifice in the bottom floats in a water basin; water slowly seeps inside until the bowl sinks, marking exactly one Ghati (24 minutes).
  • Sandglass (Hourglass): Measures an exact preset time interval (e.g. 1 hour) by the steady gravitational trickle of fine sand from an upper glass bulb to a lower bulb through a narrow constricted neck.
  • Candle Clock: A wax candle marked with numbered horizontal bands along its height. As the candle burns uniformly, the level of melting wax marks elapsed time.

2. The Simple Pendulum & The Birth of Modern Clocks

The breakthrough that led to modern accurate mechanical clocks came in 1583 when Italian polymath Galileo Galilei observed the swinging of a bronze chandelier suspended from the ceiling of Pisa Cathedral. Timing the swings with his own heartbeat, Galileo discovered that the time taken by the chandelier to complete one full swing remained virtually constant, regardless of whether the swing was wide or narrow.

Definition: Simple Pendulum & Associated Terms
A Simple Pendulum consists of a small heavy metallic sphere (called the bob) suspended by a light, inextensible thread from a rigid, friction-free support.
  • Mean Position (O): The central rest position of the bob when suspended vertically motionless.
  • Extreme Positions (A & B): The maximum displacement points reached by the swinging bob on either side of the mean position.
  • Oscillation: One complete to-and-fro motion of the pendulum bob. For example, moving from the mean position $O o A o B o O$, or from extreme position $A o B o A$.
  • Time Period ($T$): The precise time taken by the pendulum to complete exactly one full oscillation.
Mathematical Formula: Time Period of Pendulum
$$T = rac{ ext{Total Time Taken}}{ ext{Total Number of Oscillations}}$$
Activity 8.2: Measuring Time Period of a Simple Pendulum
Procedure: Set up a pendulum with a thread of length $1 ext{ metre}$ ($100 ext{ cm}$). Pull the bob gently to one side (about 5-8 cm displacement) and release it smoothly without pushing.
• Use a stopwatch to measure the total time taken for 20 complete oscillations.
• If 20 oscillations take $40.0 ext{ seconds}$, then: $$ ext{Time Period } T = rac{40.0 ext{ s}}{20} = 2.0 ext{ s}$$
• Repeat the measurement 3 times to find the average time period, minimizing reaction time error.
Teacher Exam Secret: What Affects the Time Period?
  • Length of the Pendulum ($L$): The time period depends directly on length. A longer pendulum swings more slowly and takes longer ($T \propto \sqrt{L}$). If you lengthen the string, $T$ increases!
  • Mass of the Bob: Does NOT change the time period! Whether the bob is a 20g wooden sphere or a 200g brass sphere, the pendulum takes the exact same time to oscillate.
  • Amplitude of Swing: For small angles of swing, amplitude has NO effect on the time period (Galileo's principle of isochronism).

Modern Timekeeping Technologies

  • Pendulum Clocks: Designed by Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens in 1656, utilizing the steady oscillation of a pendulum regulated by an escapement gear mechanism.
  • Quartz Clocks and Watches: Contain a miniature tuning-fork-shaped quartz crystal. When connected to a tiny battery cell, the crystal vibrates at an extraordinarily steady frequency (precisely 32,768 cycles per second). These vibrations drive an electronic microchip, providing accuracy to within seconds per year!
  • Atomic Clocks: Rely on the ultra-precise quantum resonance frequency of cesium-133 atoms ($9,192,631,770$ cycles per second). They lose or gain less than 1 second in 30 million years! Used in GPS satellites, ISRO NavIC constellation, and global telecommunications.
  • Assistive Clocks:
    • Braille Watch: Features raised tactile dots on the dial and sturdy mechanical hands that can be felt by visually challenged individuals.
    • Talking Watch: Synthesizes and announces the current time in audio upon pressing a button.

3. Slow or Fast: The Concept of Speed

In everyday observations, some objects move fast while others move sluggishly:
• A cheetah sprints at $100 ext{ km/h}$, while a snail crawls at barely $0.001 ext{ km/h}$.
• An aeroplane cruises at $800 ext{ km/h}$, while a bullock cart lumbers at $5 ext{ km/h}$.
To mathematically compare which object is moving faster, we evaluate their speed.

Definition: Speed
Speed is defined as the distance travelled by an object per unit of time. It quantifies how fast or slow an object covers ground.
Fundamental Speed Equation
$$ ext{Speed } (v) = rac{ ext{Distance } (s)}{ ext{Time } (t)}$$
Rearranged forms: $\quad ext{Distance } (s) = ext{Speed } (v) imes ext{Time } (t) \quad \Big| \quad ext{Time } (t) = rac{ ext{Distance } (s)}{ ext{Speed } (v)}$

Units of Speed & The Magic Conversion Factor

  • SI Base Unit: Metre per second ($ ext{m/s}$ or $ ext{m s}^{-1}$).
  • Vehicular / Practical Unit: Kilometre per hour ($ ext{km/h}$ or $ ext{km h}^{-1}$).
The $5/18$ Magic Shortcut (Master for Exams!)
Since $1 ext{ kilometre} = 1000 ext{ metres}$ and $1 ext{ hour} = 60 imes 60 = 3600 ext{ seconds}$: $$1 ext{ km/h} = rac{1000 ext{ m}}{3600 ext{ s}} = rac{5}{18} ext{ m/s}$$
• To convert $ ext{km/h} \longrightarrow ext{m/s}$ : Multiply by $\mathbf{ rac{5}{18}}$.
Example: $72 ext{ km/h} = 72 imes rac{5}{18} = 4 imes 5 = \mathbf{20 ext{ m/s}}$.

• To convert $ ext{m/s} \longrightarrow ext{km/h}$ : Multiply by $\mathbf{ rac{18}{5}}$ (or $ imes 3.6$).
Example: $25 ext{ m/s} = 25 imes rac{18}{5} = 5 imes 18 = \mathbf{90 ext{ km/h}}$.

Speedometer and Odometer

Instrument What it Measures Standard Display Unit
Speedometer Measures the instantaneous speed of the vehicle at that exact moment. $ ext{km/h}$
Odometer Measures the cumulative total distance travelled by the vehicle over its lifetime. $ ext{km}$

4. Uniform vs Non-Uniform Linear Motion

When an object moves along a straight path, its velocity can either remain perfectly constant or fluctuate continuously.

Parameter Uniform Motion Non-Uniform Motion
Definition An object travels along a straight line covering equal distances in equal intervals of time, no matter how small these intervals may be. An object travels along a straight line covering unequal distances in equal intervals of time (speed changes).
Speed Remains perfectly constant throughout the journey. Varies from moment to moment (accelerates, decelerates, stops).
Distance-Time Graph A perfectly straight oblique line sloping upward through the origin. A curved, zig-zag, or stepped non-linear line.
Real-World Frequency Extremely rare in daily life. (e.g. Light beam in space, satellite in circular orbit, high-speed train on a clear straight track at cruise speed). Overwhelmingly common in daily life. (e.g. A city bus, a child running, an auto-rickshaw in traffic).

Average Speed

Since almost all real-world motions are non-uniform, we define the overall motion by its Average Speed:

Average Speed Formula
$$ ext{Average Speed} = rac{ ext{Total Distance Travelled}}{ ext{Total Time Taken}}$$

Question 1: Calculate the speed of a car that travels 150 metres in 10 seconds. Express your answer in km/h.

Given Data

  • Distance travelled ($s$) = $150 ext{ m}$
  • Time taken ($t$) = $10 ext{ s}$

Step 1: Calculate Speed in m/s

$$ ext{Speed } (v) = rac{ ext{Distance}}{ ext{Time}} = rac{150 ext{ m}}{10 ext{ s}} = 15 ext{ m/s}$$

Step 2: Convert to km/h

$$ ext{Speed in km/h} = 15 imes rac{18}{5} = 3 imes 18 = \mathbf{54 ext{ km/h}}$$

Final Answer: The speed of the car is $54 ext{ km/h}$ (or $15 ext{ m/s}$).

Question 2: A runner completes 400 metres in 50 seconds. Another runner completes the same distance in 45 seconds. Who has a greater speed and by how much?

Given Data Distance for both runners = $400 ext{ m}$

  • Runner 1: $t_1 = 50 ext{ s}$
  • Runner 2: $t_2 = 45 ext{ s}$

Calculation of Speeds

$$ ext{Speed of Runner 1 } (v_1) = rac{400 ext{ m}}{50 ext{ s}} = 8.0 ext{ m/s}$$ $$ ext{Speed of Runner 2 } (v_2) = rac{400 ext{ m}}{45 ext{ s}} = rac{80}{9} ext{ m/s} pprox 8.89 ext{ m/s}$$

Comparison & Difference

Since $8.89 ext{ m/s} > 8.0 ext{ m/s}$, Runner 2 has a greater speed.

$$ ext{Difference} = 8.89 - 8.0 = \mathbf{0.89 ext{ m/s}} \quad \left( ext{or } rac{8}{9} ext{ m/s} ight)$$

Final Answer: Runner 2 has a greater speed by $0.89 ext{ m/s}$ (approx $3.2 ext{ km/h}$).

Question 3: A train travels at a speed of 25 m/s and covers a distance of 360 km. How much time does it take?

Given Data

  • Speed ($v$) = $25 ext{ m/s}$
  • Distance ($s$) = $360 ext{ km} = 360 imes 1000 ext{ m} = 360,000 ext{ m}$

Method 1 (SI Units - seconds to hours)

$$ ext{Time } (t) = rac{ ext{Distance}}{ ext{Speed}} = rac{360,000 ext{ m}}{25 ext{ m/s}} = 14,400 ext{ seconds}$$ $$ ext{Time in hours} = rac{14,400}{3600 ext{ s/h}} = \mathbf{4 ext{ hours}}$$

Method 2 (Convert Speed to km/h first)

$$ ext{Speed } (v) = 25 imes rac{18}{5} = 5 imes 18 = 90 ext{ km/h}$$ $$ ext{Time } (t) = rac{360 ext{ km}}{90 ext{ km/h}} = \mathbf{4 ext{ hours}}$$

Final Answer: The train takes exactly $4 ext{ hours}$ (or $14,400 ext{ s}$).

Question 4: A train travels 180 km in 3 h. Find its speed in (i) km/h, (ii) m/s. (iii) What distance will it travel in 4 h if it maintains the same speed throughout the journey?

Given Data Distance ($s_1$) = $180 ext{ km}$, Time ($t_1$) = $3 ext{ h}$

(i) Speed in km/h:

$$ ext{Speed } (v) = rac{ ext{Distance}}{ ext{Time}} = rac{180 ext{ km}}{3 ext{ h}} = \mathbf{60 ext{ km/h}}$$

(ii) Speed in m/s:

$$ ext{Speed in m/s} = 60 imes rac{5}{18} = rac{300}{18} = rac{50}{3} pprox \mathbf{16.67 ext{ m/s}}$$

(iii) Distance travelled in 4 h at constant speed:

$$ ext{Distance } (s) = ext{Speed } (v) imes ext{Time } (t) = 60 ext{ km/h} imes 4 ext{ h} = \mathbf{240 ext{ km}}$$

Final Summary: (i) $60 ext{ km/h}$, (ii) $16.67 ext{ m/s}$, (iii) $240 ext{ km}$.

Question 5: The fastest galloping horse can reach the speed of approximately 18 m/s. How does this compare to the speed of a train moving at 72 km/h?

Conversion to Common Unit

  • Speed of galloping horse = $18 ext{ m/s}$
  • Speed of train = $72 ext{ km/h}$

Converting the train's speed to $ ext{m/s}$:

$$ ext{Train speed} = 72 imes rac{5}{18} = 4 imes 5 = \mathbf{20 ext{ m/s}}$$

Alternatively, converting the horse's speed to $ ext{km/h}$:

$$ ext{Horse speed} = 18 imes rac{18}{5} = rac{324}{5} = \mathbf{64.8 ext{ km/h}}$$

Comparison

Since $20 ext{ m/s} > 18 ext{ m/s}$ ($72 ext{ km/h} > 64.8 ext{ km/h}$):

The train is moving faster than the galloping horse by a margin of $2 ext{ m/s}$ (or $7.2 ext{ km/h}$).

Question 8: A car covers 60 km in the first hour, 70 km in the second hour, and 50 km in the third hour. Is the motion uniform? Justify your answer. Find the average speed of the car.

Uniformity Evaluation

No, the motion of the car is NOT uniform.

Justification: In equal time intervals of $1 ext{ hour}$, the car covers unequal distances: $60 ext{ km}$ in the 1st hour, $70 ext{ km}$ in the 2nd hour, and $50 ext{ km}$ in the 3rd hour. Since the distance covered per unit time changes, the speed is variable, which defines non-uniform motion.

Calculation of Average Speed

$$ ext{Total Distance} = 60 ext{ km} + 70 ext{ km} + 50 ext{ km} = 180 ext{ km}$$ $$ ext{Total Time Taken} = 1 ext{ h} + 1 ext{ h} + 1 ext{ h} = 3 ext{ h}$$ $$ ext{Average Speed} = rac{ ext{Total Distance}}{ ext{Total Time}} = rac{180 ext{ km}}{3 ext{ h}} = \mathbf{60 ext{ km/h}}$$

Final Answer: The motion is non-uniform; average speed is $60 ext{ km/h}$.

Question 9: Which type of motion is more common in daily life—uniform or non-uniform? Provide three examples from your experience to support your answer.

Core Verdict Non-uniform motion is overwhelmingly more common in daily life. Perfectly uniform motion requires an object to travel along a completely straight path without encountering any friction, obstacles, or requiring changes in speed or direction — conditions rarely found in our everyday world.

Three Everyday Examples

  1. A School Bus: A school bus starts from rest, accelerates on clear lanes, slows down over speed breakers, halts completely at bus stops to pick up students, and stops at traffic lights. Its speed is constantly changing.
  2. A Person Jogging in a Colony Park: A runner speeds up initially, slows down when tired, navigates around walkers and sharp bends, and walks during cooldown. Unequal distances are covered in equal minutes.
  3. A Passenger Train Journey: A train departs the station slowly from $0 ext{ km/h}$, gains momentum to cruise at $100 ext{ km/h}$, slows down while crossing switches, junctions, or bridges, and brakes to a complete halt at destination platforms.

Question 11: A vehicle moves along a straight line and covers a distance of 2 km. In the first 500 m, it moves with a speed of 10 m/s and in the next 500 m, it moves with a speed of 5 m/s. With what speed should it move the remaining distance so that the journey is complete in 200 s? What is the average speed of the vehicle for the entire journey?

Given Total Parameters

  • Total Distance ($S$) = $2 ext{ km} = 2 imes 1000 ext{ m} = 2000 ext{ m}$
  • Total Target Time ($T$) = $200 ext{ s}$

Step 1: Time Taken in First Stage

$$s_1 = 500 ext{ m}, \quad v_1 = 10 ext{ m/s}$$ $$t_1 = rac{s_1}{v_1} = rac{500 ext{ m}}{10 ext{ m/s}} = 50 ext{ s}$$

Step 2: Time Taken in Second Stage

$$s_2 = 500 ext{ m}, \quad v_2 = 5 ext{ m/s}$$ $$t_2 = rac{s_2}{v_2} = rac{500 ext{ m}}{5 ext{ m/s}} = 100 ext{ s}$$

Step 3: Remaining Distance and Time for Third Stage

$$ ext{Remaining Distance } (s_3) = S - (s_1 + s_2) = 2000 - (500 + 500) = 1000 ext{ m}$$ $$ ext{Time Elapsed so far} = t_1 + t_2 = 50 ext{ s} + 100 ext{ s} = 150 ext{ s}$$ $$ ext{Remaining Time Available } (t_3) = T - 150 ext{ s} = 200 ext{ s} - 150 ext{ s} = 50 ext{ s}$$

Step 4: Required Speed for the Remaining Distance

$$v_3 = rac{s_3}{t_3} = rac{1000 ext{ m}}{50 ext{ s}} = \mathbf{20 ext{ m/s}}$$ $$ ext{In km/h: } 20 imes rac{18}{5} = 4 imes 18 = \mathbf{72 ext{ km/h}}$$

Step 5: Average Speed for Entire Journey

$$ ext{Average Speed} = rac{ ext{Total Distance}}{ ext{Total Time}} = rac{2000 ext{ m}}{200 ext{ s}} = \mathbf{10 ext{ m/s}}$$ $$ ext{In km/h: } 10 imes rac{18}{5} = 2 imes 18 = \mathbf{36 ext{ km/h}}$$

Final Summary:

  • Required speed for remaining $1000 ext{ m}$ = $20 ext{ m/s}$ ($72 ext{ km/h}$)
  • Average speed for the entire journey = $10 ext{ m/s}$ ($36 ext{ km/h}$)

Exam Hall Readiness: Traps & Lightning Revision

Common Exam Pitfalls & Mistakes

Trap 1: Multiplying by 18/5 instead of 5/18
Remember: When converting from $ ext{km/h}$ (larger unit) to $ ext{m/s}$ (smaller unit), the numerical answer must be smaller! Hence multiply by the fraction less than 1 ($ rac{5}{18}$). When converting from $ ext{m/s}$ to $ ext{km/h}$, multiply by $ rac{18}{5}$!
Trap 2: Counting a half swing as a full oscillation
Moving from extreme $A$ to extreme $B$ is only half an oscillation! A complete oscillation requires the bob to swing all the way back to $A$ ($A o B o A$).
Trap 3: Averaging speeds directly without considering time
If a vehicle travels $500 ext{ m}$ at $10 ext{ m/s}$ and $500 ext{ m}$ at $5 ext{ m/s}$, the average speed is NOT $ rac{10 + 5}{2} = 7.5 ext{ m/s}$! Because it spent twice as much time at $5 ext{ m/s}$ ($100 ext{ s}$ vs $50 ext{ s}$), the correct average speed is $ rac{1000 ext{ m}}{150 ext{ s}} = 6.67 ext{ m/s}$. Always use: $ ext{Total Distance} / ext{Total Time}$!
