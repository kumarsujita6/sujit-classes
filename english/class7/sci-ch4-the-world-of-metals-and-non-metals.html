1. The Five Defining Physical Properties of Metals

1. Malleability

Malleability is the physical property of a substance by which it can be beaten or hammered into thin sheets without cracking or breaking.

  • Metals are Malleable: Gold and silver are the most malleable metals in the world. They can be hammered into ultra-thin ornamental foils (like the silver vark on Indian sweets). Aluminium is hammered into lightweight foils used for wrapping fresh food and sandwiches.
  • Non-metals are NOT Malleable (Brittle): When a lump of coal, charcoal, or sulfur crystals is struck with a hammer, it does not flatten into a sheet; it immediately shatters into fine powder. Materials that break into pieces when hammered are called brittle.

2. Ductility

Ductility is the property of a substance by which it can be stretched and drawn into long, thin wires without snapping.

  • Metals are Ductile: Copper, aluminium, and iron can be drawn into very fine wires. A single gram of gold can be stretched into a wire nearly 2 kilometres long! The glowing filament inside bulbs is drawn from ultra-thin tungsten metal.
  • Non-metals are Non-ductile: You cannot draw coal, sulfur, or phosphorus into wires. Attempting to pull them snaps them instantly.

3. Sonority

Sonority is the property of producing a deep, ringing musical sound when struck against a hard surface.

  • Metals are Sonorous: Striking a metal temple bell or brass school gong sets off acoustic sound waves that ring for several seconds. Musical instrument strings (sitar, guitar, violin) are made of metal wires.
  • Non-metals are Non-sonorous: Dropping a piece of wood or a block of sulfur produces a dull, dead "thud" with no sustained ringing sound.

4. Conduction of Heat & Electricity

  • Thermal Conductivity: Metals are superb conductors of heat. That is why cooking pots and frying pans are made of iron, copper, and aluminium. Their handles are covered with wood or bakelite plastic (insulators) so we can hold them without burning our hands.
  • Electrical Conductivity: Metals have free electrons that drift easily under voltage. Electric wires are made of copper and aluminium. Non-metals (like rubber, wood, plastic) are electrical insulators.
  • The Famous Exception: Graphite (a form of the non-metal Carbon) is an excellent electrical conductor because of its unique hexagonal layered crystal lattice!

5. Lustre and Hardness (With Crucial Exceptions!)

  • Lustre: Freshly cut metal surfaces reflect light with a bright metallic shine (lustre). Non-metals are dull (exception: Iodine crystals and Diamond are lustrous).
  • Hardness: Most metals are hard solids with high tensile strength (iron, chromium).
  • Exam Exception #1 • Soft Metals: Sodium (Na) and Potassium (K) are so soft they can be sliced like butter with an ordinary kitchen knife!
  • Exam Exception #2 • Liquid Metal: Mercury (Hg) is the only metal that is a liquid at room temperature!

2. The Mystery of Rusting of Iron (Activity 4.5 Investigated)

Why do shiny new bicycle handles or outdoor iron gates turn reddish-brown and flaky after the monsoon rains? This process is called rusting (a form of corrosion).

Activity 4.5 Walkthrough • What Causes Rusting?
Three clean iron nails are placed into three separate glass bottles:
• Bottle (a) - Dry Air Only: Contains dry air and silica gel (which absorbs all moisture). Observation: NO RUST!
• Bottle (b) - Air-Free Water Only: Contains boiled distilled water (boiling drives out dissolved air) sealed beneath a layer of oil. Observation: NO RUST!
• Bottle (c) - Both Air and Water Present: Contains regular water exposed to air. Observation: HEAVY REDDISH-BROWN RUST!

Scientific Conclusion: Rusting requires BOTH Oxygen (from air) AND Moisture (water) simultaneously! If either is missing, iron cannot rust.

Figure 4.2: Scientific Proof • Rusting of iron requires both oxygen and moisture simultaneously.

How to Prevent Rusting of Iron:
1. Painting & Greasing: Applying a coat of oil, grease, or paint cuts off direct contact between iron, atmospheric oxygen, and moisture.
2. Galvanisation: Depositing a thin protective layer of metallic Zinc (Zn) over iron surfaces. Even if the zinc coating is scratched, zinc corrodes preferentially, shielding the underlying iron!

3. Chemical Reactions: Basic Oxides vs. Acidic Oxides

Reaction 1: Metals Form Basic Oxides (Magnesium Ribbon)

When a clean ribbon of magnesium metal is held over a burner flame, it ignites with a dazzling, brilliant white light. A white powdery ash called Magnesium Oxide (MgO) is formed:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Magnesium Oxide)

When this white ash is dissolved in warm water, it forms Magnesium Hydroxide [Mg(OH)₂]. Testing with litmus shows:
• Red litmus turns BLUE!
• Blue litmus remains blue.
Conclusion: Metal oxides are BASIC in nature!

Reaction 2: Non-metals Form Acidic Oxides (Sulfur Powder)

When yellow sulfur powder is heated in a deflagrating spoon, it burns with a pale blue flame producing choking Sulfur Dioxide gas (SO₂):

S + O₂ → SO₂ (Sulfur Dioxide)

When sulfur dioxide gas dissolves in water inside a closed jar, it forms Sulfurous Acid (H₂SO₃):

SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (Sulfurous Acid)

Testing with litmus shows:
• Blue litmus turns RED!
• Red litmus remains red.
Conclusion: Non-metal oxides are ACIDIC in nature!

4. Vital Non-metals: The Unsung Heroes of Life

Although metals build bridges, cars, and electrical lines, non-metals are the foundation of life itself:

Non-metal Why It Is Essential for Life Everyday Application
Oxygen (O) Essential for respiration in all aerobic organisms to release energy from digested food. Hospitals, scuba diving cylinders, steel manufacturing.
Carbon (C) The fundamental structural backbone of every biomolecule (DNA, proteins, carbohydrates, fats). Graphite in pencil cores, diamond drill bits, charcoal filters.
Nitrogen (N) Constituent of amino acids and proteins; plants require nitrogen for chlorophyll and leaf growth. Chemical fertilisers (urea, ammonium nitrate), crisp food packaging bags.
Chlorine (Cl) Powerful disinfectant that kills harmful waterborne disease-causing bacteria. Drinking water purification plants, swimming pools, bleaching.
Iodine (I) Synthesises thyroxine hormone in human thyroid gland; prevents goitre. Tincture of iodine (purple antiseptic lotion applied to wounds).

End-of-Chapter Exercises (Questions 1 to 12)

Question 1: Which metal is commonly used to make food packaging materials as it is cheaper, and its thin sheets can be folded easily? Exercise Q1

Correct Answer: (i) Aluminium

Reason: Aluminium has high malleability, allowing it to be rolled into micro-thin flexible foils. It is lightweight, non-toxic, resistant to corrosion, and much cheaper than gold or silver.

Question 2: Which of the following metals catches fire when it comes in contact with water? Exercise Q2

Correct Answer: (iv) Sodium

Reason: Sodium is an extremely reactive alkali metal. It reacts violently with cold water in an exothermic reaction, producing sodium hydroxide and liberating highly flammable hydrogen gas:
2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ ↑ + Heat
The heat produced instantly ignites the hydrogen gas, causing sodium to catch fire and burn with a golden-yellow flame!

Question 3: State with reason(s) whether the following statements are True [T] or False [F]. Exercise Q3
  • (i) Aluminium and copper are examples of non-metals used for making utensils and statues. [ FALSE ]
    Reason: Both aluminium and copper are metals, possessing high malleability, metallic lustre, and thermal conductivity.
  • (ii) Metals form oxides when combined with oxygen, the solution of which turns blue litmus paper to red. [ FALSE ]
    Reason: Metal oxides are basic in nature. Their aqueous solutions turn red litmus paper to blue, not blue to red!
  • (iii) Oxygen is a non-metal essential for respiration. [ TRUE ]
    Reason: Oxygen is a diatomic non-metal gas (O₂) that living cells require to oxidise food and release energy during cellular respiration.
  • (iv) Copper vessels are used for boiling water because they are good conductors of electricity. [ FALSE ]
    Reason: Copper vessels are used for cooking and heating because they are excellent conductors of heat (thermal conductors), not because of electrical conductivity!
Question 4: Why are only a few metals suitable for making jewellery? Exercise Q4

Answer:

Only noble metals like Gold (Au), Silver (Ag), and Platinum (Pt) are suitable for jewellery because of four unique qualities:

  1. High Metallic Lustre: They possess a permanent, captivating bright shine that reflects light beautifully.
  2. Extreme Chemical Inertness (Corrosion Resistance): Unlike iron, they do NOT react with atmospheric oxygen, moisture, carbon dioxide, or human sweat. Gold never rusts or tarnishes, keeping its sparkle for centuries.
  3. High Malleability and Ductility: They can be beaten into wafer-thin foils and drawn into microscopic filigree wires to craft intricate artistic ornaments.
  4. Biocompatibility: They do not irritate the skin or cause allergic rashes.

Question 6: What happens when oxygen reacts with magnesium and sulfur? What are the main differences in the nature of products formed? Exercise Q6

1. Reaction of Magnesium with Oxygen:

Magnesium metal burns with a dazzling white flame to form white Magnesium Oxide ash: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Dissolving this ash in water yields Magnesium Hydroxide: MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂. This turns red litmus to BLUE → It is BASIC.

2. Reaction of Sulfur with Oxygen:

Sulfur non-metal burns with a blue flame to form pungent Sulfur Dioxide gas: S + O₂ → SO₂. Dissolving this gas in water yields Sulfurous Acid: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃. This turns blue litmus to RED → It is ACIDIC.

Main Difference: Metal oxides are basic, while non-metal oxides are acidic!

Question 7: Complete the flowchart for Magnesium burning and testing. Exercise Q7

Completed Flowchart:

  • Starting Element: Magnesium Ribbon
  • Reagents: Air (Oxygen) + Heat
  • Product 1 (Ash): Magnesium Oxide (MgO)
  • Add: Water (H₂O) → forms Magnesium Hydroxide [Mg(OH)₂]
  • Test with Litmus:
    • Change in Red litmus solution: Turns BLUE.
    • Change in Blue litmus solution: No Change (remains blue).
  • Inference: The oxide is Basic in nature.
Question 8: Materials provided: Iron, copper, sulfur, coal, plastic, wood, cardboard. Discuss which material would be your choice to make a pan for boiling water and why? Exercise Q8

Best Choice: COPPER (or Iron/Aluminium)

Scientific Justification:

  1. High Thermal Conductivity: Copper is one of the best conductors of heat. It transfers heat rapidly and evenly from the stove burner to the water inside, boiling it quickly and saving fuel.
  2. High Melting Point: Copper melts at 1085°C, so it will never melt or deform on a domestic kitchen flame.
  3. Why other options are unusable:
    • Plastic, cardboard, and wood are thermal insulators and will melt or catch fire on the burner!
    • Coal and sulfur are brittle combustible non-metals that would crumble or catch fire!
Question 9: You are provided with three iron nails dipped in oil, water, and vinegar. Which iron nail will not rust, and why? Exercise Q9

Answer:

The iron nail dipped in OIL will NOT rust!

Scientific Justification:

  • Nail in Oil: Oil forms an impermeable protective coating around the iron surface that completely cuts off contact with dissolved oxygen and moisture. Since both oxygen and water are required for rusting, the nail in oil remains shiny and rust-free.
  • Nail in Water: Normal water contains dissolved atmospheric oxygen. Both water and oxygen are present, causing heavy reddish-brown rusting.
  • Nail in Vinegar: Vinegar contains acetic acid. Acid actively attacks and dissolves iron, reacting aggressively to produce hydrogen gas and iron salts, causing severe chemical corrosion!
Question 10: How do the different properties of metals and non-metals determine their uses in everyday life? Exercise Q10

Structure-Property Relationships:

  • Ductility & Conductivity → Copper Wires: Because copper is ductile and conducts electricity with minimal resistance, it is used in household electrical wiring.
  • Malleability → Aluminium Foil: Aluminium can be rolled into 0.01 mm thin sheets without tearing, making it ideal for packaging food hygienically.
  • Sonority → Brass Bells: Brass rings with sustained resonance, making it ideal for musical instruments and school bells.
  • Chemical Reactivity → Chlorine in Water Treatment: Chlorine's strong oxidising property destroys pathogenic microbial cell walls, sterilising drinking water.
  • Low Conductivity (Insulation) → Plastic Wire Covers: Plastic insulators prevent lethal electric shocks by sealing bare current-carrying wires.
Question 11: Iron is protected from rust by zinc coating. Since sulfur does not react with water, can it be used for this purpose? Justify your answer. Exercise Q11

Answer: NO, Sulfur CANNOT be used to protect iron from rusting.

Scientific Justification:

  1. Brittleness & Lack of Malleability: Sulfur is a brittle non-metal. It cannot be hammered, rolled, or plated into an even, continuous, adherent layer over iron. A sulfur coating would crack and peel off with minor vibration.
  2. Low Melting Point & Flammability: Sulfur melts at just 115°C and catches fire easily, releasing toxic sulfur dioxide gas.
  3. Why Zinc Works (Galvanisation): Zinc forms a tough metallic bond with iron. Even if scratched, zinc acts as a sacrificial anode because it is more reactive than iron, corroding first to protect the iron underneath!
Question 12: An ironsmith heats iron before making tools. Why is heating necessary in this process? Exercise Q12

Answer:

  1. Increased Malleability at High Temperatures: At room temperature, iron is extremely hard and rigid. Hammering cold iron requires enormous force and can cause the iron to crack.
  2. Thermal Softening of Metallic Crystals: When iron is heated red-hot in a blacksmith's furnace (around 800°C - 1000°C), its metallic lattice expands and softens. It becomes highly malleable and ductile, allowing the ironsmith to hammer and shape it into hoes, sickles, knives, and ploughshares effortlessly!

Q1. Which of the following is a non-metal that exists as an exceptional electrical conductor?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (c)
Graphite is an allotrope of the non-metal carbon. Each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds in planar hexagonal layers, leaving one free mobile electron per atom to conduct electricity.

Q2. What are the two mandatory conditions required simultaneously for iron to rust?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (b)
Activity 4.5 proves that iron will not rust in dry air alone (without water) or in boiled water alone (without dissolved oxygen). Both oxygen and moisture are strictly required.

Q3. When sulfur is burned in air and the resulting gas is dissolved in water, the solution will:

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (a)
Sulfur reacts with oxygen to produce sulfur dioxide (SO₂). When dissolved in water, it forms sulfurous acid (H₂SO₃). Acids turn blue litmus red.

Q4. Sodium metal is stored immersed under kerosene oil because:

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (c)
Sodium is an alkali metal that reacts vigorously with water and oxygen, generating explosive hydrogen gas and intense heat. Kerosene is a non-reactive hydrocarbon liquid that shields sodium completely.

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (a)
Dhokra is an ancient lost-wax metal casting art practiced by tribal communities across Odisha (Dhenkanal, Mayurbhanj, Rayagada) and Chhattisgarh for over 4,000 years!

Exam Hall Traps & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Bulletproof your preparation with common exam pitfalls and a rapid 2-minute memory checklist.

Exam Hall Trap #1 • Assuming All Metals are Hard and Solid:
Trap: Stating that "all metals are rigid, high-melting solids".
Correction: Mercury is a liquid metal at room temperature! Sodium and Potassium are so soft they can be sliced with a butter knife!
Exam Hall Trap #2 • Mixing Up the Acidity of Metal vs Non-metal Oxides:
Trap: Writing that magnesium oxide turns blue litmus red.
Correction: Metal oxides form bases with water (turns Red litmus Blue). Non-metal oxides (like SO₂, CO₂) form acids with water (turns Blue litmus Red)!

15 Lightning Revision Points for Quick Exam Recap

  1. Malleability: Property of metals to be beaten into thin sheets (e.g., gold, silver, aluminium).
  2. Ductility: Property of metals to be drawn into thin wires (e.g., copper, aluminium, tungsten).
  3. Sonority: Metals emit a characteristic ringing musical sound when struck against hard surfaces.
  4. Thermal & Electrical Conductors: Metals conduct heat and electricity freely; non-metals are insulators.
  5. Graphite Anomaly: The only common non-metal that conducts electricity due to free delocalised electrons.
  6. Soft Metals: Sodium and potassium are soft alkali metals that can be cut with a knife.
  7. Liquid Metal: Mercury (Hg) is the sole metallic element liquid at room temperature (used in thermometers).
  8. Rusting Conditions: Iron rusts only when BOTH oxygen (air) and water (moisture) are present simultaneously.
  9. Rust Formula: Hydrated iron(III) oxide: 2Fe₂O₃ • xH₂O (reddish-brown flaky deposit).
  10. Galvanisation: Depositing a protective sacrificial coating of zinc over iron to prevent rusting.
  11. Metal Oxides: Magnesium oxide (MgO) is basic; turns red litmus solution blue.
  12. Non-metal Oxides: Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) dissolves to form sulfurous acid (H₂SO₃); turns blue litmus red.
  13. Sodium Reactivity: Catches fire in water; must be safely stored submerged in kerosene.
  14. Vital Non-metals: Oxygen (respiration), Carbon (biomolecules), Nitrogen (fertilisers), Chlorine (disinfectant), Iodine (antiseptic).
  15. Indian Metal Heritage: Dhokra lost-wax craft (Odisha), Bidriware silver inlay (Karnataka), Pembarthi brass (Telangana).
