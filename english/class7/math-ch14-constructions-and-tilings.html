2.1 The Philosophy of Geometric Constructions & The "Eyes" Problem

In elementary school, students often draw geometric shapes freehand or by tracing physical stencils. However, in formal mathematics, freehand drawing relies purely on subjective human spatial estimation. If two people attempt to sketch a pair of human eyes freehand, the upper and lower eyelids will inevitably turn out asymmetrical, distorted, or inconsistent in curvature.

In Grade 6, we introduced the famous ‘Eyes’ Construction Problem. The goal was to draw a pair of realistic, symmetrical human eyes using geometric tools. To make both the upper eyelid and the lower eyelid perfectly symmetrical, we observed that:

  • The upper arc and the lower arc must have the exact same radius of curvature.
  • The two centers of curvature, say Point A (for the lower arc) and Point B (for the upper arc), must be located symmetrically on opposite sides of the eye.
  • The horizontal line connecting the inner corner X and the outer corner Y of the eye serves as a supporting line (or axis of symmetry). Although this supporting line may be erased or left faint in the final artwork, it is the mathematical spine of the entire construction!
Formal Definition: Supporting Lines and Supporting Arcs
A supporting line (or supporting arc) is a geometric guideline drawn with an unmarked straightedge or compass that does not form part of the final visible outline of a design, but provides the precise framework, centers, and axes of symmetry required to locate its vertices and boundary curves.

For the eye to possess bilateral symmetry across the supporting line XY, the distance from center A to both endpoints X and Y must be equal (i.e., AX = AY). Similarly, the distance from center B to both endpoints X and Y must be equal (i.e., BX = BY). Furthermore, for the upper and lower arcs to have the exact same radius, we must have:

AX = AY = BX = BY

This single geometric requirement immediately raises the central question of Euclidean construction: How do we systematically locate points A and B without guessing or trial-and-error? The answer lies in the geometry of the perpendicular bisector!

2.2 The Perpendicular Bisector: Definition, Congruence Proof, & The Locus Theorem

When we join the two centers A and B with a straight line, we make a remarkable visual discovery: line AB intersects the horizontal line segment XY at its exact center (midpoint) and forms perfect right angles (90°) with it!

Definition: Perpendicular Bisector
A perpendicular bisector of a given line segment is a line that divides the line segment into two equal halves (bisects it) and is perpendicular (at an angle of 90°) to it. If line AB is the perpendicular bisector of segment XY intersecting at point O, then:
(1) OX = OY (O is the midpoint of XY), and
(2) ∠AOX = ∠AOY = ∠BOX = ∠BOY = 90° (AB ⊥ XY).

Will the line connecting the two intersection points of equal arcs always be the perpendicular bisector, regardless of the length of segment XY or the radius chosen? In mathematics, observation alone is never sufficient; we must construct an airtight congruence proof.

Perpendicular Bisector Construction & Triangle Congruence Proof
Figure 2.1: Complete geometric proof diagram. Points A and B are constructed equidistant from X and Y (AX = AY = BX = BY = r). Triangle congruence shows △ABX ≅ △ABY (SSS), followed by △AOX ≅ △AOY (SAS), proving that OX = OY and ∠AOX = ∠AOY = 90°.
Step-by-Step Two-Stage Congruence Proof

Given: Line segment XY. Points A and B such that AX = AY and BX = BY. Line AB intersects XY at point O.

To Prove: (i) OX = OY (O is the midpoint of XY), and (ii) ∠AOX = ∠AOY = 90° (AB is perpendicular to XY).

Stage 1
Prove that the large outer triangles are congruent (SSS):
Consider △ABX and △ABY:
• AX = AY (radii of equal arcs from X and Y to A)
• BX = BY (radii of equal arcs from X and Y to B)
• AB = AB (common shared side)
Therefore, by the SSS (Side-Side-Side) Congruence Condition:
△ABX ≅ △ABY.
By Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles (CPCTC):
∠XAB = ∠YAB, which can be rewritten as ∠XAO = ∠YAO.
Stage 2
Prove that the small upper adjacent triangles are congruent (SAS):
Now consider △AOX and △AOY:
• AX = AY (given by equal radii)
• ∠XAO = ∠YAO (proved in Stage 1 above)
• AO = AO (common shared side)
Therefore, by the SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Congruence Condition:
△AOX ≅ △AOY.
Stage 3
Deduce Midpoint and 90° Right Angle (CPCTC):
From the congruence △AOX ≅ △AOY, CPCTC gives:
(1) OX = OY. Since OX equals OY, point O divides XY into two equal halves. Hence, O is the exact midpoint of segment XY!
(2) ∠AOX = ∠AOY. But angles ∠AOX and ∠AOY lie on the straight line XY and form a linear pair:
∠AOX + ∠AOY = 180°
Since they are equal, 2 × ∠AOX = 180° ⇒ ∠AOX = ∠AOY = 90°.
Conclusion: Line AB passes through the midpoint O and forms a 90° angle with XY. Hence, line AB is the unique perpendicular bisector of segment XY! (Q.E.D.)

The Equidistant Locus Theorem

The congruence proof established a profound geometric principle known in higher mathematics as the Perpendicular Bisector Locus Theorem:

The Equidistant Locus Theorem (Two-Way Equivalence)
Forward Statement: Any point that is equidistant (at the same distance) from two given points X and Y MUST lie on the perpendicular bisector of the line segment XY.

Converse Statement: Conversely, every single point that lies on the perpendicular bisector of segment XY is at the exact same distance from point X as it is from point Y.

This theorem explains why we can construct eyes of completely different shapes! If we choose any other pair of points C and D on the perpendicular bisector line AB such that CX = CY and DX = DY, we obtain an eye with different eyelid curvatures. Since segment XY has exactly one perpendicular bisector, every such valid center point C, D, E, ... must lie strictly along line AB!

2.3 Practical Construction of Perpendicular Bisector using Ruler & Compass

To construct the perpendicular bisector of any given line segment XY on paper:

Step 1
Set the Compass Radius: Place the sharp metal pointer of the compass on endpoint X. Open the compass pencil arm so that the radius is strictly greater than half the estimated length of segment XY (i.e., radius r > ½ XY).
Step 2
Strike Arcs from X: With center X and radius r, draw two sufficiently long circular arcs, one on the upper side of XY and one on the lower side of XY.
Step 3
Strike Arcs from Y with Same Radius: Without altering the compass opening, transfer the metal tip to endpoint Y. Draw another pair of arcs above and below XY, intersecting the first pair of arcs at points A and B, respectively.
Step 4
Draw the Bisector Line: Using an unmarked straightedge, draw a clean straight line through points A and B. Line AB is the required perpendicular bisector of segment XY.
Critical Radius Condition: Why r MUST be Strictly Greater than ½ XY
What happens if a student sets the compass radius to less than or equal to half the segment length?
• Case 1: Radius r < ½ XY: The distance between the centers X and Y is XY > 2r. The two circular arcs can never touch or intersect each other! There is a physical gap between them, making it impossible to locate points A and B.
• Case 2: Radius r = ½ XY: The two circular arcs will touch each other externally at exactly one single point (the midpoint O itself). Since a straight line requires at least two distinct points to be uniquely fixed in a plane, a single point cannot determine the line AB.
• Case 3: Radius r > ½ XY: The sum of radii (r + r = 2r) is strictly greater than the distance between centers (XY). By the Triangle Inequality and circle intersection theorem, the arcs intersect at exactly two distinct symmetric points A and B!
Accuracy Secret: Why Compass Bisecting Beats a Marked Ruler
If a line segment measures 7.35 cm, locating its midpoint by measuring with a standard 15-cm school ruler is extremely prone to human error:
1. Half of 7.35 cm is 3.675 cm, which does not exist on a millimeter ruler!
2. Parallax error: The angle of your eye shifts the reading.
3. Zero-mark wear: The plastic edges of rulers get blunt or chipped over time.
In contrast, the compass and straightedge construction is geometrically exact and immune to calibration errors!

2.4 Constructing a 90° Angle at a Given Point on a Line

Can we adapt the perpendicular bisector construction to build a 90° right angle at an arbitrary point O on an infinite line? Yes! The key insight is to artificially create a line segment for which point O is the exact midpoint.

Step 1
Create Artificial Segment XY Centered at O: Place the compass tip at the given point O. With any convenient radius r₁, draw arcs on both sides of O cutting the line at points X and Y. Because OX = OY = r₁, point O is automatically the midpoint of segment XY!
Step 2
Open Compass to Larger Radius: Expand the compass radius to r₂ > r₁ (greater than OX).
Step 3
Strike ONE Pair of Arcs Above the Line: With center X and radius r₂, draw an arc above the line. With center Y and the same radius r₂, draw an arc intersecting the previous arc at point A.
Step 4
Join OA: Draw ray OA using a straightedge. Since A lies on the perpendicular bisector of XY and O is the midpoint, line OA is the perpendicular bisector itself! Therefore, ∠AOX = ∠AOY = 90°.
Optimization Insight: Why Only ONE Pair of Arcs is Needed
Notice that when bisecting a line segment XY, we drew two pairs of intersecting arcs (point A above and point B below). But when constructing a 90° angle at point O on a line, we only need to draw one pair of arcs (point A above)!
Why? Because point O is already given on the line, and by construction OX = OY, so point O is already guaranteed to be a point on the perpendicular bisector! To define a straight line, Euclid proved that exactly two points are needed. Since O is point 1 and A is point 2, line OA is completely determined without needing a bottom point B!

2.5 Historical Masterclass: Vedic Indian Geometry in the Śulba-Sūtras

Long before compasses made of brass or steel were invented, ancient Indian mathematicians developed extraordinarily accurate geometric construction methods using the simplest tool imaginable: a rope (called śulba or rajju in Sanskrit).

These methods are preserved in the Śulba-Sūtras (composed between 800 BCE and 500 BCE by master mathematical seers including Baudhāyana, Āpastamba, Kātyāyana, and Mānava). The Śulba-Sūtras form part of the Kalpa Vedāṅga (one of the six Vedāṅgas, which literally means "limbs of the Vedas"). They served a critical religious and mathematical purpose: the design and construction of complex sacred fire altars (Yajna Vedis, such as the falcon-shaped Śyenaciti, the circular Gārhapatya, and the square Āhavanīya). Each altar had to have an exact mathematical surface area and strictly aligned cardinal orientations (North-South and East-West).

Kātyāyana-Śulbasūtra (1.2): Sacred Fire-Altar Rope Perpendicular Bisector
Figure 2.2: Historical construction of the North-South cardinal meridian (perpendicular bisector) from the Kātyāyana-Śulbasūtra (1.2). Two wooden pegs (śaṅku) are fixed at points X and Y. A rope with terminal loops is folded in half to mark its exact center. When the loop ends are hitched to the pegs and the midpoint is pulled taut North (A) and South (B), line AB establishes the exact perpendicular bisector.
Kātyāyana-Śulbasūtra 1.2: Original Sanskrit Sutra & Translation

"प्रक्रम्यान्ताभ्यां रज्ज्वा मध्यं नियम्य दक्षिणत उत्तरतश्चापकर्षेत्। सा दिक् समविभक्ता भवति॥"

Translation: Having fastened the two ends of a rope to two fixed poles (pegs) at the endpoints of a line segment, hold the midpoint of the rope and pull it taut to the South and then to the North. The line joining these two positions divides the given segment equally and sets the cardinal direction at right angles (perpendicular bisector).

Other Vedic Rope Methods for Constructing a 90° Angle

The Śulba-Sūtras describe multiple brilliant methods to construct a 90° angle in the field using ropes:

  • The 3-4-5 Pythagorean Knot Method (Baudhāyana Theorem): A rope is tied with knots dividing it into 12 equal units (proportions 3, 4, and 5). One peg is fixed at the corner where the 90° angle is required. The 3-unit segment is stretched along the base, the 4-unit segment is pulled perpendicular, and the 5-unit segment forms the hypotenuse. Since 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5², the angle between the 3-unit and 4-unit sides is guaranteed to be exactly 90°!
  • The Semicircle / Thales Rope Theorem: In the Āpastamba Śulba-Sūtra, a rope pinned at its center is swung to form a semicircle. A triangle inscribed in a semicircle with the diameter as its hypotenuse always subtends a right angle (90°) at any point on the arc!

2.6 Angle Bisection: Definition, Construction, SSS Proof, & 45°/22.5° Angles

Just as a line segment can be bisected into two equal halves, an angle can also be bisected into two equal adjacent angles.

Definition: Angle Bisector
An angle bisector is a ray that originates from the vertex of an angle and divides the angle into two equal parts (angles of equal measure). If ray OC bisects ∠AOB, then ∠AOC = ∠BOC = ½ ∠AOB.
The Angle Construction Fan: 90°, 60°, 45°, 30°, and 15° Derived via Bisection
Figure 2.3: The Euclidean Angle Family Tree. Starting with a straight line, we construct 90° (perpendicular) and 60° (equilateral triangle arc). Through repeated angle bisections, we unlock 45°, 22.5°, 30°, 15°, and 7.5° angles!
Step-by-Step Construction & SSS Congruence Proof for Angle Bisection

Given: Any angle ∠XOY with vertex O.

Step 1
Mark Equidistant Points A and B on the Arms: Place the compass tip at vertex O. With any radius, draw an arc intersecting ray OX at point A and ray OY at point B. Thus, OA = OB.
Step 2
Strike Intersecting Arcs from A and B: With compass center A and any fixed radius r (greater than ½ AB), draw an arc in the interior of the angle. With center B and the exact same radius r, draw another arc intersecting the first arc at point C. Thus, AC = BC.
Step 3
Draw the Bisector Ray OC: Join vertex O to intersection point C with a straightedge.
Proof
Airtight SSS Congruence Proof:
Join AC and BC. Now consider triangles △OAC and △OBC:
• OA = OB (radii of the same initial arc from vertex O)
• AC = BC (radii of the equal intersecting arcs from A and B)
• OC = OC (common shared side)
Therefore, by the SSS Congruence Criterion:
△OAC ≅ △OBC.
By Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles (CPCTC):
∠AOC = ∠BOC.
Hence, ray OC bisects ∠AOB into two identical halves! (Q.E.D.)

2.7 Repeating Units & Copying an Angle (Compass Arc Transfer)

In decorative art, textile patterns, architectural friezes, and engineering gear systems, complex designs often consist of a single repeating unit replicated at fixed orientations. To make exact copies of such a unit, every replica must have:

  1. Identical arm lengths (which can easily be transferred using a compass).
  2. The exact same angle between the arms.

How can we make an exact copy of a given angle ∠A onto a new ray starting at vertex X without measuring with a protractor? We utilize the principle of constructing congruent isosceles triangles (SSS).

Step 1
Form Reference Triangle at Original Angle: With the original vertex A as center and any convenient radius r, draw an arc intersecting the two arms of angle A at points B and C. This forms an isosceles triangle △ABC with AB = AC = r.
Step 2
Transfer Base Arc to New Vertex X: With the target vertex X as center and the exact same radius r, draw a long arc cutting the target base ray at point Z. Thus, XZ = r = AB.
Step 3
Measure and Transfer Chord Length BC: Place the compass metal point at C and adjust the pencil tip precisely to point B to measure the straight-line chord distance BC. Without altering this compass opening, place the metal tip at point Z and draw an arc cutting the previous arc at point Y. Thus, YZ = BC.
Step 4
Draw Target Ray XY: Join vertex X to intersection point Y with a straightedge.
Justification
SSS Congruence Justification:
In △ABC and △XYZ:
• AB = XY = r (by initial arc radius)
• AC = XZ = r (by initial arc radius)
• BC = YZ (by compass chord transfer)
Therefore, by the SSS Congruence Condition, △ABC ≅ △XYZ.
By CPCTC, ∠X = ∠A! The angle has been perfectly duplicated without a protractor!

2.8 Construction of a Line Parallel to a Given Line Through an External Point

In earlier grades, students constructed parallel lines using a ruler and a sliding set square. In Euclidean geometry, we achieve this using only an unmarked ruler and compass by translating the theorem of Corresponding Angles or Alternate Interior Angles.

The Parallel Lines Transversal Theorem
If a transversal line intersects two lines such that a pair of corresponding angles (F-shape) or a pair of alternate interior angles (Z-shape) are equal, then the two lines are strictly parallel!

2.9 Architectural Masterworks: Gothic Trefoil Arches & Mughal Pointed Arches

Geometric construction is not merely a classroom exercise; it is the mathematical backbone of world heritage architecture! Two iconic arches studied in the NCERT curriculum are the Trefoil Arch and the Pointed Arch.

Architectural Geometry: Gothic Trefoil Arch vs. Mughal Pointed Arch
Figure 2.5: Architectural geometry of arches. Left: Trefoil arch with three circular foils constructed on supporting lines with bilateral symmetry (AB = CD and ∠BAD = ∠CDA). Right: Pointed arch (equilateral gothic arch) constructed from two circular arcs centered at opposite endpoints P and Q meeting sharply at the apex.

Why did medieval and Mughal architects prefer pointed arches over rounded semicircular arches?
From an engineering perspective, a semicircular Roman arch pushes forces horizontally outward, requiring immense stone buttresses to prevent collapse. A pointed arch directs the gravitational thrust much more steeply downward into the supporting columns. This structural triumph allowed architects to build higher ceilings, wider open halls (such as the Diwan-i-Aam at the Red Fort in Delhi and Agra), and integrate large stained-glass windows!

2.10 Regular Polygons, Angles Around a Point, & 60° Angle Construction

A regular polygon is a closed two-dimensional figure that is both:

  • Equilateral: All sides have the exact same length.
  • Equiangular: All interior angles have the exact same measure.

A regular polygon with 3 sides is an equilateral triangle (all angles = 60°). A regular polygon with 4 sides is a square (all angles = 90°). A regular polygon with 6 sides is a regular hexagon (all sides equal, all angles = 120°).

Constructing a 60° Angle via an Equilateral Triangle

How do we construct a 60° angle using only an unmarked ruler and compass? We exploit the fact that every angle in an equilateral triangle is 60°:

Step 1
Draw a base ray AX. Place the compass tip at vertex A, open it to any radius r, and draw a large arc intersecting ray AX at point B. Thus, AB = r.
Step 2
With the exact same radius r, place the compass tip at point B and strike another arc cutting the first arc at point C. Thus, BC = r and AC = r.
Step 3
Join ray AC. Since AB = BC = AC = r, △ABC is an equilateral triangle! Therefore, ∠CAB = 60°!
Why 6 Equilateral Triangles Form a Regular Hexagon: The 360° Complete Angle Law
Figure 2.6: Dissection of a regular hexagon into 6 congruent equilateral triangles. Since the sum of angles around the central point O is 360°, exactly six 60° angles fit tightly without overlapping or leaving any gaps (6 × 60° = 360°). Furthermore, three adjacent 60° angles form 180° (a straight angle), proving that diagonals AOD, BOE, and COF are straight line segments!

The Circumscribed Circle Stepping-Off Method

Figure 2.6 reveals a wonderful shortcut for constructing a regular hexagon of any given side length s (e.g., s = 4 cm or s = 5 cm):

Step 1
Draw a circle with radius equal to the required side length: R = s (e.g., 4 cm).
Step 2
Pick any starting point A on the circumference. Without changing the compass radius s, step off consecutive chord marks around the circle: from A mark B, from B mark C, from C mark D, from D mark E, and from E mark F.
Step 3
The 6th step lands exactly back on starting point A! Join the consecutive vertices AB, BC, CD, DE, EF, FA with a straightedge. You have constructed a mathematically perfect regular hexagon!

2.11 Rotational Symmetry, 6-Pointed Stars, & Compass-Only Constructions

By extending the regular hexagon construction, we can create intricate ornamental designs possessing rotational symmetry:

  • The 6-Pointed Star (Hexagram): Formed by constructing an outward-pointing equilateral triangle on each of the 6 edges of a central regular hexagon (or by overlapping two large congruent equilateral triangles rotated by 180° with respect to each other). The resulting star has rotational symmetry of order 6 (rotating it by 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°, or 360° maps the figure perfectly onto itself). Every one of the 6 points is a congruent equilateral triangle with angles 60°, 60°, and 60°!
  • Inflexed Arcs (S-Curves / Ogee Arcs): An inflexed arc is a continuous compound curve formed by joining two or more circular arcs that curve in opposite directions (one convex, one concave), sharing a common tangent at their junction point. These are widely used in traditional Indian temple cornices and Islamic floral borders.
  • The 6-Petalled Rosette ("Flower of Life" - Compass-Only Construction): Draw a circle with a compass. Keeping the compass set to the exact same radius R, place the metal pointer on any point on the circumference and draw an arc passing through the center. Shift the pointer to each successive intersection on the circumference and draw matching arcs. Without using a straightedge even once, you create a breathtaking 6-petalled flower! (In 1797, Italian geometer Lorenzo Mascheroni proved the famous Mohr-Mascheroni Theorem: every single geometric construction that can be performed with a straightedge and compass can theoretically be performed using compasses alone!).

2.12 Dropping a Perpendicular from an External Point P to a Line l

In Section 2.4, we constructed a perpendicular at a point on a line. But what if point P lies outside (external to) line l?

Step 1
Strike Arc from External Point P: Place the compass pointer at the external point P. Open the compass wide enough so that its radius is strictly greater than the perpendicular distance to line l. Draw a wide arc that cuts line l at two distinct points, X and Y.
Step 2
Identify Equidistant Segment: By construction, PX = PY (they are radii of the same circle). Therefore, by the Locus Theorem, point P lies on the perpendicular bisector of the intercepted segment XY!
Step 3
Find Second Bisector Point Q: With centers X and Y and any convenient radius r > ½ XY, draw two intersecting arcs on the opposite side of line l (away from P) meeting at point Q.
Step 4
Join PQ: Draw line PQ using a straightedge. Line PQ is the perpendicular bisector of XY, passes directly through external point P, and is strictly perpendicular to line l (PQ ⊥ l).

2.13 Introduction to Tilings (Tessellations) & The Ancient Chinese Tangram

We now cross into the second magnificent pillar of this chapter: Tilings (Tessellations).

Formal Definition: Tiling (Tessellation)
Covering a geometric region or an infinite two-dimensional plane using a collection of shapes such that:
(1) There are NO GAPS between adjacent tiles, and
(2) There are NO OVERLAPS between any tiles.
The boundary of each tile touches its neighbors along common edges or vertices.

The earliest and most celebrated introduction to planar dissection and tiling is the Tangram puzzle, which originated in ancient China during the Song Dynasty (and later fascinated Western thinkers like Lewis Carroll and Napoleon Bonaparte).

2.14 Grid Tilings & Domino Polyominoes (2×1 Tiles)

Let us now investigate tiling a grid of unit squares using multiple copies of a rectangular 2×1 domino tile (which can be used horizontally or vertically).

The Area Parity Condition (Necessary Condition)

Each 2×1 domino tile covers exactly 2 unit squares. Therefore, if a grid can be completely covered by k domino tiles without gaps or overlaps:

Total Area = 2 × k = ALWAYS AN EVEN NUMBER!
The Odd Area Impossibility Rule
Any grid or region whose total number of unit squares is ODD can NEVER be tiled using 2×1 domino tiles!
• Example: A 5×7 grid contains 5 × 7 = 35 unit squares. Since 35 is odd, no matter how clever you are, you can never tile it with 2×1 tiles (17 tiles would cover 34 squares, leaving 1 lonely square exposed!).

2.15 The Checkerboard Parity Proof (Black & White Invariance)

We now arrive at one of the most stunning, mind-bending conceptual discoveries in the entire NCERT Class 7 curriculum.

Students often ask: "If a region has an EVEN number of squares, is it guaranteed to be tileable with 2×1 dominoes?"
ANSWER: ABSOLUTELY NOT! Having an even area is a necessary condition, but it is NOT a sufficient condition!

Consider a 5×3 grid. It has 5 × 3 = 15 unit squares. Suppose we remove one unit square (as shown in textbook Figure 6.13). The remaining region has:

15 - 1 = 14 Unit Squares (An EVEN Number!)

Yet, when students try to tile this 14-square region with seven 2×1 tiles, they find it completely impossible! How can we be 100% mathematically sure that no tiling exists, without spending days testing millions of combinations?

The secret was revealed by introducing a classic coloring technique: The Checkerboard (Black & White) Parity Argument!

The Checkerboard Parity Proof: Why the Defective 14-Square Region Cannot be Tiled
The Parity Contradiction Proof 1. Every 2×1 domino covers exactly 1 White + 1 Black. 2. Therefore, 7 dominoes MUST cover: Exactly 7 White AND Exactly 7 Black! 3. But our grid has 8 White and 6 Black! 8 ≠ 7 and 6 ≠ 7 ⇒ IMPOSSIBLE TO TILE! Zero trial-and-error needed. Mathematically impossible!

2.16 Tiling the Entire Infinite Plane: Regular Monohedral Tessellations

So far, we have tiled bounded rectangular regions. What if we want to tile the entire infinite two-dimensional plane using identical copies of a single regular polygon? This is called a Regular Monohedral Tessellation.

The Vertex Angle Condition

For identical regular polygons with interior angle θ to tile a flat plane without overlapping or leaving any gaps, the polygons meeting around every single shared vertex must sum up to a complete angle of 360°!

k × θ = 360°   (where k is a positive whole integer ≥ 3)

The interior angle of an n-sided regular polygon is given by the formula:
θ = (n - 2) × 180° ÷ n.

The Grand Tessellation Law of the Euclidean Plane
There are ONLY THREE regular polygons in existence that can tile the flat plane by themselves:
(1) Equilateral Triangles (6 around each vertex: 6 × 60° = 360°)
(2) Squares (4 around each vertex: 4 × 90° = 360°)
(3) Regular Hexagons (3 around each vertex: 3 × 120° = 360°)
No other regular polygon can tile the two-dimensional plane!

PYQ 5 (Probable Exam Question): Construction of a regular hexagon inscribed in a circle

Question: Construct a regular hexagon of side 4.5 cm. Explain why the side length of an inscribed regular hexagon is equal to the radius of its circumscribing circle.

Solution:

  • When the center of a regular hexagon is connected to its 6 vertices, it is divided into 6 congruent triangles.
  • The central angle for each triangle is 360° ÷ 6 = 60°.
  • Since the two radii from the center to any two consecutive vertices are equal (OA = OB = R), the base angles are also equal: (180° - 60°) ÷ 2 = 60°.
  • All three angles of △OAB are 60°, so △OAB is an equilateral triangle!
  • Therefore, side length AB = radius OA = R = 4.5 cm.
  • Construction: Draw circle of radius 4.5 cm, step off 6 chords of 4.5 cm around circumference, and join consecutive vertices.

5 Common Student Blunders & Exam Hall Traps

Trap 1: Setting Compass Radius r ≤ ½ Segment Length
The Blunder: When asked to construct the perpendicular bisector of a 6 cm line segment, a student sets the compass to 2.5 cm or 3 cm.
Why Marks are Lost: If r < 3 cm, the arcs never meet (gap remains). If r = 3 cm, arcs touch at only one point (the midpoint), which is insufficient to draw a unique line!
Exam Fix: Always open compass to visibly MORE THAN HALF the segment length (e.g., for 6 cm, use 4 cm or 4.5 cm).
Trap 2: Erasing Faint Construction Arcs and Supporting Lines
The Blunder: After completing the figure, students erase the construction arcs to make the drawing look "neat and clean".
Why Marks are Lost: In CBSE and State Board evaluation schemes, 60% of marks are awarded for the visible construction arcs! If arcs are erased, examiners assume the student used a protractor or traced the figure, awarding ZERO marks!
Exam Fix: Never erase construction arcs! Keep supporting lines thin, sharp, and permanently visible. Only darken the final required shape.
Trap 3: The "Even Area Means Tileable" Fallacy
The Blunder: When given a mutilated grid (e.g., 5×3 grid with 1 square removed = 14 squares), students write: "Since 14 is even, it is tileable by 7 dominoes."
Why Marks are Lost: Total even area is only a necessary condition, NOT a sufficient condition! Color parity must be verified.
Exam Fix: Always apply the checkerboard test! Count White squares and Black squares. If White ≠ Black, it can NEVER be tiled.
Trap 4: Confusing Angle Bisection with Side Bisection
The Blunder: In an angle bisection question, students place compass tips at the ends of the drawn rays instead of swinging an initial arc of equal radius from the vertex O.
Why Marks are Lost: If OA ≠ OB on the arms of the angle, the subsequent arcs will produce a ray that does NOT bisect the angle!
Exam Fix: Always execute Step 1: Swing an arc centered at vertex O to establish points A and B such that OA = OB!
Trap 5: Thinking Regular Pentagons Tile the Plane
The Blunder: Writing that regular pentagons can tile a flat plane because they are regular polygons.
Why Marks are Lost: Each interior angle of a regular pentagon is 108°. 360° ÷ 108° = 3.333..., which is not an integer! Three pentagons leave a 36° gap, and four overlap.
Exam Fix: Memorize the Big Three: ONLY Equilateral Triangles, Squares, and Regular Hexagons can tile the Euclidean plane regularly!
