Chapter 5: The Rise of Empires

From the rise of Magadha and Alexander's invasion to Chandragupta Maurya, Kautilya's Arthashastra, and Emperor Ashoka's revolutionary path of Dhamma and peace.

Reading Time: 35 mins Full NCERT Solutions Ashoka's Edicts & Pillars

Visual Mind Map & Chapter Overview

Imagine travelling back in time over 2,300 years to the magnificent city of Pataliputra (modern Patna), the capital of the Maurya Empire. Surrounded by a deep moat 600 feet wide and a colossal timber wall with 570 watchtowers and 64 decorated gates, it was larger and wealthier than the imperial capitals of ancient Persia or Rome!

What made an empire different from an ordinary kingdom? How did a small state in Bihar expand into the first pan-Indian empire stretching from Afghanistan all the way to Karnataka and Odisha? And why did Emperor Ashoka, at the height of military victory, voluntarily renounce warfare to rule by righteousness and compassion? Let us explore this fascinating era.

The Big Questions of Chapter 5

1. What is an empire, and how did it differ from earlier kingdoms? (Empires spanned vast subcontinental areas, ruled millions of diverse peoples, maintained huge standing armies, and relied on structured civil bureaucracies).

2. Who were the architects of the Maurya Empire? (Chandragupta Maurya and his strategist mentor Kautilya/Chanakya).

3. Why is Emperor Ashoka unique in human history? (He renounced military conquest after the bloody Kalinga War to govern through Dhamma, ethical tolerance, and universal welfare).

02

Exhaustive Concept Explanations

1. What Makes an Empire Different from a Kingdom?

While all empires are kingdoms, not all kingdoms are empires! As states expanded, their nature changed dramatically:

Feature A Kingdom (e.g., Kosala or Vatsa) An Empire (e.g., The Maurya Empire)
Size of Territory Moderate, local geographical region (e.g., a river basin or district). Enormous subcontinental landmass comprising dozens of conquered former kingdoms and provinces.
Population & Diversity Fairly uniform culture, single predominant dialect, shared traditions. Extremely diverse: hundreds of tribes, distinct languages, religious sects, and customs living under one imperial crown.
Army & Resources Small royal guard supported by temporary feudal levies during war. Colossal permanent standing army (hundreds of thousands of infantry, war elephants, cavalry, and navy) requiring vast revenues.
Bureaucracy & Taxes Simple collection of Bhaga by village headmen. Elaborate imperial civil service with superintendents overseeing mines, forests, ports, roads, espionage, and mints.

2. The Rise of Magadha & The Arrival of the Greeks

Among the 16 Mahājanapadas, Magadha rose to become the undisputed superpower of ancient India. Why?

  • Iron Monopoly: Rich iron ore deposits in Rajgir and Chota Nagpur enabled Magadha to forge superior, razor-sharp weapons and armor.
  • War Elephants: Dense monsoon forests yielded thousands of wild elephants. Magadhan generals were the first to deploy giant armored elephant corps to smash enemy cavalry and batter down timber city gates.
  • Strategic Water Fortress: Its first capital, Rajagriha, was protected by five natural rocky hills. Its second capital, Pataliputra, sat at the confluence of four major rivers (Ganga, Son, Gandak, Ghaghara), forming an impregnable water-fort (Jaladurga).

The Nanda Dynasty & Alexander the Great (326 BCE):

By the 4th century BCE, the powerful Nanda Dynasty (founded by Mahapadma Nanda) ruled Magadha from Pataliputra. Their last ruler, Dhana Nanda, amassed unimaginable wealth and an army of 200,000 foot soldiers, 20,000 cavalry, 2,000 war chariots, and 3,000 to 6,000 trained war elephants!

In 326 BCE, the young Macedonian conqueror Alexander the Great swept through Persia and entered northwest India (Punjab). After fighting a ferocious, bloody battle against the valiant Indian King Porus (Puru) at the River Hydaspes (Jhelum), Alexander wanted to march forward to conquer the Ganga valley.

Why Alexander's Soldiers Mutinied at the River Beas

At the River Hyphasis (Beas), Alexander's battle-hardened Greek troops dropped their weapons and flatly refused to take another step east!

Greek historians record that scouts brought terrified reports of the colossal Nanda army waiting on the other side of the Ganga with thousands of roaring war elephants. Exhausted, homesick, and terrified of encountering Magadha's elephant legions, Alexander was forced to turn back! He died soon after in Babylon in 323 BCE.

3. Chandragupta Maurya and Chanakya's Master Plan

Dhana Nanda was a greedy, arrogant king who imposed crushing taxes on his people. According to historical tradition, when a brilliant scholar from Taxila University named Kautilya (Chanakya / Vishnugupta) warned Dhana Nanda to mend his ways, the arrogant king publicly insulted him and threw him out of the royal court!

Chanakya untied his sacred hair-knot (Shikha) and vowed never to tie it again until he had uprooted the Nanda dynasty! He found a brave, charismatic young warrior named Chandragupta Maurya, trained him in statecraft and warfare, raised an army, and overthrew Dhana Nanda around 321 BCE, establishing the Maurya Empire.

Victory over Seleucus Nicator (305 BCE):

Alexander's general, Seleucus Nicator, who inherited the eastern Greek empire, invaded India to reclaim Alexander's conquests. Chandragupta decisively defeated him! In the peace treaty of 305 BCE:

  • Seleucus surrendered the territories of eastern Afghanistan, Baluchistan, and Gandhara (Kabul, Kandahar, Herat).
  • Chandragupta gifted Seleucus 500 war elephants.
  • Seleucus sent a Greek ambassador named Megasthenes to live at the royal court of Pataliputra.
Megasthenes' Description of Pataliputra

Megasthenes wrote an invaluable eyewitness account titled Indica:

  • The Imperial Capital: Pataliputra was 14.5 km long and 2.5 km wide, built along the Ganga. The royal palace was made of carved timber with gilded pillars adorned with silver vines and golden birds.
  • Honesty of the People: Megasthenes marveled that theft was exceedingly rare; citizens rarely locked their doors, and written contracts were unnecessary because people kept their word.
  • City Administration: The city was governed by six specialized municipal committees of five members each (overseeing crafts, foreigners, births/deaths, weights/measures, market sales, and tax collection).

4. Kautilya's Arthashastra: Blueprint of Statecraft

Kautilya authored the Arthaśhāstra, one of the world's greatest classical treatises on political science, economics, law, and diplomacy.

  • Famous Maxim on the King's Duty:
    "In the happiness of his subjects lies the king's happiness; in their welfare, his welfare. Whatever pleases himself the king shall not consider as good, but whatever pleases his subjects he shall consider as good."
  • Saptānga Theory: Kautilya viewed the state as an organic living body with seven vital limbs (Swami - ruler, Amatya - ministers, Janapada - territory & farmers, Durga - fortresses, Kosa - treasury, Danda - army, Mitra - allies). If any limb became sick or corrupt, the state could collapse!
  • Intelligence & Espionage: Recommended a vast network of secret agents (spies disguised as ascetics, merchants, and bards) to detect corruption among officials and prevent foreign invasions.

5. Emperor Ashoka: The King Who Chose Peace

Chandragupta was succeeded by his son Bindusara, who expanded the empire southward into the Deccan. Around 268 BCE, Bindusara's son Ashoka ascended the imperial throne.

The Turning Point: The Kalinga War (261 BCE)

The powerful, prosperous maritime kingdom of Kalinga (modern Odisha) guarded the eastern trade routes to Southeast Asia. Kalinga had fiercely resisted Mauryan expansion. In the 8th year of his reign (261 BCE), Ashoka led a massive army into Kalinga.

A savage, cataclysmic war was fought along the banks of the Daya River (near Dhauli, Bhubaneswar). Although Ashoka won, the human tragedy shook him to his very core:

  • Over 100,000 soldiers and civilians were killed.
  • Over 150,000 people were captured as prisoners of war.
  • Hundreds of thousands perished later from starvation, wounds, and disease.
  • The waters of the Daya River literally turned red with human blood!
Ashoka's Remorse Recorded in Rock Edict XIII

In Major Rock Edict XIII, Ashoka wrote with heartbreaking honesty:

"Directly after the conquest of Kalinga, the Beloved of the Gods felt a deep remorse. For having conquered an unconquered country, the slaughter, death, and carrying away of people that occurs there is considered extremely painful and serious by the Beloved of the Gods... Even if one-hundredth or one-thousandth part of those who died in Kalinga were to suffer today, it would weigh heavily on his mind."

From Bherighosha to Dhammaghosha

Ashoka made a momentous decision unprecedented in the entire history of imperial monarchs: he completely renounced military conquest!

  • He replaced Bherighosha (the war-drum sound of military victory) with Dhammaghosha (the call of moral righteousness and peace).
  • Under the guidance of the Buddhist monk Upagupta, Ashoka embraced Buddhism.

6. What was Ashoka's Dhamma?

Ashoka's Dhamma (the Prakrit form of the Sanskrit Dharma) was not a dogmatic religion. It had no complex rituals, temple pujas, or animal sacrifices. It was an enlightened universal moral and civic code designed to unite a diverse subcontinental empire:

  1. Ahimsa (Non-violence): Total prohibition of animal sacrifices; reduction of meat consumption in the royal kitchen; banning the slaughter of pregnant animals.
  2. Respect for Elders: Absolute obedience and devotion to parents, teachers, and elders.
  3. Kindness to Subordinates: Gentle, humane treatment of servants, laborers, and domestic slaves.
  4. Religious Tolerance: Equal respect for all philosophical sects—Brahmanas, Buddhist monks, Jains, and Ajivikas. Ashoka proclaimed: "He who honors his own sect while condemning other sects does the greatest harm to his own sect!"
  5. Public Welfare Works: Planting shady banyan trees and mango groves along highways, digging wells every half league (kos), and building free hospitals for both humans and animals.

Dhamma Mahamattas: Ashoka created a new cadre of high officials called Dhamma Mahamattas whose sole job was to travel across villages, teach ethical living, settle communal disputes, and ensure prisoners were treated fairly.

7. Inscriptions, Pillar Architecture & India's National Emblem

Ashoka was ancient India's greatest royal communicator. He inscribed his imperial messages on massive rock surfaces, cave walls, and polished monolithic sandstone pillars across India, Nepal, Pakistan, and Afghanistan:

  • Scripts of the People: Rather than using difficult classical Sanskrit, Ashoka wrote in everyday vernacular Prakrit using the Brahmi script (the parent ancestor of almost all modern Indian scripts including Devanagari, Odia, Bengali, Tamil, and Telugu!). In the northwest, he used Kharosthi, and in Afghanistan, he used Greek and Aramaic so local people could read his words.
  • Ashoka's Titles: He addressed his citizens not with boastful imperial titles, but affectionately as Devanampiya ("Beloved of the Gods") and Piyadasi ("He who looks upon all people with affection").
  • The Sarnath Lion Capital: Erected at Sarnath (where Buddha preached his first sermon). Carved out of a single block of polished Chunar sandstone, it features four majestic Asiatic lions standing back-to-back on an abacus, representing courage, power, pride, and righteousness. Below the lions is the Ashoka Chakra (a 24-spoke wheel representing the Wheel of Law / Dharma) flanked by a horse, bull, lion, and elephant.
  • India's National Emblem: On 26 January 1950, free India adopted the Sarnath Lion Capital as our official National Emblem, along with the sacred Vedic motto from the Mundaka Upanishad: "Satyameva Jayate" (Truth alone triumphs). The 24-spoke Ashoka Chakra was placed proudly at the center of India's National Tricolor Flag!

Rapid Revision Fact & Technical Term Bank

1. Empire
A vast territory incorporating multiple former kingdoms and diverse linguistic/cultural populations, ruled by an Emperor through a centralized administration.
2. Pataliputra
The magnificent imperial capital of the Mauryan Empire situated at the confluence of the Ganga and Son rivers (modern-day Patna, Bihar).
3. Arthaśhāstra
A classical Sanskrit masterwork on statecraft, economic policy, legal justice, and military strategy authored by Kautilya (Chanakya).
4. Saptānga
Kautilya's Seven Limbs of the Sovereign State: King (Swami), Ministers (Amatya), Territory (Janapada), Forts (Durga), Treasury (Kosa), Army (Danda), and Allies (Mitra).
5. Indica
An eyewitness account of Mauryan India and Pataliputra written by the Greek ambassador Megasthenes, who lived at Chandragupta Maurya's court.
6. Kalinga War (261 BCE)
A bloody battle fought by Ashoka in modern Odisha whose horrific loss of human life inspired him to renounce warfare and adopt Dhamma.
7. Ashoka's Dhamma
A universal ethical code of conduct emphasizing Ahimsa, truthfulness, religious tolerance, respect for parents, and kindness to servants.
8. Dhamma Mahamattas
Special welfare officials appointed by Ashoka to travel across the empire spreading moral teachings, settling disputes, and ensuring fair justice.
9. Brahmi Script
The ancient Indian alphabetic writing system used in Ashoka's edicts, which served as the mother of Devanagari, Odia, Bengali, Tamil, and other scripts.
10. Sarnath Lion Capital
Ashoka's monolithic sandstone sculpture featuring four back-to-back lions and the 24-spoke Dharma wheel, adopted as the National Emblem of India.

Complete NCERT In-Text Activities & Exercise Solutions

Exercise Q1: What are the features of an empire, and how is it different from a kingdom? Explain.

Answer:

  • Territorial Extent: A kingdom covers a single cultural or geographical region (e.g., Kosala), whereas an empire encompasses many conquered kingdoms and provinces stretching across a subcontinent.
  • Cultural & Linguistic Diversity: Kingdoms have relatively homogeneous populations speaking one main dialect. Empires rule millions of diverse peoples speaking multiple languages with different religious beliefs and traditions.
  • Military Scale: Kingdoms maintain small armies. Empires require huge permanent standing armies (infantry, cavalry, war elephants, chariots, and navy) stationed along borders to maintain peace.
  • Administrative Complexity: Empires require a structured multi-tiered bureaucracy (tax collectors, judges, road superintendents, and royal spies) to collect taxes and administer distant provinces.
Exercise Q2: What are some important factors for the transition from kingdoms to empires?

Answer: The transition from small kingdoms to the Mauryan Empire was driven by:

  1. Agricultural Surplus: Iron axes and ploughshares combined with wet rice transplantation generated massive food reserves to feed imperial armies, cities, and administrators.
  2. Control of Rich Mineral Resources: Direct access to the iron ore mines of Jharkhand and copper mines of Rajasthan provided weapons and currency.
  3. Forest Wealth & War Elephants: Tapping elephant-rich forests in Magadha and Kalinga gave imperial generals an invincible military assault weapon.
  4. Trade Route Control: Dominating the great trade highways (Uttarapatha and Dakshinapatha) and river ports enabled emperors to collect customs duties and tolls on luxury goods.
  5. Visionary Leadership & Statecraft: The political brilliance of Chandragupta Maurya and the systematic statecraft doctrines of Kautilya provided the administrative blueprint for imperial unity.
Exercise Q3: Alexander is considered an important king in world history — why do you think that is so?

Answer:

  • Vast Conquests: In just twelve years (334–323 BCE), he conquered the Persian Empire, Egypt, and marched all the way to the borders of northwest India, creating one of the largest empires of the ancient world.
  • Cultural Fusion (Hellenism): His campaigns opened unprecedented direct land and sea routes connecting the Mediterranean world with Central and South Asia. Greek, Persian, Egyptian, and Indian cultures, art, and philosophies mingled, laying the foundation for Gandhara art and Indo-Greek trade.
  • Catalyst in India: By shattering the small tribal kingdoms of Punjab and Sindh, Alexander's invasion created a political vacuum that enabled Chandragupta Maurya to quickly unify northwest India under the Mauryan flag.
Exercise Q4: In early Indian history, the Mauryas are considered important. State your reasons.

Answer: The Mauryas are pivotal in Indian history because:

  1. First Pan-Indian Empire: For the very first time in history, almost the entire Indian subcontinent—from modern Afghanistan in the northwest to Karnataka in the south and Bengal in the east—was politically united under one central administration.
  2. Economic Integration: Safe highways (Uttarapatha), standard punch-marked silver coins, and protected merchant guilds enabled flourishing commerce across India and overseas.
  3. Systematic Statecraft: Kautilya's Arthashastra laid down enduring principles of public administration, justice, and taxation.
  4. Ashoka's Moral Legacy: Ashoka established a revolutionary model of royal governance founded on moral righteousness (Dhamma), non-violence (Ahimsa), religious tolerance, and public welfare.
  5. Enduring National Symbols: The Mauryan Sarnath Lion Capital and the Ashoka Chakra remain the proud official emblems of modern democratic India.
Exercise Q5: What were some of Kautilya's key ideas? Which ones can you observe in the world around us today?

Key Ideas of Kautilya & Modern Parallels:

  • Welfare of the People as Supreme Duty: Kautilya declared that the ruler must find his happiness in the happiness of his citizens. Today, democratic welfare governments are judged by how well they provide healthcare, education, and social safety nets.
  • The Saptānga Theory: Kautilya saw the state as needing seven coordinated elements (ruler, civil services, fertile land, defensive borders, treasury, armed forces, and reliable foreign allies). Modern nations still rely on this exact framework (President/PM, civil bureaucracy, national economy, defense forts, RBI/treasury, military, and diplomatic treaties like UN/alliances).
  • Water Security & Canals: Kautilya insisted that farming must not depend only on rain; states must build irrigation tanks and dams. India's canal networks, dams, and rainwater harvesting policies directly follow this advice.
  • Espionage & National Intelligence: Kautilya designed intelligence networks to protect state security. Today, agencies like RAW and IB perform identical intelligence functions.
Exercise Q6: What were the unusual things about Ashoka and his empire? What has continued to influence India and why? (Opinion in ~250 words)

Opinion on Emperor Ashoka's Enduring Legacy:

Emperor Ashoka stands completely unique among all conquerors in world history. While world conquerors like Alexander, Julius Caesar, or Genghis Khan sought more territories until their death, Ashoka voluntarily gave up war at the very pinnacle of his military triumph after the Kalinga War in 261 BCE.

What was truly extraordinary was his transformation: he traded Bherighosha (the war drum) for Dhammaghosha (the call of righteousness). Rather than ruling through fear, secret police, or brute force, Ashoka viewed his subjects as his own children. He appointed Dhamma Mahamattas to ensure justice, banned animal sacrifices, opened veterinary hospitals, planted trees along highways, and dug community wells. In an era of religious dogmatism, he boldly proclaimed that respecting other faiths is the highest way to honor one's own faith.

Ashoka's ideals continue to guide modern India in profound ways:

  • National Symbols: His four-lion Sarnath Capital is India's National Emblem, and the 24-spoke Ashoka Chakra sits at the heart of our National Flag, symbolizing dynamic moral law and progress.
  • Foreign Policy: Independent India's foreign policy of Panchsheel (peaceful coexistence) and global non-alignment directly derives from Ashoka's ethos of non-aggression and universal brotherhood.
  • Secular Tolerance: The constitutional value of secularism—giving equal respect and freedom to all religions—is the living spirit of Ashoka's Dhamma.
Exercise Q7: After reading Ashoka's edict on Dhamma officers working with Buddhist, Brahmana, Ajivika, and Jain sects, do you think he was tolerant towards other religions?

Answer:

Yes, absolutely! Ashoka was exceptionally tolerant and open-minded. The edict clearly proves that:

  1. Even though Ashoka was personally a devoted follower of the Buddha, his royal welfare officers (Dhamma Mahamattas) were strictly ordered to serve and protect the interests of all religious communities alike—including the Brahmanas, the Buddhist Order (Sangha), the Jains (Nirgranthas), and the Ajivikas.
  2. He donated beautifully polished, expensive rock-cut caves in the Barabar Hills (Bihar) to the ascetic Ajivika sect, showing that royal patronage was never restricted to Buddhism alone.
  3. In Major Rock Edict XII, Ashoka explicitly stated that all sects share the common essence of self-control and purity of mind, and that harmony (Samavaya) among diverse religious beliefs is the greatest virtue.
Exercise Q8: The Brahmi Script was a widely used writing system in ancient India. Create a small project explaining its importance.

Classroom Project: The Mother of Indian Scripts — Brahmi

  • What is Brahmi? Brahmi is the oldest deciphered alphabetic-syllabic writing system of historic India, famously carved on Ashoka's pillars and rock edicts across the subcontinent in the 3rd century BCE.
  • Decipherment: In 1837, British antiquarian James Prinsep, an officer of the Calcutta Mint, successfully deciphered the ancient Brahmi script, unlocking the lost history of Emperor Ashoka for the modern world!
  • Mother of Modern Indian Scripts: Almost all modern scripts of South and Southeast Asia evolved over two millennia from ancient Brahmi:
    Brahmi ➔ Gupta Script ➔ Siddhamatrixa / Nagari ➔ Devanagari, Odia, Bengali, Assamese, Gujarati, Punjabi (Gurmukhi), Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, and Tamil!
  • Direction of Writing: Unlike Kharosthi (which was written from right to left like Urdu/Arabic), Brahmi was written from left to right, just like modern Hindi, English, and Odia!
Exercise Q9: Suppose you had to travel from Kausambi to Kaveripattanam in the 3rd century BCE. How would you undertake this journey and how long would it take?

Answer (Historical Travel Simulation):

  • Route Taken:
    1. Begin at Kausambi (near modern Prayagraj, UP) on the Yamuna River.
    2. Follow the Dakshinapatha (Great Southern Highway) south-west to Vidisha and Ujjayini in Malwa.
    3. Cross the Vindhya and Satpura ranges over the Narmada and Tapti river fords to reach Pratishthana (Paithan) on the Godavari.
    4. Travel southward through the Deccan plateau passes towards the Krishna river basin, and descend past the Eastern Ghats into the fertile Kaveri delta to reach the flourishing maritime port of Kaveripattanam (Puhar) on the Bay of Bengal.
  • Modes of Transport: Bullocks carts for luggage, pack horses or donkeys for rough hilly terrain, walking on foot, and river ferries to cross flooded rivers.
  • Expected Duration: The distance is approximately 1,800 to 2,000 kilometres. Travelling at an average pace of 20 to 25 km per day, with necessary halts at roadside sarais, temples, and craft towns to rest cattle and avoid monsoon mud or bandit-infested forests, the journey would take approximately 3 to 4 months!

Exam Hall Traps & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Common Exam Hall Traps to Avoid!
  • Trap 1: Confusing Ashoka's Dhamma with a Religion: Ashoka's Dhamma was NOT a new religion. It had no gods, prayers, or rituals; it was a civic and moral code of conduct focused on peace, truth, and respect.
  • Trap 2: Alexander Did Not Defeat Chandragupta: Alexander invaded India in 326 BCE (during Dhana Nanda's reign). Chandragupta Maurya defeated Seleucus Nicator in 305 BCE, over twenty years after Alexander!
  • Trap 3: Sarnath Capital Details: The capital features four lions (only three are visible from any single frontal angle). The base contains an elephant, a galloping horse, a bull, and a lion, separated by four 24-spoke Chakras.
  • Trap 4: Script vs Language: The language of Ashoka's main edicts was Prakrit; the script used was Brahmi (except Kharosthi in the northwest and Greek/Aramaic in Afghanistan).

15 High-Yield Revision Points

  1. Empire Definition: An extensive sovereign territory uniting diverse regions under a centralized government and large army.
  2. Magadha's Strength: Iron mines, rich agriculture, elephant corps, and strategic river capitals (Rajagriha & Pataliputra).
  3. Nanda Army: Over 200,000 infantry and thousands of war elephants intimidated Alexander's troops at the Beas River (326 BCE).
  4. Mauryan Founders: Chandragupta Maurya and his strategist mentor Kautilya (Chanakya) established the dynasty in 321 BCE.
  5. Seleucid Treaty (305 BCE): Chandragupta defeated Seleucus Nicator, gaining eastern Afghanistan and receiving ambassador Megasthenes.
  6. Megasthenes' *Indica*: Described Pataliputra's wooden palaces, 570 towers, 64 gates, and honest citizenry.
  7. Kautilya's *Arthashastra*: Classic text on administration, justice, and the king's duty to serve public happiness.
  8. Saptānga Limbs: King, Ministers, Territory/People, Forts, Treasury, Army, and Allies.
  9. Kalinga War (261 BCE): Bloody conquest of coastal Odisha with over 100,000 deaths that caused Ashoka's dramatic spiritual awakening.
  10. Bherighosha to Dhammaghosha: Renunciation of military drums of war in favor of moral victory through Dhamma.
  11. Core Dhamma Principles: Ahimsa, obedience to elders, kindness to slaves, truthfulness, and inter-religious tolerance.
  12. Dhamma Mahamattas: Royal officials appointed by Ashoka to promote moral conduct and look after social welfare across all sects.
  13. Inscriptions: Carved on rocks and pillars in Prakrit using Brahmi script; deciphered in 1837 by James Prinsep.
  14. Royal Titles: Ashoka called himself Devanampiya (Beloved of the Gods) and Piyadasi (He who regards all with affection).
  15. National Emblem: Sarnath Lion Capital with the 24-spoke Ashoka Chakra and motto "Satyameva Jayate" adopted on 26 Jan 1950.
