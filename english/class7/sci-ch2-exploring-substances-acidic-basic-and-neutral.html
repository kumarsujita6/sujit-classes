1. The Science Fair Secret Message: How Does Invisible Writing Work?

On National Science Day (28 February), siblings Ashwin and Keerthi received a plain blank white sheet at the school gate. A volunteer sprayed a clear mist onto the paper, and instantly, bright words appeared: "Welcome to the Wonderful World of Science!"

The Chemistry Behind the Mystery

How did the words appear from nowhere? The trick uses an acid-base indicator reaction:

  • Method A (Turmeric & Soap): Writing with a yellow turmeric extract solution on paper. When sprayed with basic soap water or baking soda solution, the invisible or faint yellow writing immediately turns vivid reddish-brown!
  • Method B (Phenolphthalein & Lime Water): Writing with colourless phenolphthalein indicator. When sprayed with basic lime water or washing soda, the clear writing turns bright magenta-pink!
  • Method C (Citric Acid & Mild Heat): Writing with lemon or orange juice. When the paper is gently warmed near a lamp, the citric acid caramelises and turns deep brown!

2. What Are Acids and Bases?

Key Definition • Acidic Substances (Acids):
The word acid comes from the Latin word acere, which means sour. Substances that taste sour and turn blue litmus paper to red are called acidic substances, and their chemical nature is called acidic. Examples in food: lemon juice, orange juice, curd, tamarind, and vinegar.
Key Definition • Basic Substances (Bases):
Substances that taste bitter and feel soapy or slippery when rubbed between the fingers are called bases. Their chemical nature is called basic. Bases turn red litmus paper to blue. Examples: baking soda, soap solution, washing powder, and lime water (chuna pani).
Laboratory Safety Rule • NEVER Taste Unknown Chemicals!
While food items like lemons and curd can be tasted safely, laboratory acids and bases (like hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, or sodium hydroxide) are highly corrosive and toxic! They can cause severe chemical burns. Scientists therefore use safe substances called indicators to test whether a chemical is acidic or basic without touching or tasting it!

4. Natural Indicators: Nature's Colour-Changing Detectives

An indicator is a special substance that changes its colour when added to an acidic or a basic solution. Nature provides us with three outstanding, easy-to-make indicators:

Figure 2.2: The Natural Indicator Spectrum • Colour shifts for Litmus, Turmeric, and Red Rose.

Detailed Investigation of Each Indicator

  1. Litmus: Extracted from lichens (a symbiotic composite organism of fungi and algae). In pure distilled water, litmus has a mauve (purple) colour. It is available as solution or as absorbent strips: blue litmus paper and red litmus paper.
    • Acidic solution turns blue litmus to red.
    • Basic solution turns red litmus to blue.
    • Neutral solutions (like pure water, sugar solution, salt water) do not change the colour of either strip.
  2. Turmeric (Haldi): An ancient kitchen spice. Turmeric paste spread on blotting paper creates yellow turmeric strips.
    • Turmeric turns deep reddish-brown in basic solutions (like soap water or lime water).
    • Turmeric remains yellow in both acidic (lemon juice, vinegar) and neutral solutions.
  3. Red Rose Petals Extract: Fresh petals boiled or soaked in warm water yield a gentle light pink solution.
    • Turns dark pink or magenta-red when mixed with an acid.
    • Turns vibrant green when mixed with a base.
  4. Olfactory Indicators (For Visually Challenged Friends): Some substances change their smell rather than colour! For example, raw chopped onions and vanilla essence lose their characteristic aroma in basic solutions (like soap water) but retain their smell in acidic solutions.

5. Neutralisation: What Happens When Acid and Base Meet?

What happens when you mix an acid with a base in equal strengths? They fight each other and cancel each other's effects!

Key Definition • Neutralisation Reaction:
The chemical reaction between an acid and a base is called a neutralisation reaction. In this reaction, the acidic nature of the acid and the basic nature of the base are both destroyed. A brand-new substance called a salt and water are produced, accompanied by the evolution of heat.

Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat

Figure 2.3: Molecular Neutralisation Reaction • Formation of salt, water, and heat energy.

6. Neutralisation in Everyday Life

Neutralisation is not just an activity in test tubes; it is working continuously to protect our health and environment:

Four Real-World Lifesavers

1. Relief from Ant Stings

When a red ant bites, it injects formic acid into the skin, causing redness, swelling, and acute burning pain. We can neutralise the acid immediately by rubbing moist baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate) or calamine lotion (which contains basic zinc carbonate). The base cancels the acid, giving rapid soothing relief!

2. Soothing Indigestion (Acidity)

Our stomach naturally secretes dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl) to kill harmful bacteria and digest proteins. But eating too much spicy food triggers excessive acid production, causing painful heartburn. We take an antacid like Milk of Magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) or sodium bicarbonate syrup. The mild base neutralises excess stomach acid!

3. Agricultural Soil Treatment

Plants cannot grow well if the farm soil is either too acidic or too basic:
• Too Acidic Soil: Caused by excessive chemical fertilisers. Treated by adding bases like quicklime (calcium oxide, CaO) or slaked lime (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂).
• Too Basic Soil: Treated by adding organic matter (manure/compost), which releases gentle natural acids during decomposition to balance the soil pH!

4. Factory Waste Water Treatment

Wastes from chemical factories and manufacturing plants often contain strong acids. If dumped directly into rivers and ponds, the acid will kill fish, frogs, aquatic plants, and pollute community drinking water. Therefore, factories must treat acidic industrial effluents with basic substances (like slaked lime) to neutralise them before releasing them into water bodies.

Question 2: Ammonia is found in many household products, such as window cleaners. It turns red litmus blue. What is its nature? NCERT Exercise

Answer:

Ammonia is basic in nature.

Scientific Reason: Any substance that turns red litmus paper to blue is alkaline or basic. When ammonia dissolves in water, it forms ammonium hydroxide, which releases hydroxide ions, exhibiting basic properties.

Question 3: Name the source from which litmus solution is obtained. What is the use of this solution? NCERT Exercise

Answer:

  1. Source: Litmus is a natural dye extracted from lichens (a symbiotic partnership between a fungus and an alga).
  2. Use: Litmus solution is used as an acid-base indicator to test whether a given unknown chemical liquid is acidic, basic, or neutral without needing to taste or touch it.
Question 4: Is distilled water acidic/basic/neutral? How would you verify it? NCERT Exercise

Answer:

Distilled water is neutral in nature (it is neither acidic nor basic).

Verification Method:

  • Take two strips: one blue litmus paper and one red litmus paper.
  • Put a drop of distilled water on both strips using a clean dropper.
  • Observation: The blue litmus remains blue, and the red litmus remains red. Neither strip changes colour. This proves distilled water is neutral.
Question 5: Manya is blindfolded. She is given two unknown solutions to test and determine whether they are acidic or basic. Which indicator should Manya use to test the solutions and why? NCERT Exercise

Answer:

Manya should use an olfactory indicator (such as onion extract, vanilla essence, or clove oil).

Why? (Scientific Justification):

  • Because Manya is blindfolded, she cannot see colour changes in visual indicators like litmus or turmeric.
  • Olfactory indicators change their odour (smell) in different mediums:
  • In a basic solution, the characteristic pungent smell of onion or sweet aroma of vanilla is completely destroyed and disappears.
  • In an acidic solution, the smell of onion or vanilla is retained without any change.
  • By using her sense of smell, Manya can accurately identify which solution is acidic and which is basic!

Question 7: Grape juice was mixed with red rose extract; the mixture got a tint of red colour. What will happen if baking soda is added to this mixture? Justify your answer. NCERT Exercise

Answer:

When baking soda is added to the red mixture, effervescence (bubbling) will occur with the release of carbon dioxide gas, and as more baking soda is added, the colour of the solution will turn green!

Scientific Justification:

  1. Grape juice contains tartaric acid. When mixed with red rose extract, the acidic medium turns the rose indicator red.
  2. Baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate) is a base. When added to acid, it undergoes a neutralisation reaction, releasing CO₂ gas bubbles.
  3. Once enough baking soda is added to neutralise all tartaric acid and make the solution alkaline, the red rose indicator reacts to the basic medium and turns green!
Question 8: Keerthi wrote a secret message to her grandmother using orange juice. Can you assist her grandmother in revealing the message? Which indicator would you use? NCERT Exercise

Answer:

Grandmother can reveal Keerthi's message using two wonderful methods:

  • Method 1 (Using Red Rose Extract Spray): Spray the paper gently with red rose petals extract. Because orange juice contains citric acid (acidic), the writing will develop a distinct dark pink / red colour against the background!
  • Method 2 (Using Red Litmus Solution): Spraying with red cabbage or red litmus extract will turn bright red where the acid is present.
  • Method 3 (Gentle Warming): Gently holding the paper above a warm electric bulb or iron box dehydrates and oxidises the citric acid carbohydrates, causing the letters to turn visible golden-brown!
Question 9: How can natural indicators be prepared? Explain by giving an example. NCERT Exercise

Answer:

Natural indicators can be easily prepared by extracting colored pigments (anthocyanins, curcumin, etc.) from natural plant parts like flower petals, spices, or vegetables using warm water or alcohol.

Step-by-Step Example • Preparation of Turmeric Indicator:

  1. Take one teaspoon of yellow turmeric powder in a small cup.
  2. Add 2 tablespoons of water or rubbing alcohol and mix thoroughly to form a smooth paste.
  3. Dip a white filter paper or drawing sheet into the paste, or spread it evenly using a flat brush.
  4. Let the paper dry completely in the sun or shade.
  5. Once dry, cut the yellow sheet into thin strips (about 1 cm × 5 cm). Your home-made natural turmeric indicator strips are ready!
Question 10: Three liquids are given to you: vinegar, baking soda solution, and sugar solution. Can you identify them using ONLY turmeric paper? Explain. NCERT Exercise

Answer: YES! We can systematically identify all three liquids using only turmeric paper through a 3-step logical deduction:

  1. Step 1 • Identify Baking Soda: Put a drop of each of the three liquids onto three separate yellow turmeric strips.
    → The liquid that turns the yellow turmeric paper to reddish-brown is the Baking Soda Solution (because bases turn turmeric reddish-brown).
    → The other two liquids produce NO colour change (turmeric remains yellow). These two are vinegar (acid) and sugar solution (neutral).
  2. Step 2 • Prepare Reddish-Brown Test Strips: Take two fresh turmeric strips that have already been turned reddish-brown using a drop of the identified baking soda solution.
  3. Step 3 • Distinguish Vinegar from Sugar Solution:
    • Add a few drops of the first remaining liquid to one reddish-brown strip.
    • Add a few drops of the second remaining liquid to the other reddish-brown strip.
    → The liquid that reverses the colour back to yellow is Vinegar! (The acid in vinegar neutralises the base, returning turmeric to its original yellow colour).
    → The liquid that produces no change (the strip remains reddish-brown) is the Sugar Solution (neutral liquids do not neutralise bases).
Question 11: The extract of red rose turns the liquid X to green. What will the nature of liquid X be? What will happen when excess of amla juice is added to liquid X? NCERT Exercise

Answer:

  1. Nature of Liquid X: Liquid X is basic (alkaline) in nature. Red rose extract turns green only in a basic medium.
  2. When Excess Amla Juice is Added:
    • Amla juice is rich in ascorbic acid and citric acid.
    • When amla juice is added drop by drop, a neutralisation reaction occurs between the base (liquid X) and the acid (amla juice).
    • The solution transitions from green back to light pink as neutral point is reached.
    • When excess amla juice is added, the mixture becomes predominantly acidic. Consequently, the red rose indicator will turn dark pink / magenta-red!
Question 12: Complete the flowchart for testing and treating garden soil showing signs of poor health. NCERT Exercise

Completed Flowchart Information:

  • Indicator to test soil nature: Universal indicator solution, pH paper, or Red/Blue Litmus paper (soil mixed in distilled water and filtered).
  • If the soil is Acidic in nature: The acidic soil can be treated with Quicklime (Calcium Oxide, CaO) or Slaked Lime [Calcium Hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂] or chalk.
  • If the soil is Basic in nature: The basic soil can be treated with Organic Matter (Manure / Compost), which releases organic acids during decomposition to neutralise the excess alkali.
Dive Deeper Challenge: Aman accidentally spilt vinegar on eggshell/marble pieces and noticed bubbling. Pouring soap solution produced no bubbles. Why? HOTS / Dive Deeper

Scientific Explanation:

  • Eggshells and marble chips are made of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃).
  • Vinegar contains acetic acid (CH₃COOH). When an acid reacts with a metal carbonate, a chemical reaction occurs producing calcium acetate, water, and vigorous effervescence of carbon dioxide gas (CO₂):
    CaCO₃ + 2CH₃COOH → (CH₃COO)₂Ca + H₂O + CO₂ ↑ (Bubbles!)
  • Soap solution is a base. Bases do not react with metal carbonates to release gases. Hence, no carbon dioxide bubbles appeared!

Correct Answer: (b)
Turmeric paper turns reddish-brown exclusively in the presence of basic substances. Lime water contains calcium hydroxide, which is a base. Lemon juice and vinegar are acidic, while distilled water is neutral.

Q2. When an ant bites, it injects an acid into the skin. Which of the following should be rubbed on the skin for instant relief?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (c)
Ant venom contains formic acid. Applying a mild base such as baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate) or calamine lotion (zinc carbonate) neutralises the acid and stops the pain.

Q3. In a science laboratory, a student touches the bottom of a test tube immediately after carrying out a neutralisation reaction between hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide. The test tube feels warm because:

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (a)
Neutralisation is an exothermic chemical reaction. When hydrogen ions from the acid combine with hydroxide ions from the base to form water, bond formation releases thermal energy, warming the container.

Q4. A farmer discovers that crops are withering because the soil has become overly acidic from excess chemical fertilisers. What should the farmer add to restore the soil?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (b)
Acidic soil must be neutralised by adding a base. Quicklime (CaO) and slaked lime [Ca(OH)₂] are agricultural bases that react with soil acids to restore neutral pH.

Q5. What colour change is observed when red rose petals extract is added to a soap solution?

Teacher Explanation
Correct Answer: (c)
Red rose petal extract is an acid-base indicator. In an acidic solution (like lemon juice), it turns dark pink/magenta. In a basic solution (like soap water or lime water), it turns green!

Exam Hall Traps & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Master common exam pitfalls and review 15 rapid bullet points before test day.

Exam Hall Trap #1 • Confusing "Lime" with "Lime Water":
Trap: Writing that lime water is squeezed from a green lime fruit.
Correction: In chemistry, lime water is a clear aqueous solution of Calcium Hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂], prepared by mixing slaked lime (chuna) with water! Lemon/lime fruit juice is an acid (citric acid), whereas lime water is a base!
Exam Hall Trap #2 • Assuming Neutral Substances Have "No Colour":
Trap: Stating that neutral substances turn litmus paper clear or colourless.
Correction: Neutral substances cause NO change in litmus paper. Blue litmus stays blue, and red litmus stays red.

15 Lightning Revision Points for Quick Exam Recap

  1. Acid Etymology: The term acid comes from Latin acere (sour). Acids taste sour and turn blue litmus red.
  2. Base Characteristics: Bases taste bitter, feel slippery/soapy, and turn red litmus blue.
  3. Indicators: Substances that show a distinct colour shift in acidic versus basic solutions.
  4. Litmus Source: Litmus is extracted from lichens. Its neutral colour in pure water is purple (mauve).
  5. Turmeric Reaction: Turmeric paper remains yellow in acid and neutral, but turns reddish-brown in bases.
  6. Red Rose Extract: Turns dark pink/magenta in acidic solutions and green in basic solutions.
  7. Olfactory Indicators: Indicators like onion and vanilla detect acids/bases through changes in odour (vital for visually challenged students).
  8. Natural Organic Acids: Citrus fruits (citric acid), vinegar (acetic acid), curd (lactic acid), tamarind (tartaric acid), amla (ascorbic acid), ant sting (formic acid).
  9. Common Bases: Lime water [Ca(OH)₂], baking soda (NaHCO₃), milk of magnesia [Mg(OH)₂], soap solutions.
  10. Neutralisation Formula: Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat.
  11. Exothermic Nature: Neutralisation reactions generate heat, warming the reaction vessel.
  12. Ant Bite Treatment: Calamine lotion (zinc carbonate) or moist baking soda neutralises acidic formic acid venom.
  13. Indigestion Cure: Antacids containing milk of magnesia neutralise excess hydrochloric acid in the stomach.
  14. Soil Management: Acidic soil is treated with quicklime (CaO) or slaked lime [Ca(OH)₂]; basic soil is treated with organic compost.
  15. Industrial Effluents: Acidic factory wastewater must be neutralised with slaked lime before disposal into aquatic bodies.
