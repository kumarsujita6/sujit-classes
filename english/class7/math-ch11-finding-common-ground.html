Part 2.1: The Anatomy of Factors & The Prime Multiset

In arithmetic, every natural number greater than 1 can be viewed as a chemical molecule constructed from indivisible mathematical atoms called prime numbers.

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (Unique Factorization Theorem)

Any composite integer greater than 1 can be expressed as a product of prime numbers in exactly one way, apart from the order in which the prime factors appear.

Whether we branch 90 as 3 × 30, or as 2 × 45, or as 9 × 10:

Branch A: 90 = 3 × 30 = 3 × (2 × 15) = 3 × 2 × (3 × 5) = 2 × 3² × 5
Branch B: 90 = 2 × 45 = 2 × (9 × 5) = 2 × (3 × 3) × 5 = 2 × 3² × 5
Branch C: 90 = 9 × 10 = (3 × 3) × (2 × 5) = 2 × 3² × 5

The destination is always invariant! The multiset of primes {2, 3, 3, 5} is the unique biological DNA of the number 90.

The Factor Subpart Theorem

How do factors arise from prime factorisation? A number d is a factor of N if and only if the prime factorisation of d is a subpart (subset) of the prime factorisation of N.

Consider 840 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 = 2³ × 3¹ × 5¹ × 7¹.

  • Is 28 a factor of 840? Prime factorisation of 28 = 2 × 2 × 7. Since 840 contains three 2s and one 7, 2 × 2 × 7 is an available subpart! Regrouping: 840 = (2 × 2 × 7) × (2 × 3 × 5) = 28 × 30. Yes, 28 is a factor!
  • Is 14 a factor of 840? 14 = 2 × 7. Both primes are available. 840 = (2 × 7) × (2 × 2 × 3 × 5) = 14 × 60. Yes!
  • Is 8 a factor of 840? 8 = 2 × 2 × 2. Three 2s are present. 840 = 8 × 105. Yes!
  • Is 27 a factor of 840? 27 = 3 × 3 × 3 = 3³. But 840 contains only one 3! It is impossible to extract three 3s. Therefore, 27 is NOT a factor of 840!
Teacher's Secret: Combinatorial Factor Counting Formula

If a number N has prime factorisation N = pᵃ · qᵇ · rᶜ ..., the total count of factors is given by:

Total Number of Factors = (a + 1)(b + 1)(c + 1) ...

Why? For each prime p, a factor can choose power p⁰, p¹, ..., pᵃ (which gives a + 1 choices). For 840 = 2³ × 3¹ × 5¹ × 7¹: Total factors = (3 + 1)(1 + 1)(1 + 1)(1 + 1) = 4 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32 factors!

Part 2.2: Conjectures, Counterexamples & The Architecture of Proof

NCERT Chapter 11 introduces a profound philosophical pillar of science and mathematics: how do we establish truth?

Anshu's Claim (The Danger of Hasty Conjectures)

Anshu observed several numbers and claimed: "The larger a number is, the longer its prime factorisation will be."

In mathematics, a statement made based on patterns without formal deductive proof is termed a Conjecture (ଅନୁମାନ).

Is Anshu's conjecture true? No! We demolish this claim instantly by producing a Counterexample (ପ୍ରତି-ଉଦାହରଣ):

Look at the smaller number 96: 96 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 = 2⁵ × 3 (6 prime factors!)
Look at the larger number 121: 121 = 11 × 11 = 11² (only 2 prime factors!)
96 < 121, but 96 has THREE TIMES as many prime factors as 121!

Even more dramatically: Consider 1024 (2¹⁰, length 10) versus the prime number 1,000,003 (length 1). A million-magnitude number has only one prime factor!

Golden Epistemological Rule of Mathematics: To prove a statement for all cases, you need a deductive proof covering every infinity of integers. But to completely disprove and annihilate a claim, you need only ONE single valid counterexample!

Part 2.3: Highest Common Factor (HCF) as Prime Multiset Intersection

To find the HCF of two numbers, we seek the largest common subpart. A prime factor can only participate in the common subpart if it exists in BOTH factorisations, and it can only be repeated as many times as it appears in the number that has fewer occurrences of that prime.

Formal Prime Power Rule for HCF

To calculate the HCF of any set of numbers:

  1. Express every number in prime power exponential notation: A = ∏ pᵃⁱ, B = ∏ pᵇⁱ.
  2. Identify the primes common to all factorisations.
  3. Take each common prime raised to the MINIMUM exponent occurring across the numbers:
HCF(A, B) = ∏ p^min(a_i, b_i)

Detailed Example: HCF of 225 and 750

225 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 5 = 3² × 5²
750 = 2 × 3 × 5 × 5 × 5 = 2¹ × 3¹ × 5³

• Prime 2: Appears in 750 (power 1) but in 225 (power 0). min(0, 1) = 0 → 2⁰ = 1 (Omit)
• Prime 3: Appears in 225 (power 2) and in 750 (power 1). min(2, 1) = 1 → 3¹
• Prime 5: Appears in 225 (power 2) and in 750 (power 3). min(2, 3) = 2 → 5² = 25

HCF(225, 750) = 3¹ × 5² = 3 × 25 = 75

Part 2.4: Lowest Common Multiple (LCM) as Prime Multiset Union

A multiple of N must contain all prime factors of N as a subpart. Therefore, a common multiple of two numbers A and B must contain A's prime factors as well as B's prime factors. To find the LOWEST common multiple, we do not waste any unnecessary prime factors; we take just enough to cover both!

Formal Prime Power Rule for LCM

To calculate the LCM of any set of numbers:

  1. Express every number in prime power exponential notation.
  2. List every prime that appears in any of the numbers.
  3. Take each prime raised to the MAXIMUM exponent occurring in any of the factorisations:
LCM(A, B) = ∏ p^max(a_i, b_i)

Detailed Example: LCM of 96 and 360

96 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 = 2⁵ × 3¹
360 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 2³ × 3² × 5¹

• Prime 2: Max power across {5, 3} is 2⁵ = 32
• Prime 3: Max power across {1, 2} is 3² = 9
• Prime 5: Max power across {0, 1} is 5¹ = 5

LCM(96, 360) = 2⁵ × 3² × 5¹ = 32 × 9 × 5 = 1440
Why is there NO "Highest Common Multiple"?

Multiples stretch toward positive infinity. For 4 and 6, common multiples are 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 120, 1200, 12000000... Since natural numbers are unbounded and infinite, there is no largest integer, hence a "Highest Common Multiple" cannot exist mathematically.

Part 2.5: Algebraic Generalisations & Parity Theorems

Mathematics transitions from mere computation to high-level reasoning when we identify properties that hold for all numbers of a given class. This is called Generalisation (ସାଧାରଣୀକରଣ).

Theorem 1: Consecutive Integers are ALWAYS Co-Prime

For any natural number n, the consecutive pair (n, n + 1) has:

HCF(n, n + 1) = 1   |   LCM(n, n + 1) = n(n + 1)

Rigorous Proof: Suppose integer d is a common divisor of both n and n + 1. Then d must also divide their difference: (n + 1) - n = 1. Since the only positive divisor of 1 is 1 itself, d must equal 1! Thus, HCF is strictly 1, and LCM = (Product)/HCF = n(n + 1).

Theorem 2: Consecutive Even Integers Always Have HCF = 2

Any two consecutive even integers can be expressed algebraically as 2k and 2k + 2 (where k is a positive integer):

HCF(2k, 2k + 2) = 2   |   LCM(2k, 2k + 2) = 2k(k + 1) = (Product) / 2

Proof: Both numbers are even, so 2 divides both. Let d be any common divisor. Then d must divide their difference: (2k + 2) - 2k = 2. The only positive divisors of 2 are 1 and 2. Since both are even, HCF must be exactly 2!

Example: For 14 and 16: HCF(14, 16) = 2; LCM = (14 × 16) / 2 = 112.

Theorem 3: Consecutive Odd Integers are ALWAYS Co-Prime

Any two consecutive odd integers can be written as 2k + 1 and 2k + 3:

HCF(2k + 1, 2k + 3) = 1   |   LCM(2k + 1, 2k + 3) = (2k + 1)(2k + 3)

Proof: The difference between two consecutive odd numbers is (2k + 3) - (2k + 1) = 2. Any common divisor must divide 2. But odd numbers are never divisible by 2! Hence, the only common divisor left is 1.

Example: For 19 and 21: HCF = 1; LCM = 19 × 21 = 399.

Theorem 4: Factor-Multiple Pairs ("Be My Factor, I'll Be Your Multiple")

If a number a is an exact factor of b (meaning b = ka for some positive integer k):

HCF(a, ka) = a (the smaller number)
LCM(a, ka) = ka = b (the larger number)

Examples from NCERT:

  • HCF(6, 18) = 6 because 18 = 3 × 6. LCM(6, 18) = 18.
  • HCF(3, 24) = 3; LCM(3, 24) = 24.
  • If one number is m, any other number km (like 2m, 3m, 5m) ensures HCF is m.
  • If one number is 7k, any factor of 7k (like 7 or k) or any multiple (like 14k, 21k) satisfies the property.
Theorem 5: The Scaling Multiplier Law (Doubling & Multipliers)

If both numbers in a pair are scaled by a common multiplier c, both their HCF and LCM scale by that exact same factor c!

HCF(c · a, c · b) = c · HCF(a, b)
LCM(c · a, c · b) = c · LCM(a, b)

NCERT Textbook Investigation (Page 14):

Consider 270 and 50: HCF(270, 50) = 10. If we double both to 540 and 100: Each number acquires an additional prime factor of 2. That extra 2 joins the common subpart: New HCF = 2 × 10 = 20!

Now consider multiples of 14: 14 × 6 and 14 × 9: Both share 14. We factor out 14: HCF(14 × 6, 14 × 9) = 14 × HCF(6, 9) = 14 × 3 = 42!

When is the HCF equal to the common multiplier itself? Only when the remaining quotients are co-prime! For example, 10 × 38 and 10 × 21: HCF = 10 × HCF(38, 21). Since 38 = 2 × 19 and 21 = 3 × 7 share no primes, HCF(38, 21) = 1. Therefore, HCF = 10 × 1 = 10!

Part 2.7: The Fundamental Product Identity & The 3-Number Trap

One of the most celebrated theorems of elementary number theory establishes an exact arithmetic bridge between the product of two numbers and the product of their HCF and LCM.

The Fundamental Duality Theorem (Two Numbers)

For any two positive integers a and b:

HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b

Rigorous Exponent Proof: Let p be any prime factor appearing in a and b, with powers a = pˣ ... and b = pʸ ....

  • In the HCF, the exponent of p is min(x, y).
  • In the LCM, the exponent of p is max(x, y).
  • When multiplying HCF × LCM, the exponent of p becomes: min(x, y) + max(x, y).
  • For any two real numbers x and y, the sum of their minimum and maximum is identically equal to their sum: min(x, y) + max(x, y) = x + y!
  • Therefore, in HCF × LCM, the prime p has power x + y, which is precisely the power of p in the product a × b!
THE DEADLY 3-NUMBER TRAP: Why the Identity FAILS for 3 Numbers!

Thousands of students commit this fatal examination blunder: they write HCF(a, b, c) × LCM(a, b, c) = a × b × c. THIS IS COMPLETELY FALSE!

The Decisive Counterexample (NCERT Exploration): Consider the three numbers 4, 6, and 8:

4 = 2²
6 = 2 × 3
8 = 2³

• HCF(4, 6, 8) = 2 (only common prime is 2¹)
• LCM(4, 6, 8) = 2³ × 3 = 24

Now compute HCF × LCM = 2 × 24 = 48
Now compute Product a × b × c = 4 × 6 × 8 = 192

48 ≠ 192! The product is FOUR TIMES LARGER!

Why does it fail? Because when three numbers are considered, some prime factors are shared between only two of the numbers (like the factor 3 shared by 6 and not by 4 or 8). In the simple product a × b × c, those pairwise shared factors are multiplied repeatedly, distorting the simple union-intersection balance.

The True Formula for 3 Numbers:

LCM(a, b, c) = [ a · b · c · HCF(a, b, c) ] / [ HCF(a, b) · HCF(b, c) · HCF(c, a) ]

Folklore Mathematics: The Karnataka Cowherd Crossing Puzzle

"A cowherd took his cows to graze. At a crossing with 3 gates, an equal number passed each gate. At a second crossing with 5 gates, an equal number passed each gate. At a third crossing with 7 gates, an equal number passed each gate. If he had fewer than 200 cows, how many cows did he have?"

The total number of cows N must be simultaneously divisible by 3, 5, and 7. Since 3, 5, and 7 are all distinct prime numbers, their LCM is simply their product: LCM(3, 5, 7) = 3 × 5 × 7 = 105 cows. The next common multiple is 105 × 2 = 210, which exceeds 200. Thus, he had exactly 105 cows!

  4. "The L-Shape Rule in Ladder Division": In the simultaneous ladder, the vertical left column gives HCF. The entire L-shaped path (left column × bottom row) gives LCM!
  5. "The Two-Partner Product Rule": For two numbers only, HCF × LCM = a × b. One partner missing? Divide the product by the known partner!

Problem Statement: Sameeksha's room is 12 ft by 16 ft. (ii) How many tiles should she purchase? (iii) What if the length of the tile could be a fractional number of feet—would the answer change?

1
Given Data & Interpretation

Length of room = 16 ft; Breadth of room = 12 ft. Square tile has side s. To fit without cutting, s must divide 16 ft evenly, and s must divide 12 ft evenly. To use as few tiles as possible, the area of each tile (s²) must be as large as possible, so s must be maximal.

2
Mathematical Principle

The side length s is the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of 12 and 16. The number of tiles is given by: Total Tiles = (Length ÷ s) × (Breadth ÷ s).

3
Step-by-Step Calculation
Factors of 12: {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}
Factors of 16: {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}
Common factors: {1, 2, 4}
Highest Common Factor (HCF) = 4 ft

Tiles along length = 16 ÷ 4 = 4 tiles
Tiles along breadth = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 tiles
Total tiles required = 4 × 3 = 12 tiles

Fractional Tile Exploration: If the side length of the square tile is not restricted to a whole number of feet, could she use a larger square tile? To tile a 12 ft by 16 ft room with a single row of square tiles, the tile side cannot exceed the width of the room (12 ft). If we chose a 12 ft × 12 ft square tile, it fits the breadth (12 ft), but along the length (16 ft), it leaves 16 - 12 = 4 ft uncovered! A single square tile of side > 4 ft cannot divide both 12 and 16 without cutting. Thus, even allowing fractions, 4 ft remains the largest square tile that can tile the floor without cutting!

4
Verification

12 tiles × (4 ft × 4 ft) = 12 × 16 sq ft = 192 sq ft. Area of room = 16 ft × 12 ft = 192 sq ft. Perfect coverage with zero waste.

5
Final Answer

(i) Tile size: 4 ft × 4 ft. (ii) Total tiles required: 12 tiles. (iii) The answer does not change because 4 ft is the absolute geometric upper bound for a square tile fitting both dimensions without cutting.

SAQ 3 • 3 Marks Algebraic Proof: Common Divisor Divides the Difference

Question: Prove algebraically that if an integer d is a common divisor of two integers a and b (with a > b), then d must also divide (a - b). (3 Marks)

1
Algebraic Proof

By definition of divisibility, if d divides a, there exists an integer k₁ such that:

a = k₁ · d

Similarly, if d divides b, there exists an integer k₂ such that:

b = k₂ · d

Now consider the difference (a - b):

a - b = k₁ · d - k₂ · d = (k₁ - k₂) · d

Since k₁ and k₂ are integers, their difference (k₁ - k₂) is also an integer, say k₃. Thus:

a - b = k₃ · d

Therefore, d divides (a - b) with zero remainder. Q.E.D.

LAQ 3 • 5 Marks Duality Theorem Proof & The 3-Number Fallacy

Question: (i) State the Fundamental Duality Theorem for two numbers and prove it using prime exponent representation. (ii) Disprove the claim HCF(a, b, c) × LCM(a, b, c) = a × b × c using a concrete counterexample. (iii) State the correct algebraic identity for three numbers. (5 Marks)

1
Step 1: Statement & Proof for 2 Numbers

Statement: For any two positive integers a and b, HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b.

Proof: Let a = ∏ pᵃⁱ and b = ∏ pᵇⁱ.
HCF(a, b) = ∏ p^min(a_i, b_i)
LCM(a, b) = ∏ p^max(a_i, b_i)
HCF × LCM = ∏ p^[min(a_i, b_i) + max(a_i, b_i)]
Since min(x, y) + max(x, y) = x + y for all real numbers:
HCF × LCM = ∏ p^(a_i + b_i) = (∏ pᵃⁱ) × (∏ pᵇⁱ) = a × b. Q.E.D.
2
Step 2: Concrete Counterexample for 3 Numbers
Consider a = 4, b = 6, c = 8:
4 = 2², 6 = 2 × 3, 8 = 2³
HCF(4, 6, 8) = 2
LCM(4, 6, 8) = 2³ × 3 = 24
HCF × LCM = 2 × 24 = 48
Product a × b × c = 4 × 6 × 8 = 192
Since 48 ≠ 192, the statement is DISPROVED!
3
Step 3: True 3-Number Formula
LCM(a, b, c) = [ a · b · c · HCF(a, b, c) ] / [ HCF(a, b) · HCF(b, c) · HCF(c, a) ]
4
Marking Rubric (5 Marks)

  • Counterexample with explicit computation: [2 Marks]
  • True 3-number identity formulation: [1 Mark]

Case Study 2 Odisha State Disaster Relief Logistics (Balasore Relief Operations)

Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Arm yourself against the 5 most lethal student examination blunders, and reinforce mastery with 15 crisp, high-retention rapid revision checkpoints.

5 Fatal Exam Hall Traps & How to Neutralize Them

Trap 1: The "HCF of Consecutive Even Numbers is 1" Blunder
The Fatal Student Error

Students read "consecutive" and automatically write HCF = 1, mistakenly treating consecutive even numbers (e.g. 14 and 16) like consecutive counting numbers (e.g. 14 and 15).

Why Students Stumble

They remember the rule "Consecutive numbers are co-prime" without noticing the qualifier EVEN.

The Bulletproof Fix

Consecutive numbers: (n, n + 1) → HCF = 1.
Consecutive EVEN numbers: (2k, 2k + 2) → Both numbers are even, so 2 MUST be a common factor! Difference is 2. HCF is strictly 2!

Trap 2: The "Three-Number Product Identity" Fallacy
The Fatal Student Error

Students extend the two-number formula to three numbers: HCF(a, b, c) × LCM(a, b, c) = a × b × c.

Why Students Stumble

Superficial generalization: assuming algebraic laws for pairs automatically hold for triplets.

The Bulletproof Fix

Never apply the product rule to 3 numbers! Remember the 4, 6, 8 test: HCF(4, 6, 8) = 2, LCM = 24. HCF × LCM = 48, but 4 × 6 × 8 = 192 (48 ≠ 192!). The product identity is valid ONLY FOR TWO NUMBERS.

Trap 3: The "Tolls Together" Off-By-One Omission
The Fatal Student Error

When asked how many times bells ring together in 30 minutes with an LCM of 2 minutes, students calculate 30 ÷ 2 = 15 and stop there, losing the final mark!

Why Students Stumble

They calculate the number of elapsed intervals between tolls, forgetting the toll that occurred at time t = 0!

The Bulletproof Fix

Always check if the problem states: "They toll together at the start" or "commence tolling together". If yes, the formula is: Total Tolls = (Total Time ÷ LCM) + 1. For 30 mins: 15 + 1 = 16 times!

Trap 4: The "Non-Factor HCF/LCM" Impossible Pair Blunder
The Fatal Student Error

Students are given HCF = 15 and LCM = 350 and waste 10 minutes trying to set up algebraic equations to find the numbers!

Why Students Stumble

They fail to verify the fundamental divisibility invariant: HCF must divide LCM without remainder!

The Bulletproof Fix

First check: Is LCM divisible by HCF? 350 ÷ 15 = 23.33 (not an integer!). Immediately write: "No such pair of numbers can exist because HCF must be a factor of LCM."

Trap 5: The "Partial Ladder Division" in 3-Number LCM
The Fatal Student Error

When finding the LCM of 3 numbers (e.g. 12, 15, 18), a student divides 12 and 18 by 2, and divides 15 by 2 too, writing 7.5 or dropping 15 completely!

Why Students Stumble

Confusion between HCF and LCM ladder rules. For HCF, a divisor must divide ALL numbers. For LCM, if a divisor divides only two numbers, the third number must simply be carried down unchanged!

The Bulletproof Fix

In an LCM ladder: if a number is not divisible by the chosen prime, bring it down exactly as it is to the next row. Stop only when all remaining quotients are pairwise co-prime!

15 High-Yield Rapid Revision Takeaways

  • 1. Prime Atoms: Primes are whole numbers > 1 with exactly two distinct factors (1 and itself). 1 is neither prime nor composite. 2 is the ONLY even prime!
  • 2. Unique Factorisation: Every composite integer has exactly one unique prime factorisation (Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic), regardless of the tree branching path.
  • 3. Factor Formation: Any factor of a number is formed by taking a subpart (subset) of its prime factorisation. If N = pᵃ · qᵇ, total factors = (a + 1)(b + 1).
  • 4. HCF Rule: HCF is the largest common factor. In prime exponent form, take the MINIMUM power of all common prime factors: HCF = ∏ p^min(a_i, b_i).
  • 5. LCM Rule: LCM is the smallest common multiple. In prime exponent form, take the MAXIMUM power of every prime appearing in any number: LCM = ∏ p^max(a_i, b_i).
  • 6. Ladder Division: In the simultaneous division ladder, the vertical left column multiplied gives the HCF; the full L-shaped perimeter (left column × bottom co-prime row) gives the LCM.
  • 7. The Product Duality Law: For any TWO positive integers: HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b. (Never apply to 3 or more numbers!).
  • 8. Divisibility Hierarchy: The HCF of two numbers is always an exact divisor of their LCM. LCM mod HCF = 0 is a necessary condition for existence.
  • 9. Consecutive Numbers: Any two consecutive integers (n, n + 1) are co-prime: HCF = 1 and LCM = n(n + 1).
  • 10. Consecutive Even Numbers: Any two consecutive even integers (2k, 2k + 2) have HCF = 2 and LCM = 2k(k + 1) = (Product) / 2.
  • 11. Consecutive Odd Numbers: Any two consecutive odd integers (2k + 1, 2k + 3) are co-prime: HCF = 1 and LCM = (2k + 1)(2k + 3).
  • 12. Factor-Multiple Pairs: If a divides b, then HCF(a, b) = a (the smaller factor) and LCM(a, b) = b (the larger multiple).
  • 13. Scaling Multipliers: If both numbers are multiplied by c, both HCF and LCM are multiplied by c: HCF(ca, cb) = c · HCF(a, b) and LCM(ca, cb) = c · LCM(a, b).
  • 14. Mahaviracharya's Niruddha: To add or subtract fractions with distinct denominators, compute the LCM of all denominators (Niruddha), scale the numerators, and sum them over the common denominator.
  • 15. Word Problem Decoders: Look for keywords:
    • "Largest tile", "maximum capacity", "greatest length", "dividing equal packets" → Calculate HCF.
    • "Meet again", "toll together", "revolve in orbits", "minimum identical toran length" → Calculate LCM.
