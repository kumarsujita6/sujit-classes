Chapter 3: Climates of India

From tropical heat to Himalayan snows: understanding the six seasons (Ritus), the South-West and North-East Monsoons, harvest festivals, natural disasters, and global climate change.

Reading Time: 32 mins Full NCERT Solutions Monsoon Mechanics

Visual Mind Map & Chapter Overview

While weather can change from one hour to the next, climate represents the long-term personality of the atmosphere—the average weather pattern recorded over a span of 30 to 35 years!

India is internationally classified as having a Tropical Monsoon Climate. Yet, within our borders, you can find the frozen alpine tundra of Ladakh, the scorching arid dunes of Jaisalmer, the drenching rainforests of Meghalaya, and the balmy, breeze-swept coconut shores of Kerala. Let us explore the natural forces that orchestrate this wonderful climate tapestry.

The Big Questions of Chapter 3

1. What makes India's climate so incredibly diverse? (The interplay of latitude, mountain walls, elevation, ocean breezes, and seasonal monsoon reversals).

2. How does the monsoon rhythm govern our agriculture, culture, and festivals? (Over 70% of Indian farms depend on monsoon rain, inspiring harvest celebrations from Baisakhi to Pongal and Nuakhai).

3. How is global climate change impacting India? (Rising temperatures, shrinking glaciers, unpredictable droughts, and intensified coastal cyclones).

Exhaustive Concept Explanations

1. Weather, Seasons, and Climate: Key Distinctions

Young students often use the words weather and climate interchangeably, but they mean completely different things:

Concept Time Scale Area Covered Example
Weather Short-term: Hour-to-hour or day-to-day. Highly variable. Small, local area (e.g., a city or town). "It is 32°C and drizzling in Bhubaneswar this afternoon."
Seasons (Ritus) Medium-term: Predictable 2 to 3 month cycles repeating every year. Broad regional or national scale. Summer (Grishma), Monsoon (Varsha), Winter (Shishira).
Climate Long-term: Statistical average of weather over 30 to 35+ years. Vast geographical territory or subcontinent. "India has a Tropical Monsoon Climate with hot summers and wet monsoons."
India's Traditional Six Seasons (Shad Ritu)

In Indian classical tradition, the year is divided into six distinct two-month seasons (Ritus):

  1. Vasanta (Spring): March–April (Chaitra–Vaishakha) — pleasant flowers bloom, celebrated with Holi.
  2. Grishma (Summer): May–June (Jyeshtha–Ashadha) — hot days, mango season.
  3. Varsha (Monsoon): July–August (Shravana–Bhadrapada) — rain clouds fill rivers and quench parched fields.
  4. Sharad (Autumn): September–October (Ashvina–Kartika) — clear blue skies, festive season (Durga Puja, Diwali).
  5. Hemanta (Pre-Winter): November–December (Margashirsha–Pausha) — crisp, cool mornings.
  6. Shishira (Winter): January–February (Magha–Phalguna) — chilly winter winds, harvest festivals (Makar Sankranti).

2. The Five Key Factors Determining India's Climate

(a) Latitude: The Tropic of Cancer

The Tropic of Cancer (23°30' N) passes through the middle of eight Indian states (Gujarat, Rajasthan, MP, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura, Mizoram).

  • The region south of the Tropic of Cancer lies in the Tropical Zone. Here, the sun is high overhead all year, resulting in warm or hot temperatures throughout the year with no real winter (e.g., Chennai, Bengaluru, Kochi).
  • The region north of the Tropic of Cancer lies in the Subtropical Zone. It experiences severe seasonal extremes—very hot summers (up to 48°C) and freezing cold winters (down to 2°C in Delhi or Punjab).

(b) Altitude (Height Above Sea Level)

As you go higher up in the atmosphere, air pressure and density decrease. The thinner air cannot trap heat effectively. On average, temperature drops by about 6.5°C for every 1,000 metres of ascent (known scientifically as the Normal Lapse Rate).

Why Ooty is Cold while Coimbatore is Hot!

Look at the map of Tamil Nadu: Udhagamandalam (Ooty) and Coimbatore are situated at almost the exact same latitude, just 80 km apart!

Yet, on a hot May afternoon, Coimbatore swelters at 34°C, while Ooty is a chilly and refreshing 16°C. Why? Because Coimbatore sits in the low plains at 410 metres, whereas Ooty is perched high up in the Nilgiri Hills at 2,240 metres above sea level!

(c) Proximity to the Ocean (Continentality)

Land and water heat up and cool down at completely different rates!

  • Land heats up quickly and cools down quickly.
  • Water heats up very slowly and cools down very slowly.
  • Equable / Maritime Climate: Coastal cities like Mumbai, Chennai, Panaji, and Puri enjoy the moderating influence of the ocean. Daytime sea breezes and nighttime land breezes keep temperature fluctuations small. In Mumbai, the summer temperature is around 33°C and winter temperature is around 25°C—a difference of only 8°C!
  • Extreme / Continental Climate: Interior cities located far from the sea (like Delhi, Jaipur, and Amritsar) receive no moderating ocean breeze. In Delhi, summer temperatures soar past 45°C while winter nights plunge down to 3°C—a staggering temperature range of over 42°C!

(d) Relief & Mountain Barriers

India's mighty mountain ranges act as colossal climate barriers:

  1. The Himalayas: Shield India from the bone-chilling, sub-zero winds blowing across Siberia and Central Asia in winter. Without them, north India would be a frozen desert. They also trap the moisture-rich summer monsoon winds, forcing them to release abundant rain over our plains.
  2. The Western Ghats: Stand directly across the path of Arabian Sea monsoon winds. The moist air is forced to rise up the western slopes, cooling and condensing into torrential orographic rainfall (over 300 cm in coastal Karnataka, Goa, Kerala). By the time the winds cross over the crest onto the Deccan Plateau (e.g., Pune, Solapur), they have lost their moisture, creating a semi-arid rain-shadow area!

3. The Indian Monsoon: Lifeblood of the Nation

The word Monsoon comes from the Arabic word Mawsim, which literally means "season". It describes a complete seasonal reversal of wind directions between summer and winter.

Figure 3.2: Comparison of South-West Summer Monsoon vs North-East Winter Retreating Monsoon.

Two Main Branches of the South-West Monsoon:

  1. Arabian Sea Branch: Hits the Kerala coast around June 1. It slams into the Western Ghats, dumping torrential rain on the coast, before traveling across the Deccan plateau and Gujarat into Rajasthan.
  2. Bay of Bengal Branch: Moves north-eastwards towards the coast of Myanmar and Bengal. It strikes the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya (causing the highest rainfall on Earth at Mawsynram and Cherrapunji) and is deflected westwards along the Ganga valley, watering Kolkata, Patna, Varanasi, and Delhi.

4. Weather, Farming, and India's Harvest Festivals

Because over 70% of Indian farmers depend on rain for their crops, our entire calendar of festivals mirrors the rhythm of the seasons:

  • Makar Sankranti / Pongal / Magh Bihu: Celebrated in mid-January when the Sun enters the northern zodiac (Uttarayana). Farmers harvest their winter crops (sugarcane, rice, sesame) and cook festive sweets.
  • Baisakhi (Punjab & Haryana): Celebrated in April marking the harvesting of the golden winter wheat (Rabi) crop.
  • Onam (Kerala): Celebrated in August–September to welcome King Mahabali and celebrate the bountiful monsoon harvest of paddy.
  • Nuakhai (Odisha): Celebrated in August–September (the day after Ganesh Chaturthi) to welcome the new season's rice crop (Nua means new, Khai means food). Families offer the first grains to Goddess Samaleswari.

5. Natural Disasters Linked to Climate

When weather systems reach extreme limits, they can cause devastating disasters:

Disaster Causes Key Impacts Safety & Mitigation Measures
Tropical Cyclones Intense low-pressure systems forming over warm sea water (>27°C) in Bay of Bengal & Arabian Sea, generating destructive spiral winds (>120 km/h). Massive tidal storm surges drown coastal villages, uproot trees, snap power lines, and destroy crops. Construction of cyclone shelters, mangrove belt preservation, early Doppler radar warnings, timely mass evacuation.
Floods Incessant torrential monsoon downpours, silted riverbeds, or sudden Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOF) in mountain valleys. Submergence of homes, loss of livestock, waterborne disease epidemics (cholera), contamination of drinking wells. Embankments, desilting of rivers, rainwater harvesting dams, preserving wetlands, not constructing on natural floodplains.
Landslides Heavy rain saturating unstable mountain soil and rocks on steep slopes, worsened by deforestation and unscientific road cutting. Blocked national highways, buried mountain villages, crushed vehicles, disrupted communications. Afforestation with deep-rooted trees, building retaining walls, installing early warning sensors, avoiding slope cutting.
Droughts Prolonged failure or delayed arrival of the summer monsoon rains over consecutive years. Depleted groundwater tables, dried wells, crop withering, shortage of fodder for cattle, farmer distress. Check dams, drip and sprinkler irrigation, rooftop rainwater harvesting (Tankas), cultivating drought-resistant millets (Bajra, Jowar).

6. Global Climate Change: The Greenhouse Dilemma

The natural Greenhouse Effect is essential for life: trace gases like carbon dioxide (CO2), water vapour, and methane act like a warm blanket, trapping outgoing infrared heat radiated by the Earth. Without this natural blanket, Earth's average temperature would be a freezing −18°C!

However, since the Industrial Revolution, humans have burned trillions of tonnes of fossil fuels (coal in power plants, petrol and diesel in vehicles) and chopped down ancient forests.

  • This has pumped billions of extra tonnes of CO2 into the atmosphere, making the heat blanket thicker and thicker.
  • The result is Global Warming—our entire planet is heating up at an unnatural, dangerous speed!
Real Consequences Already Visible in India

In early 2025 and 2026, temperatures across central and northern India reached 1°C to 3°C above normal. Winters are becoming shorter and milder, causing wheat plants to mature prematurely with smaller grains. Himalayan glaciers (which feed the Ganga and Indus) are retreating rapidly, increasing the danger of flash floods followed by summer water shortages!

What Can We Do? (Mitigation & Adaptation):

  1. Renewable Energy: Shifting to solar panels and wind energy instead of burning dirty coal.
  2. Mass Afforestation: Planting native trees that naturally absorb atmospheric CO2 through photosynthesis.
  3. Mission LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment): India's global initiative encouraging citizens to save electricity, turn off running taps, walk or bicycle short distances, and eliminate single-use plastics.

Rapid Revision Fact & Technical Term Bank

1. Climate
The composite statistical average of weather patterns and atmospheric conditions recorded over a prolonged period of 30 to 35+ years.
2. Normal Lapse Rate
The steady decrease in air temperature with increasing altitude, averaging a drop of about 6.5°C per 1,000 metres of elevation.
3. Continentality
The climatic condition experienced by inland areas located far from oceans, characterised by extreme summer heat and severe winter cold.
4. Monsoon
Derived from Arabic Mawsim (season); a seasonal wind system that reverses its direction completely between summer and winter.
5. Orographic Rainfall
Precipitation produced when moisture-laden winds are forced to rise over a mountain barrier, cooling and condensing on the windward side.
6. Rain Shadow Area
A dry, sheltered region on the leeward (opposite) side of a mountain range that receives very little rain because winds have already shed their moisture.
7. Tropical Cyclone
A ferocious low-pressure storm system with spiraling gale-force winds and heavy rain that forms over warm tropical oceans.
8. Glacial Burst (GLOF)
A catastrophic flash flood occurring when a natural dam holding back a high-altitude glacial lake suddenly breaches and empties.
9. Greenhouse Gases
Atmospheric gases such as carbon dioxide, water vapour, and methane that trap outgoing terrestrial infrared heat, warming the planet.
10. Climate Mitigation
Deliberate actions taken to reduce or prevent the emission of greenhouse gases, such as transitioning to solar power and planting forests.

In-Text Activity (Page 7): Udhagamandalam (Ooty) and Coimbatore are at almost the same latitude. Why are their temperatures so different?

Answer:

  • Role of Altitude (Elevation): Although both cities share roughly the same latitude (approx. 11° N), Coimbatore is situated in the plains at an altitude of about 410 metres, while Ooty is perched high in the Nilgiri Hills at 2,240 metres above sea level.
  • Normal Lapse Rate: For every 1,000 metres of height, temperature falls by roughly 6.5°C. With an altitude difference of about 1,830 metres, Ooty is naturally around 12°C to 15°C cooler than Coimbatore!

Exercise Q2 (a): What is the difference between weather and climate?

Answer:

  • Weather: Refers to the short-term, day-to-day state of the atmosphere (such as temperature, rain, or humidity) over a small local area. It can change rapidly from morning to evening.
  • Climate: Refers to the long-term average of weather patterns recorded over a large region over at least 30 to 35 years. It represents the permanent climatic character of a country.
Exercise Q2 (b): Why do places near the ocean have milder temperatures than places far away from it?

Answer:

  • Water has a high specific heat capacity—it heats up much more slowly and cools down much more slowly than dry land.
  • During hot summer days, cool air from the sea blows onto the land as a sea breeze, preventing coastal cities from becoming burning hot.
  • During cold winter nights, warm air from the sea moves towards the land, keeping it pleasantly mild. This moderating effect gives coastal regions an equable maritime climate.
Exercise Q2 (c): What role do monsoon winds play in affecting India's climate?

Answer:

  1. Brings Major Annual Rainfall: The South-West Monsoon delivers more than 75% of India's total yearly rainfall between June and September, filling our rivers, lakes, and reservoirs.
  2. Agricultural Backbone: It dictates the entire cropping calendar (Kharif crops like rice, maize, cotton, and pulses).
  3. Temperature Relief: The arrival of monsoon showers cools down scorching summer heat across the country.
  4. Winter Rain for South-East: The Retreating North-East Monsoon provides critical winter rainfall to the coast of Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh.
Exercise Q2 (d): Why is Chennai warm or hot throughout the year, while Leh is cold?

Answer:

  • Latitude: Chennai is located near the southern tip of India at 13° N (close to the equator in the Tropical Zone), where solar rays strike directly throughout the year. Leh is located far north at 34° N in the Subtropical Zone, where sun rays strike at an oblique, weaker angle.
  • Altitude: Chennai is at sea level (6 metres), wrapped in warm, humid coastal air. Leh is situated at a staggering altitude of over 3,500 metres in the Ladakh plateau, where thin alpine air loses heat rapidly.

Exercise Q7: Short essay on a Natural Disaster (Cyclones): Causes, impacts, and mitigation actions.

Essay: Tropical Cyclones on India's Coast

1. Causes: Tropical cyclones form over warm sea surfaces (temperatures above 27°C) in the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea during pre-monsoon (April–May) and post-monsoon (October–November) periods. Low atmospheric pressure pulls in warm, moist air that spirals upwards at tremendous speeds due to the Coriolis effect, creating a giant vortex with winds exceeding 150 km/h.

2. Impacts: Cyclones trigger destructive storm surges (giant walls of seawater crashing onto the shore), flattening thatch huts, uprooting ancient banyan trees, breaking electrical poles, contaminating freshwater ponds with salt, and ruining standing paddy crops.

3. Action Plan to Reduce Impacts:

  • Individual Level: Keep emergency battery radios, torches, and dry food handy; avoid going near beaches; strictly heed evacuation warnings.
  • Community Level: Manage community cyclone shelters; conduct mock rescue drills; protect mangrove belts along estuaries (e.g., Bhitarkanika in Odisha) that act as natural wave breakers.
  • Government Level: Track storms using INSAT satellites and Doppler weather radars; send automated SMS alerts; construct pucca disaster shelters; preposition NDRF and ODRAF rescue teams.

Exam Hall Traps & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Common Exam Hall Traps to Avoid!
  • Trap 1: Chennai Winter vs North India Winter: In North India, summer brings rain and winter is dry. In Tamil Nadu / Coromandel Coast, winter is the rainiest season due to the North-East Retreating Monsoon!
  • Trap 2: Natural vs Enhanced Greenhouse Effect: Do not write that the greenhouse effect is bad! The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth comfortably warm for living beings. It is the man-made excess greenhouse gases that trigger harmful global warming.
  • Trap 3: Western Ghats Rain Shadow: The western slope of the Western Ghats gets over 300 cm of rain, but the eastern/leeward slope (the Deccan Plateau) is a dry rain-shadow zone!
  • Trap 4: Tropical vs Subtropical India: The dividing line is the Tropic of Cancer (23.5° N), NOT the Equator!

15 High-Yield Revision Points

  1. Climate Definition: The 30 to 35-year statistical average of weather patterns in a region.
  2. India's Climate Classification: Officially classified as Tropical Monsoon Climate.
  3. Six Indian Ritus: Vasanta (Spring), Grishma (Summer), Varsha (Monsoon), Sharad (Autumn), Hemanta (Pre-winter), Shishira (Winter).
  4. Tropic of Cancer: Divides India into a warm tropical southern half and a seasonal subtropical northern half.
  5. Normal Lapse Rate: Temperature drops ~6.5°C per 1,000 m ascent, making high-altitude hill stations pleasantly cool.
  6. Maritime vs Continental: Oceans moderate coastal temperatures; interior continental regions experience extreme temperature swings.
  7. Himalayan Climate Shield: Blocks freezing Siberian winter winds and traps summer monsoon moisture.
  8. Western Ghats Orographic Rain: Moist Arabian Sea winds rise up western slopes, dropping over 300 cm of rain.
  9. Monsoon Meaning: Derived from Arabic Mawsim (seasonal wind reversal).
  10. South-West Monsoon: Advances from Kerala on June 1, bringing over 75% of India's annual rain.
  11. Retreating Monsoon: North-East winds blowing from land to sea that bring winter downpours to coastal Tamil Nadu.
  12. Harvest Festivals: Makar Sankranti, Pongal, Baisakhi, Onam, and Nuakhai celebrate seasonal farm yields.
  13. Cyclones: Low-pressure sea storms with winds >120 km/h and devastating coastal surges.
  14. Greenhouse Gases: CO2 and methane trap infrared heat; burning coal and oil triggers dangerous global warming.
  15. Mission LiFE: India's global community movement promoting environment-friendly daily habits to combat climate change.
