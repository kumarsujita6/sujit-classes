1.1 A Lakh Varieties! How Big is One Lakh?

The chapter opens in a market in Chintamani, Karnataka. Farmer Eshwarappa overhears that India once had over one lakh varieties of rice! Today, farmers grow only a few dozen. This makes us wonder: How big is one lakh?

The Discovery Pattern of One Lakh:
• Largest 1-digit number: 9. Add 1 ⇒ 10 (smallest 2-digit number)
• Largest 2-digit number: 99. Add 1 ⇒ 100 (smallest 3-digit number)
• Largest 3-digit number: 999. Add 1 ⇒ 1,000 (smallest 4-digit number)
• Largest 4-digit number: 9,999. Add 1 ⇒ 10,000 (smallest 5-digit number, Ten Thousand)
• Largest 5-digit number: 99,999. Add 1 ⇒ 1,00,000 (smallest 6-digit number, One Lakh!)

1.2 Land of Tens: The Button Machine

Imagine a digital cash register with special buttons. Each button adds or subtracts a fixed power of 10. How can we make any number with the least number of button clicks?

  • Creative Chitti Machine: Has buttons for +1, +10, +100, +1000, +10000, etc., and also -1, -10, -100, etc.
  • Example: Make 2,990.
    • Additive method: 2 × 1000 + 9 × 100 + 9 × 10 = 2 + 9 + 9 = 20 clicks.
    • Smart Subtractive method: 3 × 1000 − 1 × 10 = 3 + 1 = only 4 clicks!
Teacher's Button Hack: Whenever a digit is 6, 7, 8, or 9, jumping to the next higher place value and subtracting saves many clicks! For example, 90 is 9 clicks of +10, but 1 click of +100 minus 1 click of 10 takes only 2 clicks!

1.3 Indian vs. International Place Value System

How do people in India and other countries place commas in large numbers? Let's compare them side-by-side:

Feature Indian System (Lakhs & Crores) International System (Millions & Billions)
Grouping / Periods First group of 3 (ones, tens, hundreds), then groups of 2 (thousands, lakhs, crores) Groups of 3 digits throughout (ones, thousands, millions, billions)
Comma Pattern 3, 2, 2, 2 (from right to left) 3, 3, 3, 3 (from right to left)

1.4 Exact and Approximate Values (Estimation)

In real life, we rarely need exact figures when talking about huge crowds, stadium attendees, or municipal budgets. We round off numbers to make them easier to remember and communicate.

Golden Rule of Rounding Off: Look at the digit immediately to the right of the target place:
• If that digit is less than 5 (0, 1, 2, 3, 4): Round down (keep target digit unchanged, change all digits to the right to 0).
• If that digit is 5 or more (5, 6, 7, 8, 9): Round up (increase target digit by 1, change all digits to the right to 0).

1.5 Mental Math Patterns in Products

Multiplying large numbers by 10, 20, 25, 50, or 100 can be done in your head in seconds using friendly fractions:

• To multiply by 50: Multiply by 100, then divide by 2 ⇒ 28 × 50 = (28 × 100) ÷ 2 = 2800 ÷ 2 = 1,400.
• To multiply by 25: Multiply by 100, then divide by 4 ⇒ 36 × 25 = (36 × 100) ÷ 4 = 3600 ÷ 4 = 900.
• To multiply by 125: Multiply by 1000, then divide by 8 ⇒ 64 × 125 = (64 × 1000) ÷ 8 = 64000 ÷ 8 = 8,000.

Activity 1: Population of Chintamani (Textbook Page 3)

Problem 1: According to the 2011 Census, the population of Chintamani was about 75,000. How much less than one lakh is 75,000?
Step 1: One Lakh = 1,00,000. Given population = 75,000.
Step 2: Difference = 1,00,000 − 75,000.
Step 3: 1,00,000 − 75,000 = 25,000.
✓ Answer: 75,000 is 25,000 less than one lakh.
Problem 2: The estimated population of Chintamani in 2024 is 1,06,000. How much more than one lakh is this?
Step 1: Estimated population = 1,06,000. Target benchmark = 1,00,000.
Step 2: Excess = 1,06,000 − 1,00,000.
✓ Answer: It is 6,000 more than one lakh.
Problem 3: By how much did the population of Chintamani increase from 2011 to 2024?
Step 1: Increase = Population in 2024 − Population in 2011 = 1,06,000 − 75,000.
Step 2: 1,06,000 − 75,000 = 31,000.
✓ Answer: The population increased by 31,000 people.

Activity 2: Button Machine Challenges (Textbook Page 5-7)

Find the least number of button clicks to make 4,850 using Creative Chitti:

Method A: Pure Addition = 4 × (+1000) + 8 × (+100) + 5 × (+10) ⇒ 4 + 8 + 5 = 17 clicks.
Method B: Smarter Subtraction = 5 × (+1000) − 1 × (+100) − 5 × (+10) ⇒ 5 + 1 + 5 = 11 clicks.
Method C: Even Smarter = 5 × (+1000) − 2 × (+100) + 5 × (+10) = 4850 ⇒ 5 + 2 + 5 = 12 clicks.
✓ Conclusion: Method B gives the least clicks (11 button clicks).

Q2. Comparing Large Numbers with ‘<’, ‘>’ or ‘=’ (Page 30)
(i) 10 Lakhs _____ 1 Million ⇒ Both represent 1,000,000. Therefore: 10 Lakhs = 1 Million.
(ii) 1 Crore _____ 9 Million ⇒ 1 Crore = 10 Million. Since 10 > 9: 1 Crore > 9 Million.
(iii) 99,99,999 _____ 1,00,00,000 ⇒ 99,99,999 has 7 digits, while 1,00,00,000 has 8 digits. Therefore: 99,99,999 < 1,00,00,000.
(iv) 1 Billion _____ 100 Crores ⇒ 1 Billion = 1,000,000,000 = 100 × 10,000,000 = 100 Crores. Therefore: 1 Billion = 100 Crores.
Q3. Population Growth Analysis (Page 31-32)

Problem: In the 2001 census, city A had a population of 4,80,000. In 2011, it grew to 9,45,000. Did the population double? What was the estimated increase?

Step 1: Double of 2001 population = 2 × 4,80,000 = 9,60,000.
Step 2: Actual 2011 population = 9,45,000. Since 9,45,000 < 9,60,000, it did not quite double, but came extremely close!
Step 3: Absolute increase = 9,45,000 − 4,80,000 = 4,65,000.
✓ Answer: The population almost doubled (fell short by only 15,000) with an increase of 4,65,000 people.
Q4. Strike Out Digits Puzzle (Page 35)

Puzzle: Strike out 10 digits from the 20-digit number 12345123451234512345 so that the remaining 10 digits form the largest possible number.

Strategy: To make a number as large as possible, we want the highest digits (5, then 4, 3...) at the highest place values (furthest to the left).
Execution: Look at the 4 repeating blocks: [12345] [12345] [12345] [12345].
• Strike out 1, 2, 3, 4 from block 1 (4 digits) ⇒ Leading digit becomes 5.
• Strike out 1, 2, 3, 4 from block 2 (4 digits) ⇒ Next digit becomes 5.
• We have struck 8 digits so far. We must strike 2 more digits from block 3. Strike 1 and 2 (2 digits) ⇒ Remaining digits in block 3 are 345.
• Keep all 5 digits of block 4 intact: 12345.
✓ The largest possible 10-digit number is: 5534512345 (5,53,45,12,345).

Quick Revision Notes & Common Pitfalls

Key points, formulas, and common mistakes to avoid in exams.

Don't Make This Mistake in Exams:
• Wrong Comma Placement: Writing 1,234,567 in an Indian system question is marked incorrect! Indian system must be 12,34,567.
• Confusing Millions with Crores: Remember that 10 Million is 1 Crore, NOT 1 Million!
• Forgetting Zeroes in Expanded Form: In 5,04,020, do not omit place values. Expanded form = (5 × 1,00,000) + (4 × 1,000) + (2 × 10).
Super Summary of Chapter 1:
1. Smallest 6-digit number = 1,00,000 (One Lakh).
2. Smallest 8-digit number = 1,00,00,000 (One Crore).
3. Indian comma rule = 3, 2, 2, 2 • International comma rule = 3, 3, 3, 3.
4. 1 Million = 10 Lakhs • 1 Billion = 100 Crores.
5. Rounding off: Check the next digit to the right: ≥ 5 rounds UP, < 5 stays SAME.
