2.1 Fraction Multiplication: From Repeated Addition to Scaling

Let us begin with a simple comparison. Imagine two walkers setting out on a morning trail:

  • Aaron walks at normal speed: He covers 3 kilometres in 1 hour. In 5 hours, how far can he walk? As every student knows, we multiply: Distance = 5 × 3 km = 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 15 km. Here, multiplication is simply repeated addition of the whole number 3.
  • Aaron's pet tortoise walks at a much slower pace: It crawls only 14 kilometre in 1 hour. How far does it crawl in 3 hours? Even though the distance covered in 1 hour is a fraction, the mathematical relationship remains identical: Distance = 3 × 14 km = 14 + 14 + 14 = 34 km.
What if Time Itself is a Fraction of an Hour?

Now suppose Aaron walks 3 km in 1 hour. How far does he walk in 15 hour?

We cannot easily use "repeated addition" here because we are not adding 3 multiple times; instead, we are taking a fractional part of 3!

  • In 1 whole hour, Aaron covers 3 km.
  • In 15 of an hour, he covers the distance obtained by dividing 3 km into 5 equal parts: 3 ÷ 5 = 35 km.
  • Therefore: 15 × 3 = 35 km.

Now, what if he walks for 25 hours? Since 25 is twice 15, the distance must be twice 35 km: Distance = 2 × 35 = 65 km = 115 km.

2.2 The General Rule: Multiplying a Whole Number by a Fraction

Whenever we multiply a whole number W and a fraction ab:

The Numerator Scaling Principle

Step 1: Regard the whole number W as a fraction with denominator 1: W = W1.
Step 2: Multiply the whole number directly by the numerator, leaving the denominator unchanged:

W × ab = W × ab

Example: A farmer has 5 grandchildren and gives each grandchild 23 acre of farmland. Total land given = 5 × 23 = 5 × 23 = 103 = 313 acres.

2.3 Multiplying Two Fractions: The Unit Square Area Model

What happens when both numbers in the multiplication are fractions? For example, if the tortoise crawls 14 km in 1 hour, how far will it crawl in 12 an hour?

Mathematically, this is 12 × 14. In words, this asks: "What is half of one-quarter?"

To see this geometrically, let us take a standard unit square (a square of side 1 unit and area 1 square unit):

  1. First, divide the unit square into 4 equal vertical strips. Each strip represents 14 of the whole.
  2. Next, divide each vertical strip into 2 equal horizontal parts by drawing a horizontal midline.
  3. The entire unit square is now divided into 4 × 2 = 8 equal rectangular pieces.
  4. One such shaded piece represents 18 of the unit square.
  5. Therefore: 12 × 14 = 18!

Now let us generalize this: What is 1b × 1d? If a square is divided into b vertical columns and d horizontal rows, the total number of equal sub-rectangles created is b × d. Exactly one sub-rectangle has sides 1b and 1d. Hence, the area of this tiny rectangle is:

Fundamental Unit Fraction Product Law

1b × 1d = 1b × d

Example: 112 × 118 = 112 × 18 = 1216.

2.4 Brahmagupta's Universal Product Formula (628 CE)

Building upon the area model, let us multiply any two arbitrary fractions ab and cd. In 628 CE, the legendary Indian astronomer and mathematician Brahmagupta stated this fundamental law in verse 12.1.3 of his masterpiece Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta:

"Multiplication of two or more fractions is obtained by taking the product of the numerators divided by the product of the denominators."
Brahmagupta's Formula for Multiplying Fractions

ab × cd = a × cb × d

This universal formula works seamlessly for all types of fractions:

  • Two proper fractions: 34 × 25 = 3 × 24 × 5 = 620 = 310.
  • Improper fractions: 54 × 32 = 5 × 34 × 2 = 158 = 178.
  • Whole number with fraction: Simply write 3 = 31, so 3 × 34 = 3 × 31 × 4 = 94 = 214.
  • Mixed fractions: MUST first be converted to improper fractions: 114 × 8 = 54 × 81 = 404 = 10.

2.5 Apavartana: Simplifying by Cancelling Common Factors

In ancient India, the process of reducing a fraction to its lowest terms was called Apavartana (ଅପବର୍ତ୍ତନ). This term was so famous and ingrained in Indian culture that around 150 CE, the renowned Jaina philosopher Umāsvāti used it as an everyday metaphor in his philosophical treatise Tattvārthasūtra!

Rather than multiplying giant numerators and denominators and struggling with long division later, we can cancel out common factors beforehand:

Apavartana in Action: Step-by-Step Cancellation

Problem: Evaluate 1415 × 2542.

The Hard Way (Multiplying first): 14 × 25 = 350, and 15 × 42 = 630. Then you must simplify 350630. This is slow and prone to arithmetic blunders!

The Apavartana Way (Cancelling first):

  1. Look at 14 (numerator) and 42 (denominator). Both share a common factor of 14 (14 ÷ 14 = 1, 42 ÷ 14 = 3).
  2. Look at 25 (numerator) and 15 (denominator). Both share a common factor of 5 (25 ÷ 5 = 5, 15 ÷ 5 = 3).
  3. Rewrite the simplified product: 1 × 53 × 3 = 59

With Apavartana, heavy multiplication becomes effortless 1-digit mental math!

2.6 The Great Magnitude Paradox: Does Multiplication Always Increase Value?

In primary school, children learn that "multiplication makes things bigger" (e.g., 3 × 5 = 15, which is greater than both 3 and 5). However, in the world of fractions, this naive rule completely breaks down!

Situation Characteristics of Multiplicands Concrete Example Product Value Behavioral Rule
Situation 1 Both numbers are greater than 1 43 × 4 163 = 513 Product is GREATER than both numbers.
(513 > 4 and 513 > 113)
Situation 2 Both numbers are between 0 and 1 (Proper fractions) 34 × 25 620 = 310 Product is LESS than BOTH numbers!
(310 < 34 and 310 < 25)
Situation 3 One number is between 0 and 1, and the other is greater than 1 34 × 5 154 = 334 Product is BETWEEN the two numbers.
(It is less than 5, but greater than 34)
Why Does Proper Fraction × Proper Fraction Shrink?

Think of multiplication by a proper fraction as taking a slice of a slice. If you have 12 of a cake, and you take 14 of that piece, you obviously have less than the half-cake, and also less than a quarter-cake! Multiplying by a number less than 1 scales down the quantity.

2.7 Commutative Property: Order of Multiplication Does Not Matter

Does 12 × 14 = 14 × 12? Yes! In both cases, the product is 18.

Geometrical Reason: The area of a rectangle depends only on its dimensions (Length × Breadth). Rotating a rectangle by 90° swaps its length and breadth, but its total enclosed area remains unchanged! Thus, for all fractions: ab × cd = cd × ab

2.8 Division of Fractions: The Missing Multiplier Technique

What does 12 ÷ 4 actually mean? In whole numbers, division can be restated as an inverse multiplication question: "What number must be multiplied by 4 to give 12?" 4 × ? = 12 ⇒ ? = 3.

NCERT Ganita Prakash applies this exact same insight to fractions!

Deriving Division of Fractions Step-by-Step

Inquiry 1: What is 1 ÷ 23?

Rewrite as multiplication: 23 × ? = 1.

What should we multiply by 23 to cancel out the numerator 2 and denominator 3 and leave 1? Clearly, we multiply by its inverted form 32!
Therefore: 1 ÷ 23 = 32.

Inquiry 2: What is 3 ÷ 23?

Rewrite as multiplication: 23 × ? = 3.

We already know that multiplying by 32 gives 1. To get 3, we simply multiply that result by 3:
? = 32 × 3 = 92 = 412.
Therefore: 3 ÷ 23 = 3 × 32 = 92.

Inquiry 3: What is 23 ÷ 35?

Rewrite as multiplication: 35 × ? = 23.

Multiply 35 by its reciprocal 53 to make 1, and then multiply by the target 23:
? = 53 × 23 = 109 = 119.

2.9 The Reciprocal (Multiplicative Inverse)

The reciprocal of a fraction is formed by swapping (inverting) its numerator and denominator.

Mathematical Definition of Reciprocal

Two non-zero numbers are called reciprocals (or multiplicative inverses) of each other if their product is equal to 1:

ab × ba = a × bb × a = 1

  • The reciprocal of 35 is 53.
  • The reciprocal of a whole number like 7 (which is 71) is 17.
  • The reciprocal of 1 is 1 itself.
  • CRITICAL EXAM TRAP: Does ZERO have a reciprocal?
    NO! Zero has no reciprocal because any number multiplied by 0 is 0, never 1! Division by zero is undefined in mathematics.
  • Reciprocal of a mixed fraction: To find the reciprocal of 234, you MUST first convert it to improper fraction 114. Its reciprocal is then 411. (Do NOT write 243!).

2.10 The Universal Division Algorithm (Bhāskara II & Brahmagupta)

In 1150 CE, Bhāskarāchārya (Bhāskara II) in verse 2.3.40 of his famous book Līlāvatī stated the general rule for dividing fractions:

"Division of one fraction by another is equivalent to multiplication of the first fraction by the reciprocal of the second."
The Two-Step Golden Rule of Fraction Division

To divide any fraction (Dividend) by another fraction (Divisor):

  1. Step 1: Find the reciprocal of the Divisor (invert the second fraction). Leave the Dividend (first fraction) completely untouched!
  2. Step 2: Multiply the Dividend by this reciprocal, applying Apavartana (cancellation) wherever possible.

ab ÷ cd = ab × dc = a × db × c

Example: 146 ÷ 73 = 146 × 37 = 2 × 12 × 1 = 1.

2.12 Ancient Indian Mathematical Heritage

The arithmetic of general (non-unit) fractions as used worldwide today is one of India's greatest gifts to human civilization. While ancient Egyptians restricted themselves almost exclusively to unit fractions (fractions with numerator 1 like 1/2, 1/3, 1/4), ancient Indian mathematicians worked effortlessly with general fractions having any numerator and denominator:

  • Baudhāyana's Śulbasūtra (c. 800 BCE): Ancient geometric manuals for constructing Vedic fire altars (vedis). As seen in Example 4 of the textbook, altar makers calculated exact numbers of square bricks having fractional sides (e.g., 15 unit sides) to cover an area of 712 square units.
  • Umāsvāti's Tattvārthasūtra (c. 150 CE): Referenced the process of Apavartana (cancelling common factors to lowest terms) as a well-known cultural metaphor.
  • Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE): Codified the modern formulas for addition, subtraction, multiplication (ac/bd), and division of fractions.
  • Bhāskara I's Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya (629 CE): Provided the first known geometric proof of fraction multiplication by dividing a square into a rectangular grid.
  • Śrīdharācārya's Pāṭīgaṇita (c. 750–850 CE): Formulated famous mental arithmetic problems involving sums of reciprocals: 1 ÷ (1/6) + 1 ÷ (1/10) + ... = 40.
  • Caturveda Pṛthūdakasvāmī (c. 860 CE): Solved the famous multi-fountain cistern problem in his commentary on Brahmagupta, showing how reciprocal filling rates add up.
  • Bhāskarāchārya II's Līlāvatī (1150 CE): Authored the famous witty puzzle of the miser's donation of a tiny fractional part of a silver dramma, reducing down to exactly 1 cowrie shell!
  • Global Transmission: Moroccan mathematician al-Hassār (c. 1192 CE) and Arab scholars transmitted Indian fraction theory to Europe, where it was adopted in the 17th century.

Q6 (A&R 1): Existence of the Reciprocal of Zero CBSE Solution

Assertion (A): The number 0 has no reciprocal (multiplicative inverse).

Reason (R): There is no real number which when multiplied by 0 gives the product 1, as division by 0 is undefined.

Correct Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: By definition, the reciprocal of x is a number y such that x × y = 1. Since 0 × y = 0 for all numbers y, no such number exists.

Q7 (A&R 2): Magnitude of Product of Proper Fractions CBSE Solution

Correct Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: Product = 635. Converting to common denominators: 25 = 1435, and 37 = 1535. Since 6/35 < 14/35 and 6/35 < 15/35, Assertion A is true, and Reason R explains the exact scaling behavior.

Correct Answer: (d) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) is true.

Explanation: 8 ÷ 14 = 8 × 4 = 32 (not 2!). Students who answer 2 mistakenly multiply 8 × 1/4. Thus, Assertion A is false, while Reason R is a true mathematical axiom.

Q9 (A&R 4): Commutativity of Fraction Multiplication vs Non-Commutativity of Division CBSE Solution

Assertion (A): While fraction multiplication is commutative, fraction division is strictly non-commutative.

Reason (R): For non-equal fractions p and q, p × q = q × p, but p ÷ q is the reciprocal of q ÷ p, meaning p ÷ q ≠ q ÷ p.

Correct Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: Example: 12 ÷ 13 = 32, whereas 13 ÷ 12 = 23. Since 3/2 ≠ 2/3, division does not satisfy commutativity.

Q10 (A&R 5): Reciprocal of an Improper Fraction CBSE Solution

Assertion (A): The reciprocal of any improper fraction (greater than 1) is always a proper fraction (less than 1).

Reason (R): In an improper fraction a/b, the numerator a > b. Inverting it gives b/a, where the new numerator is strictly less than the new denominator.

Correct Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: For example, the improper fraction 7/4 (> 1) has reciprocal 4/7 (< 1). Reason R gives the rigorous algebraic justification.

Question: The cost of 1 kg of apples is ₹80. What is the cost of 514 kg of apples?

Solution: 1. Rate = ₹80 per kg.
2. Quantity = 514 = 5 × 4 + 14 = 214 kg.
3. Cost = Rate × Quantity = 80 × 214.
4. Cancel 80 and 4: 80 ÷ 4 = 20.
5. Total Cost = 20 × 21 = ₹420.
Final Answer: ₹420
PYQ 2 (CBSE Exam Standard): Evaluating Compound Operation: [(3/5) ÷ (9/20)] × (6/7) Model Answer

Question: Simplify using BODMAS rules: [ 35 ÷ 920 ] × 67.

Solution: 1. First evaluate expression inside brackets: 35 ÷ 920 = 35 × 209.
Cancel 3 and 9 (gives 1 and 3); cancel 5 and 20 (gives 1 and 4):
Bracket value = 1 × 41 × 3 = 43.

2. Multiply by outer fraction: 43 × 67.
Cancel 6 and 3 (gives 2 and 1):
Final Value = 4 × 21 × 7 = 87 = 117.
Final Answer: 87 (or 117)
PYQ 3 (CBSE Exam Standard): Vessel Juice Pouring Capacity with Remainder Model Answer

Question: A juice container holds 412 litres of mango juice. Glasses of capacity 38 litre each are to be filled from it. How many glasses can be filled completely, and how much juice (if any) remains in the container?

Solution: 1. Total juice = 412 = 92 = 368 litres.
2. Glass capacity = 38 litre.
3. Number of glasses = 92 ÷ 38 = 92 × 83.
Cancel 9 and 3 (gives 3 and 1); cancel 8 and 2 (gives 4 and 1):
Glasses = 3 × 4 = 12 glasses.
4. Remainder = 0 litres (since 12 × 3/8 = 36/8 = 9/2 = 4 1/2 litres exactly).
Final Answer: Exactly 12 glasses can be filled completely; 0 litres left over.
PYQ 4 (NCERT Exemplar / BSE Odisha): Does Reciprocal Distribute Over Addition? Model Answer

Question: Find the reciprocal of [ 23 + 14 ]. Is it equal to [ Reciprocal(23) + Reciprocal(14) ]?

Solution: 1. First compute sum: 23 + 14 = 8 + 312 = 1112.
2. Reciprocal of the sum = 1211.

3. Now compute sum of individual reciprocals:
Reciprocal of 23 is 32.
Reciprocal of 14 is 4.
Sum of reciprocals = 32 + 4 = 3 + 82 = 112.

4. Compare: 1211 ≠ 112 (In fact, 12/11 ≈ 1.09, whereas 11/2 = 5.5!).
Therefore, the reciprocal operation does NOT distribute over addition.
Final Answer: Reciprocal is 1211. NO, it does NOT equal the sum of individual reciprocals!
PYQ 5 (Most Probable Exam Question): Multi-Operator Order of Operations with Mixed Numbers Model Answer

Question: Evaluate: 212 × 315 ÷ 135 − 12.

Solution: 1. Convert all mixed fractions to improper fractions:
212 = 52,    315 = 165,    135 = 85.
Expression: 52 × 165 ÷ 85 − 12.

2. In BODMAS, Division and Multiplication have equal priority and are evaluated from left to right.
Convert division to multiplication by inverting 85 into 58:
52 × 165 × 58 − 12.

3. Apply cancellation across the multiplication chain:
• Cancel 5 in first numerator with 5 in second denominator.
• 16 in numerator cancels with (2 × 8 = 16) in denominator!
Multiplication chain evaluates simply to 5.

4. Final Subtraction: 5 − 12 = 412 = 92 (or 4.5).
Final Answer: 412 (or 92 = 4.5)

The top 5 recurring traps that cost students marks in board examinations, accompanied by 15 high-speed bullet points for final revision.

6.1 Top 5 Exam Hall Traps & Student Blunders

Blunder 1: Adding Denominators When Multiplying Fractions

Common Student Error: Writing 13 × 14 = 17 (adding 3 + 4).
Correct Method: Multiplication requires multiplying denominators, not adding them! 13 × 14 = 1 × 13 × 4 = 112.

Blunder 2: Inverting the Dividend (First Number) in Division

Common Student Error: In 6 ÷ 23, inverting 6 to get 16 × 23 = 19.
Correct Method: ALWAYS keep the dividend (first number) unchanged and invert ONLY the divisor (second number)! 6 ÷ 23 = 6 × 32 = 9.

Blunder 3: Inverting Only the Fraction Part of a Mixed Number

Common Student Error: Writing the reciprocal of 213 as 231 = 2 × 3 = 6.
Correct Method: You MUST convert the mixed number to an improper fraction first: 213 = 73. Its reciprocal is then 37.

Blunder 4: Cancelling Across Addition or Subtraction Signs

Common Student Error: In 3 + 44, crossing out 4 with 4 to write 3.
Correct Method: Apavartana (cancellation) is valid ONLY across multiplication! 3 + 44 = 74 = 1.75 (not 3!).

Blunder 5: Assuming "Multiplication Always Increases and Division Always Decreases"

Common Student Error: Guessing that 12 × 14 is greater than 1/2, or that 6 ÷ 13 is smaller than 6.
Correct Method: Remember the universal magnitude law: Multiplying by a proper fraction shrinks value (1/8 < 1/2), while dividing by a proper fraction magnifies value (6 ÷ 1/3 = 18 > 6)!

6.2 15 Lightning Master Revision Points

  1. Multiplication as Scaling: Multiplying a whole number by a fraction W × (a/b) = (W × a)/b. Multiply the numerator by the integer; keep denominator unchanged.
  2. Unit Fraction Law: The product of two unit fractions is 1 divided by the product of their denominators: (1/b) × (1/d) = 1/(b × d).
  3. Brahmagupta's Rule (628 CE): (a/b) × (c/d) = (a × c)/(b × d). Numerators multiply together; denominators multiply together.
  4. Area Model: The area of a rectangle with fractional dimensions L and B equals their product L × B.
  5. Apavartana (Cancellation): Always divide out common factors between numerators and denominators BEFORE multiplying to work with small, clean numbers.
  6. Proper × Proper Fraction: The product of two proper fractions is ALWAYS strictly LESS than both individual fractions.
  7. Improper × Improper Fraction: The product of two improper fractions (both > 1) is ALWAYS strictly GREATER than both factors.
  8. Commutative Law: Fraction multiplication is strictly commutative: (a/b) × (c/d) = (c/d) × (a/b). Rotating a rectangle 90° preserves its area.
  9. Multiplicative Identity: Multiplying any fraction by 1 leaves its value unchanged.
  10. Reciprocal (Multiplicative Inverse): The reciprocal of a/b is b/a. Their product is always 1 (a/b × b/a = 1).
  11. Zero has No Reciprocal: 0 has no multiplicative inverse because division by zero is undefined.
  12. Reciprocal of Mixed Number: Always convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before flipping! (Reciprocal of 2 1/3 is 3/7, not 2 3/1).
  13. Division Algorithm (Bhāskara II, 1150 CE): To divide fractions, multiply the dividend by the reciprocal of the divisor: (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c).
  14. Division Magnitude Law: When the divisor is a proper fraction (< 1), the quotient is strictly GREATER than the dividend (a ÷ 1/4 = 4a).
  15. Universal Telescoping Product: In the product chain (1 − 1/2)(1 − 1/3)...(1 − 1/n), all intermediate terms collapse diagonally, leaving exactly 1/n.
