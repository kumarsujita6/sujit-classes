2.1 The Soul of Indian Hospitality: Why India Became a Global Haven

Why did India, unlike many other ancient and medieval civilisations, welcome persecuted strangers from foreign lands with open arms? The answer lies not merely in geographic abundance or economic trade routes, but in the core civilisational philosophy that has animated the Indian mind for millennia.

In most parts of the ancient world, people from other lands, languages, or religions were viewed with deep suspicion, branded as "barbarians," enslaved, or forced to renounce their native customs. In contrast, Indian thought developed several profound concepts that recognized the sacred divinity and fundamental brotherhood of all living creatures:

📖 Core Sanskrit & Buddhist Dictums of Inclusivity
  • Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam (वसुधैव कुटुम्बकम्): Found in the ancient Maha Upanishad and popularised in the Hitopadeśa, this immortal phrase declares: “The whole world is one family.” It warns against narrow-minded tribalism (thinking "this person belongs to my clan, that person is a stranger") and urges enlightened human beings to embrace all people as members of one cosmic household.
  • Atithi Devo Bhava (अतिथि देवो भव): Originating from the Taittiriya Upanishad, this ethical principle commands: “The guest is equivalent to God.” In traditional Indian culture, welcoming a traveller or refugee who seeks shelter is not an act of charity, but a sacred spiritual duty.
  • Sarve Bhavantu Sukhinah (सर्वे भवन्तु सुखिनः): An ancient Vedic prayer wishing: “May all beings be happy, may all beings be free from illness, may all see what is auspicious, may no one suffer.” It does not restrict its prayer to one religion or one nation; it includes every creature under the sun.
  • Karaniya Metta Sutta (The Buddha's Discourse on Loving-Kindness): The Buddha taught that one should radiate unconditional loving-kindness (Metta) and boundless compassion (Karuna) over the entire world: “Even as a mother protects with her life her only child, so with a boundless heart should one cherish all living beings; radiating kindness over the entire world—above, below, and all around without limit.”

This civilisational ethos was beautifully summarised in modern times by Sri Sarada Devi, the spiritual consort of Sri Ramakrishna Paramahamsa. In her very last message before passing away, she gave humanity this golden advice:

“Learn to make the whole world your own. No one is a stranger, my child; the whole world is your own.”

When Swami Vivekananda addressed the World Parliament of Religions in Chicago on 11 September 1893, he made this civilisational tradition the cornerstone of his opening speech, proclaiming with pride to the global audience:

“I am proud to belong to a nation which has sheltered the persecuted and the refugees of all religions and all nations of the Earth. I am proud to tell you that we have gathered in our bosom the purest remnant of the Israelites, who came to Southern India and took refuge with us in the very year in which their holy temple was shattered to pieces by Roman tyranny. I am proud to belong to the religion which has sheltered and is still fostering the remnant of the grand Zoroastrian nation.”

2.2 The Story of Indian Jews: Bene Israel & Cochin Synagogues

The Jews are members of a cultural and religious community originating from the ancient Hebrews of the Kingdom of Israel in West Asia. Their religion is Judaism, one of the world's oldest monotheistic faiths, centered on the worship of one God (Yahweh) and the moral laws codified in their sacred scripture, the Torah.

Throughout European and West Asian history, Jews faced continuous religious persecution, forced displacement, and violence under Babylonian, Roman, and medieval Christian and Islamic rulers. However, India proved to be a remarkable exception: it is one of the very few countries in human history where Jews never experienced anti-Semitism or religious persecution from the local population.

1. The Bene Israel (Children of Israel) on the Konkan Coast

The oldest and largest Jewish community in India is known as the Bene Israel. According to historical tradition, their ancestors fled the ancient Kingdom of Israel around 175 BCE to escape persecution under the Seleucid Greek ruler Antiochus IV Epiphanes. Sailing eastward in merchant ships, their vessel was caught in a violent storm and shipwrecked near the village of Navgaon on the Konkan coast, just south of present-day Mumbai in Maharashtra.

  • Miraculous Survival & Loss of Scriptures: Only seven men and seven women survived the shipwreck. In the disaster, their printed scriptures and holy books (the Torah scrolls) were lost to the sea.
  • The Eternal Prayer: Despite losing their books, the survivors remembered and chanted by heart their core Hebrew prayer, the Shema Yisrael (“Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is One”), preserving their strict monotheistic faith across generations.
  • Cultural Blending into Maharashtrian Society: The Bene Israel took up oil-pressing (extracting cooking oil from sesame and coconuts). Because they strictly observed the Jewish Sabbath (resting from Friday sunset to Saturday sunset and refusing to press oil on Saturday), the local Marathi villagers affectionately named them Shanwar Teli (“Saturday Oil-Pressers”).
  • Language & Dress: Over centuries, the Bene Israel adopted Marathi as their mother tongue, wrote Marathi translations of prayers, dressed in traditional Maharashtrian sarees, angarkhas, and turbans (as documented in 19th-century photographs, Fig. 5.4 in NCERT), and added the suffix -kar to their ancestral village names (e.g., Navgaonkar, Penkar, Divekar).
  • Demographic Peak: By the time of India's independence in 1947, the Bene Israel community had grown to more than 25,000 members, serving with distinction as doctors, educators, civil servants, and military officers in the Indian Armed Forces.

2. The Cochin Jews & The Paradesi Synagogue of Kerala

Another prominent Jewish stream arrived on the tropical Malabar coast (in present-day Kochi, Kerala) between the 12th and 19th centuries, fleeing persecutions in West Asia and Spain. Some traditions trace their earliest arrivals to the 1st century CE, following the destruction of the Second Temple of Jerusalem by Roman legions in 70 CE.

📜 Royal Patronage: The Cochin Copper Plate Charter

The local Hindu monarch, the Raja of Kochi, welcomed the Jewish refugees with extraordinary warmth. He granted them fertile land free of all taxes and bestowed upon their leader, Joseph Rabban, seventy-two hereditary royal privileges (including the right to ride elephants, use royal palanquins, blow trumpets, and collect certain tolls). The royal deed specified that this grant was valid “as long as the world, the sun, and the moon endure.”

Right next to his own royal palace and the famous Hindu temple of Mattancherry, the Raja permitted the Jews to construct the magnificent Paradesi Synagogue in 1568 CE. A synagogue is a consecrated Jewish house of prayer, study, and assembly where weekly religious services, Shabbat prayers, and sacred ceremonies such as weddings are conducted.

The depth of inter-religious harmony between the Hindu kings of Kerala and the Jewish community is further demonstrated by the Maharaja of Travancore. The Maharaja presented an exquisitely crafted solid gold crown and ornate silver cases to the Kochi synagogue to adorn the sacred Torah scrolls. Even today, the plaque and the interior of the Kochi synagogue (NCERT Figs. 5.2 & 5.3) stand as living monuments to royal Indian secularism and pluralism.

2.3 The Syriac Christian Community: Escape from Imperial Crossfire

From the 4th century CE onward, Christian communities living in West Asia (modern Syria, Iraq, and Iran) found themselves caught in a terrible political and theological vise between two clashing superpowers: the Roman Empire and the Sassanid Persian Empire.

⚠️ The Double Persecution in West Asia
  • Persecution by Rome (Accused of Heresy): Inside the Roman Empire, Christianity had become the official state religion. However, the Syriac-speaking Christians held theological interpretations regarding the nature of Jesus Christ that differed from the decisions of official imperial Church councils. As a result, the Roman authorities condemned them as heretics (persons whose beliefs oppose official doctrine), banning their assemblies and exiling their bishops.
  • Persecution by Persia (Accused of Espionage): Across the border in the Zoroastrian Sassanid Empire of Persia, the rulers were engaged in constant warfare against the Roman Empire. Because the Romans were Christian, the Persian emperors suspected the Persian Syriac Christians of being secret Roman spies and sympathisers. Consequently, Christian churches were closed, leaders imprisoned, and heavy punitive taxes imposed.

Refuge on the Malabar Coast

Fleeing this intolerable double persecution, groups of Syriac Christians embarked on dhows and merchant vessels along maritime trade routes across the Arabian Sea, seeking refuge on the Malabar coast of India (Kerala). They brought with them their ancient Syriac language (a dialect of Aramaic, the language spoken by Jesus Christ), which they used as their liturgical language for chants, prayers, and scripture reading.

In Kerala, the local Chera kings received them as honoured guests, granting them copper plate charters (such as the famous Tharisapalli Copper Plates of 849 CE issued by King Ayyan Adikal Thiruvadigal). These charters granted the Christian merchant guild (Manigramam) extensive trade monopolies, land rights, and elevated social status.

Intricate Cultural Synthesis

Over centuries, the Syriac Christians (popularly called Syrian Christians or Nasranis) integrated seamlessly into Malayali society while fiercely guarding their Christian faith. As shown in NCERT Fig. 5.5 (a traditional Syrian Christian wedding), their rituals reflect an astonishing cultural harmony with indigenous Hindu customs:

  • The Minnu (Sacred Wedding Thread): In Kerala Christian weddings, the central marriage ritual is not the Western exchange of rings, but the groom tying a golden pendant called the Minnu (shaped like a banyan leaf and pierced with a cross of 21 gold beads) around the bride's neck using seven threads drawn from the wedding sari. This directly mirrors the traditional Hindu Mangalsutra or Thali tying ceremony!
  • Traditional Lighting of Brass Lamps: Syrian Christian churches and wedding halls feature the large traditional Kerala brass oil lamp (Nilavilakku), lit during prayers and festivals.
  • Attire & Floral Customs: Brides wear traditional ivory-and-gold Kerala silk sarees (Kasavu), adorn their hair with jasmine garlands, and receive ceremonial sandalwood paste (chandanam).

2.4 The Parsis: The Legend of Milk, Sugar, and Nation-Building

The Parsis are followers of Zoroastrianism, one of the world's oldest revealed monotheistic faiths. Founded in ancient Central Asia and Persia by the Prophet Zarathushtra (Zoroaster), the religion is centered on the worship of Ahura Mazda (“The Lord of Wisdom”). Its moral compass is guided by the sacred motto: Humata, Hukhta, Huvarshta (Good Thoughts, Good Words, Good Deeds). In Zoroastrian worship, pure fire (Atash) is revered as the radiant physical symbol of divine truth, order, and cosmic energy.

The Islamic Conquest of Persia & The Flight Across the Sea

For centuries, Zoroastrianism was the glorious state religion of the mighty Persian Sassanid Empire (3rd to 7th centuries CE). However, in the middle of the 7th century CE, the Sassanid Empire fell to Arab Muslim armies. Following the conquest, Zoroastrians faced severe, escalating religious persecutions: forced religious conversions, the imposition of the discriminatory Jizya tax on non-Muslims, the desecration of their sacred fire temples, and severe legal and social marginalisation.

Faced with the destruction of their religious heritage, groups of devoted Zoroastrians made the courageous choice to abandon their ancestral properties and homeland. Carrying their sacred consecrated embers and holy scriptures (the Avesta), they set sail in wooden vessels across the stormy waters of the Arabian Sea toward the west coast of India, arriving in Gujarat between the 8th and 10th centuries CE.

The Legend of King Jadi Rana and the Bowl of Milk

According to the 16th-century Parsi historical poem Qissa-i Sanjan (The Story of Sanjan), when the refugee ships reached the port of Sanjan in coastal Gujarat, the Parsi elders sought an audience with the local Hindu ruler, Raja Jadi Rana.

Because neither side knew the other’s spoken language, King Jadi Rana communicated through a vivid symbol: he ordered his attendants to bring a large metal vessel filled to the very brim with fresh white milk. Pointing to the brimming vessel, the Raja indicated that his land was already densely populated, and there was no room to accommodate an influx of foreign refugees.

Among the Parsis was an enlightened high priest (Dastur). The Dastur asked for a spoonful of granulated sugar. Walking calmly to the jug of milk, he gently poured the sugar crystals into the liquid and stirred it with care. The sugar dissolved completely into the milk, sweetening its taste, without causing a single drop of milk to overflow the rim!

The message was clear, elegant, and profound: “Just as sugar blends invisibly into milk, sweetening it without displacing it, we will blend peacefully into your land, sweeten your society with trade and wisdom, and cause no burden, disturbance, or displacement to your people.”

Delighted by this extraordinary answer, Raja Jadi Rana welcomed them warmly. He granted them land to settle, permitted them to build their sacred fire temple, and laid down only a few reasonable conditions for local harmony (such as explaining their religion, adopting Gujarati as their daily tongue, dressing in local garments, and holding weddings at dusk). The ancient sacred fire they consecrated was called the Iran Shah; today, that very same unbroken flame continues to burn at the Atash Behram in Udvada, Gujarat, having been tended continuously for over 1,200 years!

Deep Vedic & Avestan Connections

As detailed in the textbook, the Parsis did not come to an alien land; they came to a civilisation with which they shared deep, ancient spiritual and linguistic ancestry:

  • Identical Root Words: Old Avestan (the language of the Zoroastrian scriptures) and Vedic Sanskrit are so closely related that linguists can translate verses from one to the other simply by applying known phonetic sound shifts! For example:
    • Vedic Soma (sacred drink) = Avestan Haoma
    • Vedic Hotar (sacrificial priest) = Avestan Haotar
    • Vedic Yajña (sacred worship) = Avestan Yasna
    • Vedic Asura (lordly power) = Avestan Ahura
    • Vedic Sapta (seven) = Avestan Hapta (hence Sindhu became Hindu / Hendu in ancient Persian!)
  • The Cosmic Order: Both traditions revere the supreme moral order holding the universe together: called Ṛta in the Vedas and Asha in the Avesta.
  • The Theological Inversion: An intriguing divergence occurred over time. In India, devas represent benevolent divine gods, while in the Avesta, daevas are false deities who chose deceit and chaos. Conversely, Ahura is the supreme God in Zoroastrianism, whereas in later Indian mythology, asuras came to be viewed as demonic adversaries.

Eminent Parsi Nation-Builders

Although the Parsi community makes up less than 0.005% of India's population today, their contributions to building modern India in science, industry, law, defence, philanthropy, and freedom are monumental:

  • Dadabhai Naoroji (1825–1917): Known as the “Grand Old Man of India,” he authored Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, formulated the famous Drain of Wealth theory proving British colonial economic exploitation, was elected three times as President of the Indian National Congress, and was the first Indian elected to the British House of Commons.
  • Dr. Homi Jehangir Bhabha (1909–1966): The visionary nuclear physicist celebrated as the “Father of the Indian Nuclear Programme.” He founded the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR) and the Atomic Energy Commission of India (now BARC), catapulting India into the frontline of global atomic science.
  • Field Marshal Sam Manekshaw (1914–2008): Affectionately known as “Sam Bahadur,” he was the legendary Chief of Army Staff during the 1971 Indo-Pak War. His brilliant military strategy, inspirational leadership, and steadfast integrity led to the liberation of Bangladesh and the surrender of 93,000 enemy troops—the largest post-WWII military surrender. He was India’s first Field Marshal.
  • Jamsetji Tata & Ratan Tata: Pioneers of Indian modern industry who established Tata Steel in Jamshedpur, hydroelectric power stations, the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) in Bengaluru, Tata Motors, and TCS. They committed over 65% of their corporate profits to charitable trusts for public hospitals, education, and social upliftment.
  • Nani Palkhivala & Fali S. Nariman: Two of modern India's most brilliant constitutional jurists. Nani Palkhivala successfully argued the historic 1973 Kesavananda Bharati case, establishing the inviolable Basic Structure Doctrine of the Indian Constitution, preserving Indian democracy against authoritarian overreach.
  • Cornelia Sorabji (1866–1954): A trailblazing Parsi reformer, she was the first female graduate from Bombay University, the first woman to study law at Oxford University, and the first female advocate in India. She dedicated her life to defending the legal rights of purdahnashin women (women living in strict seclusion who were denied property rights).
  • Madame Bhikaiji Cama (1861–1936): A fearless freedom fighter who, at the International Socialist Congress in Stuttgart, Germany in 1907, unfurled the very first precursor to the Indian National Flag, defying the British Empire on the international stage.

2.5 The Arab Merchant Community: Peaceful Commerce & The Oldest Mosque

Unlike the later military invasions across the northwestern land frontiers, the initial arrival of Arabs in peninsular India from the 7th century CE onward was entirely peaceful, commercial, and collaborative. Arab merchants navigated the monsoon trade winds across the Arabian Sea, seeking black pepper (“black gold”), cardamom, ginger, cinnamon, cotton textiles, and ivory from the bustling ports of Kerala, Gujarat, and coastal Karnataka.

🕌 Cheraman Juma Masjid: Subcontinent's Oldest Mosque (629 CE)

Located in Kodungallur (ancient Muziris) in Kerala, the Cheraman Juma Masjid is recognized as the oldest mosque in India and one of the oldest in the world. Built around 629 CE during the lifetime of the Prophet Muhammad or shortly thereafter, it was constructed under the royal patronage of the last Chera emperor, Cheraman Perumal.

Remarkably, the mosque was constructed entirely in indigenous Kerala temple architectural style, featuring carved wooden pillars, sloping clay-tiled roofs, brass oil lamps (rather than hanging glass lanterns), and a traditional bathing pond, reflecting total architectural and spiritual syncretism.

The Arab traders settled peacefully along the Malabar coast, married local Malayalam-speaking women, and gave rise to the vibrant Mappila (Moplah) Muslim community. They became trusted naval commanders, overseas commercial diplomats, and merchants for the Hindu Zamorins (rulers) of Kozhikode (Calicut), significantly enriching the linguistic vocabulary, maritime shipbuilding techniques, and cuisine of Kerala (e.g., Malabar Biryani and Pathiri).

2.6 The Siddis: The African Connection, Dhamal Dance, & Resilience

A distinctive and fascinating immigrant stream in Indian history is represented by the Siddis (also known as Habshis). The Siddis are of African origin, primarily descended from the Bantu-speaking peoples of Southeast and East Africa (regions encompassing modern-day Mozambique, Tanzania, Kenya, and Ethiopia).

How Did They Arrive in India?

Between the 7th and 19th centuries, tens of thousands of Africans were captured and transported across the Indian Ocean by Arab, Portuguese, and British slave traders to serve as domestic servants, agricultural labourers, sailors, and palace guards in Indian kingdoms. Others arrived as free merchants, mariners, and elite mercenaries recruited by Indian sultans and kings for their exceptional physical endurance and military discipline.

Rise to Power: From Bondage to Sovereign Rulers

India offered unprecedented social mobility to skilled African individuals. Rather than remaining permanently trapped in generational chattel slavery (as occurred in the transatlantic slave trade), several Siddis rose to become kingmakers, generals, and rulers:

  • Malik Ambar (1548–1626): Born in Ethiopia, sold into slavery as a boy, and brought to India, Malik Ambar rose to become the brilliant Prime Minister and de facto ruler of the Ahmadnagar Sultanate in Maharashtra. He pioneered bargir-giri (guerrilla warfare) that repeatedly defeated mighty Mughal imperial armies, built advanced underground water aqueducts in Aurangabad, and trained early Maratha cavalrymen.
  • The Janjira Sea Fort: On an impregnable island rock off the Konkan coast of Maharashtra, Siddi naval admirals constructed the formidable Murud-Janjira Fort. Despite dozens of military sieges by Shivaji Maharaj, Sambhaji Maharaj, the Peshwas, the Portuguese, and the British East India Company, the Janjira island fort was never captured by force during its entire 350-year history!
  • The Habshi Dynasty of Bengal (1487–1493 CE): In the late 15th century, African generals took power in Bengal and ruled the Sultanate of Bengal for six years, issuing royal coins and constructing grand mosques in Gaur.

Culture, Syncretism, & The Dhamal Dance

Today, Siddi communities live primarily in the forest villages of Gujarat (especially in the Gir forest around Talala), the dense Western Ghats of Karnataka (around Yellapur and Haliyal in Uttara Kannada), Maharashtra, and Hyderabad.

  • The Dhamal Dance: The Siddis have preserved an electrifying musical tradition known as the Dhamal dance. Dancers play African-origin instruments such as the ngoma or musindo (large wooden drums covered in goatskin), the malunga (a single-string musical bow with a coconut resonator, identical to the Brazilian berimbau), and small whistle rattles called jhunjhuna. They perform acrobatic jumps, foot-tapping rhythms, and polyrhythmic chanting.
  • Syncretic Faith: Siddis follow Islam, Hinduism, or Christianity, while universally venerating their ancestral spiritual patron, Bava Gor, an African Sufi merchant and saint whose shrine in Ratanpur, Gujarat, remains a major pilgrimage site.
  • Socio-Economic Challenges & Scheduled Tribe Status: Because of centuries of geographic isolation and economic marginalisation, the Siddis faced acute poverty, lack of formal schooling, and poor healthcare. To ensure their constitutional protection and affirmative development, the Government of India designated the Siddis as a Scheduled Tribe (ST) in Gujarat and Karnataka.
🏅 Grassroots Hero: Hirabai Lobi (Padma Shri 2023)

From the Siddi settlement of Jambur in Gujarat, Hirabai Lobi emerged as a transformative social reformer. Recognizing that women held the key to transforming her community, she founded community self-help cooperatives, enrolled children in rural balwadis (kindergartens), taught adult women basic financial literacy, connected them with formal bank accounts, and trained farmers in chemical-free organic farming. In recognition of her extraordinary, selfless service to tribal welfare and women's empowerment, the President of India honoured her with the prestigious Padma Shri in 2023 (NCERT Fig. 5.12)!

2.7 The Armenians: Silk, Spices, and Royal Trust in Mughal Courts

Armenia is an ancient, mountainous country situated in the South Caucasus region, nestled between Turkey, Georgia, Azerbaijan, and Iran. Historically, Armenia was the very first nation in the world to officially adopt Christianity as its state religion in 301 CE.

Famed across Eurasia as master international merchants, skilled jewelers, and diplomats, Armenian traders established trading posts along the Malabar coast as early as the 8th century CE to purchase fine Indian muslins, indigo, and spices.

Imperial Welcome under Emperor Akbar

In the 16th century, the third Mughal Emperor, Akbar the Great, recognized the immense commercial genius, linguistic versatility, and trustworthiness of the Armenians. Akbar invited Armenian families from New Julfa (Isfahan, Persia) to settle in his imperial capital of Agra. He granted them free land, paid for the construction of an Armenian Christian church in Agra in 1562, and exempted them from commercial duties.

Armenians rose to the highest positions of confidence within Akbar's court and royal household:

  • Mir Abdul Hai: A brilliant Armenian jurist who was appointed as the Chief Justice (Mir Adl) in Emperor Akbar's imperial court, administering justice with impartiality across the empire.
  • Lady Juliana: An Armenian doctor who served as the Chief Royal Physician inside the imperial palace, overseeing healthcare for the royal women, and the sister of Akbar's Christian wife Mariam/Juliana.
  • Alexander: An Armenian merchant who was appointed as superintendent of the royal silk-weaving workshops.

Urban Legacies in Kolkata and Chennai

As maritime trade expanded, prosperous Armenian colonies flourished in key Indian ports:

  • Kolkata (Calcutta): Kolkata became the vibrant cultural and educational hub of the Armenian diaspora in Asia. They constructed the historic Holy Nazareth Church (Mother Mary Church) in 1707 CE. In 1821, they founded the prestigious Armenian College and Philanthropic Academy on Free School Street. Today, more than two centuries later, this historic institution continues to educate Armenian youth from Armenia, Iran, and across the world with full scholarships funded by century-old endowments!
  • Chennai (Madras): Armenian merchants settled in Madras around the mid-17th century, dominating trade in silk, raw diamonds, rubies, and spices. In George Town, the famous commercial artery is named Armenian Street in their honour. Their architectural gem, St. Mary's Armenian Church (built in 1712 and rebuilt in 1772), features an iconic six-bell belfry that rings across the city. The wealthy merchant Khoja Petrus Woskan personally funded the construction of the famous Marmalong Bridge across the Adyar River and the 160 stone steps leading up the holy shrine of St. Thomas Mount!

2.8 The Baha'i Community: The Message of Universal Oneness & The Lotus Temple

In the middle of the 19th century (around 1844–1863), a new universal faith known as the Baha'i Faith was founded in Persia (Iran) by Mirza Husayn-'Ali, known to the world by his spiritual title Bahá'u'lláh (meaning “The Glory of God”).

The core message of Bahá'u'lláh centers on three fundamental onenesses: the Oneness of God, the Oneness of Religion, and the Oneness of Humanity. Bahá'u'lláh taught that all major world religions (Hinduism, Buddhism, Zoroastrianism, Judaism, Christianity, Islam) originate from the same divine source and represent progressive chapters of one continuous divine education for human evolution. He called for the complete abolition of racial prejudices, religious sectarianism, and gender inequality, advocating universal peace and world citizenship.

Escape from Persian Oppression to India

The orthodox Islamic clergy and the autocratic Qajar rulers of Persia viewed these progressive teachings as dangerous heresy. Bahá'u'lláh was imprisoned, tortured, and banished into lifelong exile; tens of thousands of his early followers were publicly executed or brutally persecuted. Seeking a land where they could live in peace and share their spiritual vision without fear of execution, groups of Persian Baha'is fled to India starting in the late 19th century.

India welcomed the Baha'is warmly. Because the Baha'i message of universal brotherhood resonated so deeply with India's ancient philosophy of Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam, millions of Indians from diverse backgrounds embraced the faith. Today, with over two million members, India has the largest national Baha'i community in the entire world!

🪷 The Lotus Temple (Mashriqu'l-Adhkar) in New Delhi

Completed in 1986 in southern New Delhi, the Baha'i House of Worship—universally known as the Lotus Temple (NCERT Fig. 5.14)—is an architectural marvel of modern India. Designed by Iranian-Canadian architect Fariborz Sahba in the shape of a blooming sacred lotus flower (a symbol of purity, detachment, and divinity shared across Indian traditions), the structure features 27 free-standing petals made of pure white Greek Pentelic marble, surrounded by nine crystalline water ponds.

True to its principle of universal oneness, the Lotus Temple contains no idols, pictures, or religious symbols. Its doors are open unconditionally to men, women, and children of all religions, castes, and nationalities. Inside the serene central prayer hall, visitors sit side-by-side in pin-drop silence, reading, singing, or meditating upon scriptures of their own chosen faiths (Bhagavad Gita, Holy Quran, Bible, Guru Granth Sahib, Torah, or Avesta).

2.9 The 'Good Maharaja' Digvijaysinhji and the Polish Children (WWII)

One of the most touching and inspiring humanitarian chapters in 20th-century world history unfolded during the dark days of the Second World War (1939–1945), establishing a timeless bond of love between India and Poland.

The Plight of the Polish Orphans

In September 1939, Poland was invaded and partitioned by Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union. Hundreds of thousands of civilian Polish families were forcibly deported to brutal labour camps in the freezing wilderness of Siberia and Kazakhstan. When amnesty was declared in 1941, surviving Polish families walked thousands of miles in search of safety. Along the freezing journey, tens of thousands of parents died from typhus, dysentery, and starvation, leaving behind thousands of traumatized, malnourished, and orphaned Polish children.

These orphaned children were evacuated to Iran, but the British colonial government and several Allied nations hesitated or refused to take in non-British European refugees, citing war shortages.

The Heroic Stand of Maharaja Digvijaysinhji of Nawanagar

When the news reached Maharaja Jam Saheb Digvijaysinhji Ranjitsinhji Jadeja of Nawanagar State (present-day Jamnagar in Gujarat), he was deeply moved. As a member of the Imperial War Cabinet, he declared that he would personally sponsor, finance, and house the Polish children in his kingdom, stating:

“Do not consider yourselves orphans. You are Nawanagaris now, and I am your Bapu (father)!”

Between 1942 and 1946, the Maharaja saved the lives of approximately 1,000 Polish orphans and welcomed several thousand other Polish refugees fleeing war-torn Europe.

⛺ The Sanctuary at Balachadi: A Home Away from Home

Rather than putting the children in institutional dormitories, the Maharaja constructed a dedicated seaside camp at Balachadi, 25 kilometres from his capital Jamnagar, designed specifically to restore their childhood and heal their mental trauma:

  • Authentic Polish Lifestyle: The Maharaja brought in Polish chefs to prepare authentic European meals so the children would not suffer digestive distress, ordered special clothes, and provided comfortable individual beds.
  • Preserving Polish Language & Culture: He hired Polish teachers and priests to run a full Polish-medium school, ensuring that the children never forgot their native language, geography, songs, and literature.
  • Freedom of Worship: He arranged for the construction of a Catholic chapel inside the camp where the children could attend Sunday Mass and celebrate traditional Polish Christmas and Easter.
  • Scouting & Sports: He encouraged the Polish national scouting movement (Harcerstwo), arranged soccer matches, swimming in the sea, theatrical plays, and camel rides across the sandy beaches.

Eternal Gratitude in Warsaw

After the end of World War II, the children returned safely to Europe or settled in Britain, Canada, and Australia, forever calling themselves the “Survivors of Balachadi.”

In the 21st century, the Republic of Poland officially honoured the Maharaja’s extraordinary compassion. A prominent public square in the capital city of Warsaw was officially named Skwer Dobrego Maharadży (The Good Maharaja Square), where a bronze monument was erected in his memory (NCERT Fig. 5.15). The President of Poland posthumously awarded him the prestigious Commander’s Cross of the Order of Merit of the Republic of Poland, and the city council of Warsaw granted him posthumous honorary citizenship. In Warsaw, a public school is named in his honour, where Polish children learn about the generous Indian king who gave their ancestors life and love.

2.10 Tibetan Refugees in India: Preserving Sacred Knowledge Traditions

Located immediately across the colossal snowy barrier of the Great Himalayas, Tibet (poetically called The Land of Snows) has been India’s spiritual and cultural neighbour for more than a thousand years.

The Historic Spiritual Debt: 7th to 13th Century Connections

In the 7th century CE, Tibetan King Songtsen Gampo embraced Buddhism, sending his minister Thonmi Sambhota to India to create the Tibetan script based on ancient Indian Brahmi/Gupta scripts. Great Indian masters like Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche) in the 8th century and Atiśa Dīpaṅkara Śrījñāna in the 11th century travelled to Tibet to establish Buddhist monasteries and translate thousands of Sanskrit treatises into Tibetan.

During the 12th and 13th centuries, when violent Turko-Afghan military raids resulted in the tragic sacking and burning of Nālandā Mahāvihāra, Vikramashila, and Odantapuri in Bihar, Indian Buddhist monks gathered precious palm-leaf and birch-bark manuscripts of ancient Sanskrit texts and fled across the Himalayan mountain passes into Tibet. For centuries, Tibet carefully guarded and preserved these priceless Indian knowledge traditions, which had been destroyed in their homeland!

The 1959 Crisis & The Escape of the 14th Dalai Lama

From the 15th century onward, the succession of the Dalai Lamas (each recognized as the reincarnation of the Bodhisattva of Compassion, Avalokiteśvara) served as the supreme spiritual guides and, from the mid-17th century, the civil leaders of Tibet.

In 1950, the newly formed People’s Republic of China invaded and occupied Tibet. Tensions culminated in March 1959 with a massive popular uprising in the capital of Lhasa. Fearing assassination or capture by Chinese forces, the young 23-year-old 14th Dalai Lama (Tenzin Gyatso) disguised himself as a soldier and made a perilous, secret fortnight-long escape on horseback and foot across freezing Himalayan passes into India.

India Grants Asylum: "An Honoured Guest"

Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and the Government of India immediately granted political asylum to the Dalai Lama and welcomed approximately 80,000 Tibetan refugees who followed him into exile. The Dalai Lama was received not as a political burden, but as an “honoured guest” of India.

The Government established the headquarters of the Central Tibetan Administration (CTA)—the Tibetan government-in-exile—at McLeod Ganj near Dharamshala in Himachal Pradesh, which quickly became known internationally as “Little Lhasa.”

Rehabilitation & The Vibrant Settlements of Karnataka

In cooperation with state governments, prime agricultural land was allotted to Tibetan families to construct durable settlements and rebuild their lives with dignity:

  • Bylakuppe (Karnataka): Established in 1960 in Mysuru district, Bylakuppe is the largest Tibetan refugee settlement in the world. It is home to the breathtaking Namdroling Monastery (The Golden Temple), famous for its 40-foot-tall gilded statues of the Buddha, Padmasambhava, and Amitāyus (NCERT Fig. 5.17), as well as thousands of monk scholars.
  • Mundgod (Karnataka): Known as “Doeguling Tibetan Settlement,” it houses re-established versions of Tibet’s legendary monastic universities—Drepung, Ganden, and Sera—re-creating the intellectual rigour of ancient monastic debates.
  • Education & Cultural Preservation: The Central Tibetan Schools Administration (CTSA) established schools across India ensuring Tibetan children learn both modern sciences and their native language, calligraphy, traditional dance, and thangka painting. In major tourist centers like Goa, Tibetan markets selling woolen handicrafts, turquoise jewelry, and momos flourish during the winter season (NCERT Fig. 5.16).

Sowa Rigpa: The Traditional Healing Science

As highlighted in the textbook box “Don’t Miss Out,” Tibetans brought with them their profound medical science called Sowa Rigpa (literally “Knowledge of Nourishment” or “Art of Healing”). Codified at a historic medical council held near Lhasa in the 8th century CE, Sowa Rigpa is an exquisite synthesis of classical Indian Ayurveda (the tridosha theory of Vata, Pitta, Kapha) enriched by medical concepts from China, Persia, Central Asia, and Greece.

In Dharamshala, the Men-Tsee-Khang (Tibetan Medical and Astro-science Institute) trains doctors and produces herbal medicines used to treat chronic metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurological illnesses. In recognition of its efficacy, the Government of India officially recognized Sowa Rigpa under the Ministry of AYUSH (alongside Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, and Homoeopathy) and founded the autonomous National Institute of Sowa Rigpa in Leh, Ladakh.

2.11 Indian Values Beyond India: The Conquerors Conquered & Global Lessons

The spirit of Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam does not operate solely inside the borders of India; it is equally reflected when Indians travel, migrate, and establish communities abroad, as well as in how foreign conquerors were historically transformed by the subcontinent.

1. The Indian Diaspora Abroad

Wherever sizable populations of non-resident Indians (NRIs) and Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) live today—whether in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Singapore, the United Arab Emirates, Mauritius, Fiji, or the Caribbean—they are globally admired as a peaceful, law-abiding, highly educated, and industrious minority. Rather than forming isolated ghettos or inciting sectarian strife, Indian expatriates integrate smoothly into local civic life, respect host country laws, contribute heavily to the local economy and technological innovation, and share their festivals (such as Diwali, Yoga Day, and Holi) in a spirit of open friendship.

2. The Phenomenon of the "Conquerors Conquered"

A unique phenomenon in world history is that many groups who entered India with the sword as military conquerors ended up being peacefully conquered by Indian culture, philosophy, climate, and spirituality:

  • The Indo-Greeks (2nd Century BCE): Greek kings who conquered northwestern India embraced Indian religions. Most famous was King Menander I (known as Milinda in Buddhist texts), whose deep philosophical dialogues with Buddhist sage Nāgasena are immortalised in the classical Pali text Milinda Pañha (Questions of King Milinda). Menander converted to Buddhism, minted coins bearing the Buddhist Dharmachakra, and became a generous patron of the Sangha.
  • The Kushāṇas (1st–3rd Century CE): Originating as the Yuezhi nomadic confederation from the steppes of Central Asia, the Kushāṇas invaded northern India. Instead of imposing their steppe nomadic culture, their greatest emperor, Kanishka I, embraced Buddhism, convened the famous Fourth Buddhist Council in Kashmir, sponsored the creation of Gandhara and Mathura Buddhist sculptures, and sent Buddhist missionaries across the Silk Road to China and Central Asia!
  • Heliodorus Pillar at Vidisha: Greek ambassador Heliodorus, sent by the Indo-Greek King Antialcidas to the court of King Bhagabhadra in central India, converted to the Bhagavata tradition of Vaishnavism and erected a grand stone pillar (the Heliodorus Pillar) in Besnagar in 113 BCE, calling himself a Parama Bhagavata (devout worshipper of Lord Vishnu).

3. Universal Relevance for the 21st-Century World

Today, human civilisation faces mounting planetary crises: brutal geopolitical wars, religious intolerance, forced displacement of tens of millions of refugees, runaway climate destruction, and rising inequality. In this turbulent era, India’s timeless message of the fundamental unity of humankind offers the ultimate healing path:

  • It proves that true national strength comes not from forced conformity, but from inclusive acceptance.
  • It demonstrates that different religious traditions can coexist side by side for thousands of years without destroying one another.
  • It provides a model of human dignity where a guest is not seen as an economic competitor, but as Atithi Deva—a spark of the divine.

In-Text Activity 1: The Midnight Stranger Scenario & Refugee Ethics (NCERT Textbook Page 2)
Step 1

Evidence & Perspectives:
  • Arguments Against (Caution & Security): Unknown background of the stranger, risk of theft or violence, lack of physical space, safety of children/elderly.
  • Arguments For (Compassion & Humanity): Life-threatening weather outside, moral duty to help a fellow human in distress, the Indian cultural ethos of Atithi Devo Bhava.

Step 3
Step-by-Step Analysis & Synthesis:
  1. Exam-Ready Takeaway: India has historically reconciled national security with humanitarian refuge by practicing regulated, compassionate hospitality rooted in Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam.

NCERT Exercise 1: Tracing Migration Routes on a World Map & Physical Geographies Negotiated (Textbook Page 18)
Step 1
Context & Core Examination Question: On the outline of a world map, try to trace the routes that the communities mentioned in this chapter may have used to reach India. What types of physical geographies did they have to negotiate?
Step 2
Geographical Breakdown by Community:
  1. Bene Israel & Cochin Jews: Originating in ancient Israel/Judea on the eastern Mediterranean shore. Travelled south through the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, then sailed east across the vast Arabian Sea to shipwreck at Navgaon (Konkan coast) or reach the natural harbour of Kochi (Malabar coast).
  2. Syriac Christians: Originating in Syrian/Mesopotamian river valleys (Tigris and Euphrates) and Persia. Travelled down the Persian Gulf or overland to southern Arabian ports, then sailed south-east across the Arabian Sea along seasonal monsoon trade routes to Malabar (Kerala).
  3. Parsis (Zoroastrians): Fled mainland Persia (Iran). Travelled south to the Persian Gulf port of Hormuz, then sailed east across the turbulent northern Arabian Sea to make landfall at Sanjan in coastal Gujarat.
  4. Arab Merchants: Sailed directly from ports of the Arabian Peninsula (Oman, Yemen, Red Sea) utilizing the south-west monsoon winds in summer and north-east winds in winter to cross the Arabian Sea to the western coastline of Gujarat, Konkan, and Malabar.
  5. The Siddis: Departed from the Swahili coast and southeastern Africa (Mozambique, Zanzibar, Kenya). Navigated north-east across the western expanse of the Indian Ocean to enter ports of Gujarat (Surat, Bharuch), Maharashtra (Janjira), and Goa.
  6. The Armenians: Departed from the high Armenian Highlands in the South Caucasus. Traveled through Persian land routes, then embarked via Persian Gulf ports to Surat or traveled overland via Afghanistan and Punjab into Mughal Agra, eventually reaching the riverine port of Kolkata (Hooghly River) and the Coromandel coast (Chennai).
  7. Polish Children (WWII): Evacuated from Siberian labor camps across Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan) to Iran (Tehran/Isfahan), transported through the Persian Gulf and Arabian Sea to Mumbai port, and then transferred overland/rail to Jamnagar/Balachadi in Gujarat.
  8. Tibetan Refugees: Fled Lhasa across the colossal Trans-Himalayan Tibetan Plateau. Trekked on foot through freezing passes above 15,000 to 18,000 feet (such as Nathu La, Jelep La, or eastern Himalayan passes into Tawang, Arunachal Pradesh) down into the sub-Himalayan valleys of Assam, Bengal, and Himachal Pradesh (Dharamshala).
Step 3
Physical Geographies & Environmental Hazards Negotiated:
  • The High-Altitude Himalayan Passes: Tibetans negotiated sub-zero temperatures, blizzard snowstorms, acute oxygen deprivation (hypoxia), icy precipices, and glacial crevasses across the roof of the world.
  • Arid Deserts & Dry Plateaus: Persian, Arab, and Armenian migrants crossed the burning, arid deserts of West Asia (Dasht-e Kavir, Dasht-e Lut, Arabian sands) with extreme daytime heat, freezing night temperatures, and scarce water oases.
  • Stormy Oceans & Monsoon Gale Winds: Maritime migrants (Jews, Parsis, Arabs, Siddis) faced open ocean hazards across the Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean: violent tropical cyclones, monsoon gale winds, shipwrecks on coastal reefs (e.g., Bene Israel at Navgaon), scurvy, and dehydration.
  • Tropical Coastal Jungles & Western Ghats: Upon reaching India, communities had to adapt to hot, humid tropical monsoon climates, dense rain-forested mountain ranges (Western Ghats), and tropical diseases.
Step 4
Real-World Connection & Value Link: Teaches students to integrate physical geography (mountains, trade winds, sea routes, climates) with human history. It demonstrates the astonishing courage and resilience of refugee populations who risked starvation and shipwreck in search of freedom.
Step 5
Exam-Ready Model Answer Summary:

Immigrant communities traversed three distinct geographic corridors: (1) Maritime Arabian Sea/Indian Ocean routes negotiated monsoon gales and coral reefs; (2) Arid West Asian desert trade routes negotiated drought and heat; and (3) Himalayan mountain passes negotiated sub-zero blizzards, oxygen scarcity, and precipitous cliffs to reach safety in India.

NCERT Exercise 2: Key Values Intrinsic to Indian Culture Enabling Universal Hospitality (Textbook Page 18)
Step 1
Context & Core Examination Question: What are the key values intrinsic to Indian culture that enable it to welcome people of different backgrounds?
Step 2
Philosophical & Textual Evidence:
  1. Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam (Hitopadeśa & Maha Upanishad): The philosophical belief that the entire earth is one single cosmic family.
  2. Atithi Devo Bhava (Taittiriya Upanishad): Treating the guest as a manifestation of the divine.
  3. Sarve Bhavantu Sukhinah (Vedic Shanti Mantra): Praying for the peace and happiness of all living beings without exclusion.
  4. Karuṇā & Ahiṁsā (The Buddha & Mahavira): Boundless universal compassion and non-injury to any life.
  5. Ekam Sat Vipra Bahudha Vadanti (Rigveda 1.164.46): "Truth is one, but the wise call it by many names."
Step 3
Step-by-Step Point-Wise Analysis:
  • 1. Inherent Pluralism (Absence of Dogmatic Exclusivity): Unlike civilisations that believed there is only "one true faith" and that all other worshippers are infidels or heretics doomed to punishment, Indian thought has always recognized multiple legitimate paths to spiritual truth. Consequently, when Jews, Parsis, Christians, or Baha'is arrived, Indian society never demanded that they abandon their ancestral gods or scriptures.
  • 2. Sanctity of the Guest (Hospitality as Sacred Duty): Indian culture elevated hospitality from a polite social convention to a moral and spiritual obligation (Dharma). Rulers considered it shameful and sinful to turn away a person begging for shelter at their door.
  • 3. Non-Violent Assimilation (The "Sugar in Milk" Ethos): Indian society provided immigrants with autonomous physical space, local economic roles, and freedom to govern their internal personal laws, allowing them to integrate into the civic mainstream without losing their cultural uniqueness.
  • 4. Universal Motherhood (Sri Sarada Devi's Spirit): The conviction that "no one is an outsider" enabled local populations to look beyond differences of skin colour, foreign language, or bizarre attire and view refugees as fellow human brothers and sisters.
Step 4
Real-World Connection & Value Link: In the 21st century, when hatred against immigrants, refugees, and racial minorities is rising in many parts of the globe, India's traditional civilisational pluralism provides a proven, time-tested model for world peace and multicultural co-existence.
Step 5
Exam-Ready 5-Mark Answer Structure:
  • Point 1 (Philosophical): Belief in Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam (the whole world is one family).
  • Point 2 (Spiritual): The doctrine of Atithi Devo Bhava (the guest is equivalent to God).
  • Point 3 (Theological): Deep-rooted spiritual pluralism (accepting that all religions reach the same divine reality).
  • Point 4 (Ethical): Commitment to Karuṇā (compassion) and Ahiṁsā (non-violence) toward all distressed souls.
  • Point 5 (Practical): A tradition of inclusive assimilation that welcomes newcomers to enrich society without forced uniformity.

Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam: Ancient Sanskrit maxim from the Hitopadeśa and Maha Upanishad declaring: “The entire world is one family.”
2
Atithi Devo Bhava: Ethical commandment from the Taittiriya Upanishad commanding that a guest must be treated with the same reverence as God.
3
Sarada Devi's Final Message: “Learn to make the whole world your own. No one is a stranger, my child; the whole world is your own.”
4
Swami Vivekananda (1893): Proudly testified at Chicago that India had sheltered the persecuted remnants of the Israelites and Zoroastrians.
5
Bene Israel (175 BCE): Shipwrecked at Navgaon on the Konkan coast; preserved monotheism through the Shema prayer; nicknamed Shanwar Teli.
6
Cochin Jews & Raja of Kochi: Raja gave tax-free land “as long as world, sun & moon endure”; built Paradesi Synagogue (1568 CE) beside his palace.
7
Golden Torah Crown: The Hindu Maharaja of Travancore presented an ornate solid gold crown to adorn the sacred Hebrew Torah scrolls in Kochi.
8
Syriac Christians (4th c.): Escaped Roman heresy charges and Persian espionage accusations; welcomed in Malabar with Tharisapalli copper plates.
9
The Minnu Ceremony: Kerala Christian wedding ritual where the groom ties a banyan-leaf gold pendant with a 21-bead cross, mirroring the Hindu Thali.
10
The Sugar in Milk Parable: Parsi Dastur dissolved sugar into King Jadi Rana’s brimming milk jug at Sanjan, symbolizing peaceful, sweetening assimilation.
11
Iran Shah Sacred Fire: The unbroken Zoroastrian fire consecrated in Gujarat over 1,200 years ago continues to burn at the Atash Behram in Udvada.
12
Cheraman Juma Masjid (629 CE): Oldest mosque in the subcontinent, built in Kodungallur (Kerala) in indigenous wooden temple architecture.
13
The Siddis & Hirabai Lobi: Indo-African community famous for the Dhamal dance with ngoma drums; Hirabai Lobi awarded the Padma Shri in 2023.
14
The Good Maharaja of Nawanagar: Maharaja Digvijaysinhji rescued ~1,000 Polish war orphans (1942–46) at Balachadi; honoured with a square in Warsaw.
15
14th Dalai Lama & Sowa Rigpa: Granted asylum in 1959; Dharamshala CTA & Bylakuppe settlements; Tibetan medical science integrated into Ministry of AYUSH.
