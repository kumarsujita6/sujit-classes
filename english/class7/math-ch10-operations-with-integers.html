1. Rakesh’s Number Game & The Magic of Sum and Difference

Imagine your friend Rakesh walks up to you with a mathematical mystery:

Rakesh's Challenge 1
"I have thought of two whole numbers. Their sum is 25, and their difference is 11. Can you find my two numbers without using high school algebra formulas?"

To solve this, we don't need panic or complex equations! We simply write down candidate pairs whose sum is 25, and test their differences:

Pair 1: 10 + 15 = 25 → Difference = 10 − 15 = −5 (Too small!) Pair 2: 20 + 5 = 25 → Difference = 20 − 5 = 15 (A bit too large!) Pair 3: 19 + 6 = 25 → Difference = 19 − 6 = 13 (Getting closer!) Pair 4: 18 + 7 = 25 → Difference = 18 − 7 = 11 (✓ BINGO! The numbers are 18 and 7)
Teacher's Mental Math Secret: Why Sum and Difference Work
Let the two numbers be a (first) and b (second).
• Sum: a + b = S
• Difference: a − b = D
If you add both equations: (a + b) + (a − b) = 2a = S + D ⇒ First number a = (Sum + Difference) ÷ 2.
If you subtract them: (a + b) − (a − b) = 2b = S − D ⇒ Second number b = (Sum − Difference) ÷ 2.
For Rakesh: a = (25 + 11) ÷ 2 = 36 ÷ 2 = 18, and b = (25 − 11) ÷ 2 = 14 ÷ 2 = 7!

Now Rakesh issues his Second Challenge:
"What if two numbers still add up to 25, but their difference is −11?"
Notice what happens when you reverse the subtraction order: 7 − 18 = −11! By simply swapping the two numbers, the sign of the difference flips from +11 to −11!

2. Directed Movement on the Number Line: Carrom Coin Physics

In life, distance alone is not enough; we must also know the direction:

  • Moving Rightwards (East, Upwards, Forward, Profit) → Positive (+)
  • Moving Leftwards (West, Downwards, Backward, Loss) → Negative (−)

If a carrom coin starts at 0 and receives multiple strikes:
Strike 1 moves it by a units, and Strike 2 moves it by b units. The final position is always: P = a + b.

Example: Strike 1 = +5 units (right). Strike 2 = −7 units (left).
Net position P = (+5) + (−7) = −2.
The coin ends up 2 units to the left of 0!

3. Concrete Physical Token Model: Addition & Subtraction Recall

Recall from Class 6 how we model integers using two types of counters:

• Green Token: +1 • Red Token: −1 • Zero Pair: 1 Green + 1 Red = (+1) + (−1) = 0 • Additive Inverse: The additive inverse of a is −a because a + (−a) = 0. Example: Additive inverse of 18 is −18; Additive inverse of −18 is −(−18) = +18.

Subtracting a number is mathematically identical to adding its additive inverse:

  • 7 − 18: Start with 7 green tokens. We cannot remove 18 green tokens from only 7! So we introduce 11 zero pairs (11 green + 11 red). Now we have 18 greens and 11 reds. Removing 18 greens leaves 11 red tokens = −11. Hence, 7 − 18 = 7 + (−18) = −11.
  • 4 − (−12): Start with 4 green tokens. We want to remove 12 red tokens, but there are none! Insert 12 zero pairs (12 green + 12 red). Now remove the 12 red tokens. We are left with 4 + 12 = 16 green tokens = +16. Hence, 4 − (−12) = 4 + 12 = 16.

5. Mathematical Patterns in Multiplication

Mathematics must be logically continuous and self-consistent. Consider what happens when we decrease the multiplier by 1 at each step:

Pattern A: Multiplicand is Positive (+3)
4 × 3 = 12
3 × 3 = 9 (decreases by 3)
2 × 3 = 6 (decreases by 3)
1 × 3 = 3 (decreases by 3)
0 × 3 = 0 (decreases by 3)
−1 × 3 = −3 (decreases by 3)
−2 × 3 = −6 (decreases by 3)
−3 × 3 = −9 (decreases by 3)
Pattern B: Multiplicand is Negative (−3)
4 × (−3) = −12
3 × (−3) = −9 (increases by 3, i.e., moves rightward on number line)
2 × (−3) = −6 (increases by 3)
1 × (−3) = −3 (increases by 3)
0 × (−3) = 0 (increases by 3)
(−1) × (−3) = +3 (increases by 3!)
(−2) × (−3) = +6 (increases by 3!)
(−3) × (−3) = +9 (increases by 3!)

The pattern proves beyond any doubt: as the multiplier goes below zero, the product must continue its steady upward step of +3. Therefore, multiplying two negative numbers must produce a positive number!

6. Special Multipliers: 1 and −1

  • Multiplicative Identity (1): For any integer a, 1 × a = a × 1 = a. Multiplying by 1 preserves both the magnitude and the sign.
  • Sign-Reversing Operator (−1): For any integer a, (−1) × a = −a.
    • If a is positive (+7), then (−1) × (+7) = −7.
    • If a is negative (−7), then (−1) × (−7) = +7.
    • If a = 0, then (−1) × 0 = 0.
    Thus, multiplying by −1 reflects the number across the zero origin to its additive inverse!

7. Product of Three or More Integers: The Even-Odd Sign Law

When evaluating long expressions containing multiple factors:

(−1) × (−1) = +1 (2 negatives → Positive) (−1) × (−1) × (−1) = −1 (3 negatives → Negative) (−1) × (−1) × (−1) × (−1) = +1 (4 negatives → Positive) (−1) × (−1) × (−1) × (−1) × (−1) = −1 (5 negatives → Negative)
Universal Sign Rule for Multiple Integers
• Even number of negative factors: The product is always POSITIVE (+).
• Odd number of negative factors: The product is always NEGATIVE (−).
• Positive factors do not affect the sign of the product in any way!

8. Division of Integers: Inverse of Multiplication

Division is the exact reverse operation of multiplication:
Asking "What is (−100) ÷ 25?" is identical to asking "What number multiplied by 25 gives −100?"
Since 25 × (−4) = −100, we know: (−100) ÷ 25 = −4.

The Zero Division Trap
• Zero divided by any non-zero integer is ZERO: 0 ÷ a = 0 (where a ≠ 0).
• Division by ZERO is UNDEFINED: a ÷ 0 is meaningless, because no number multiplied by 0 can ever equal a non-zero number!

9. Alien Pibs Currency & Linear Diophantine Equations

In an alien civilization, commerce uses only two coin denominations: +13 pibs and −9 pibs. How can we pay an exact target price of T pibs?
This models the classic equation: 13x + (−9)y = T, or 13x − 9y = T.
Because HCF(13, 9) = 1 (they are co-prime), Bézout's Identity guarantees that any integer amount in the universe can be formed using these two coins! For example: 13(7) − 9(10) = 91 − 90 = +1. Once you can make +1, you can multiply by any integer target!

In-Text (Pages 1–2): Rakesh’s Sum & Difference Number Game

Problem Statement:

1. Rakesh says: "I have thought of two numbers. Their sum is 25, and their difference is 11. What are the two numbers?"

2. Rakesh gives a second challenge: "Think of two numbers whose sum is 25, but their difference is −11."

Step 1: Given & Goal: Given Sum = 25 and Difference = a − b = 11 (or −11). Goal is to find the two integers a and b.
Step 2: Conceptual Law: Difference means (First Number) − (Second Number). If a − b = D, then swapping the order gives b − a = −D.
Step 3: Tabular Verification Setup: Testing pairs that add to 25:
• 10 and 15: Sum = 25, Diff = 10 − 15 = −5
• 20 and 5: Sum = 25, Diff = 20 − 5 = 15
• 19 and 6: Sum = 25, Diff = 19 − 6 = 13
• 18 and 7: Sum = 25, Diff = 18 − 7 = 11 ✓
Step 4: Solutions to Part 1 & Part 2:
Part 1: The two numbers are 18 and 7 (18 − 7 = 11).
Part 2: If the difference is −11, swap the numbers! First number is 7 and second number is 18 (7 − 18 = −11).
Teacher's Pedagogical Takeaway: Subtraction is non-commutative (a − b ≠ b − a). In fact, reversing the order of subtraction always produces the additive inverse: b − a = −(a − b).
Figure it Out (Page 2): Finding Number Pairs from Sum & Difference

Problem: Find the pairs of integers from their sums and differences:

(a) Sum = 27, Difference = 9  |  (b) Sum = 4, Difference = 12  |  (c) Sum = 0, Difference = 10

(d) Sum = 0, Difference = −10  |  (e) Sum = −7, Difference = −1  |  (f) Sum = −7, Difference = −13

Step 1: Universal Mathematical Formula: For any pair where a + b = S and a − b = D:
First integer: a = (S + D) ÷ 2
Second integer: b = (S − D) ÷ 2
Step 2: Step-by-Step Evaluations:
(a) Sum = 27, Diff = 9:
a = (27 + 9) ÷ 2 = 36 ÷ 2 = 18
b = (27 − 9) ÷ 2 = 18 ÷ 2 = 9
Check: 18 + 9 = 27 ✓, 18 − 9 = 9 ✓. Pair: (18, 9).

(b) Sum = 4, Diff = 12:
a = (4 + 12) ÷ 2 = 16 ÷ 2 = 8
b = (4 − 12) ÷ 2 = (−8) ÷ 2 = −4
Check: 8 + (−4) = 4 ✓, 8 − (−4) = 12 ✓. Pair: (8, −4).

(c) Sum = 0, Diff = 10:
a = (0 + 10) ÷ 2 = 5
b = (0 − 10) ÷ 2 = −5
Check: 5 + (−5) = 0 ✓, 5 − (−5) = 10 ✓. Pair: (5, −5).

(d) Sum = 0, Diff = −10:
Swap the numbers from part (c):
a = (0 + (−10)) ÷ 2 = −5
b = (0 − (−10)) ÷ 2 = 5
Check: (−5) + 5 = 0 ✓, (−5) − 5 = −10 ✓. Pair: (−5, 5).

(e) Sum = −7, Diff = −1:

Question: In a 3×3 magic product square, the product of the three numbers in each row, each column, and each diagonal is −1000. If the center number is −10, find the product of the four corner elements.

In any multiplicative magic square with constant product P and center entry c:
The product along the two main diagonals each equals P.

Diagonal 2 product = (top-right) × c × (bottom-left) = P.

Product of 4 corners = P² ÷ c² = (P ÷ c)².

The 5 Deadliest Student Exam Blunders (Bad vs Good Approach)

Trap 1: The "Two Negatives Always Make a Positive" Illusion

The Blunder: A student sees (−7) + (−5) and writes +12 because they vaguely recall that "two minuses make a plus".

Trap 2: Distributive Sign Neglect with Negative Multipliers

The Blunder: Expanding −4(x − 6) as −4x − 24.

✗ Bad Approach: −4(x − 6) = −4x − 24 (The negative sign on the 6 was completely ignored!)
✓ Good Approach: Distribute the full negative multiplier (−4) to BOTH terms inside:
−4(x − 6) = [(−4) × x] − [(−4) × 6] = −4x − (−24) = −4x + 24!
Trap 3: Confusing 0 ÷ a with a ÷ 0

The Blunder: Writing 18 ÷ 0 = 0 or 18 ÷ 0 = 18.

✗ Bad Approach: 18 ÷ 0 = 0 (Total misconception!)
✓ Good Approach:
• Zero divided by a number: 0 ÷ 18 = 0 (Sharing 0 sweets among 18 friends leaves 0 sweets each).

Trap 4: Assuming Division is Associative

The Blunder: Evaluating (−64) ÷ 8 ÷ (−2) by grouping the last two terms first.

✗ Bad Approach: (−64) ÷ [8 ÷ (−2)] = (−64) ÷ (−4) = +16 (WRONG ORDER!)
✓ Good Approach: Division is NOT associative! You must strictly evaluate from left to right:
[(−64) ÷ 8] ÷ (−2) = (−8) ÷ (−2) = +4!

✗ Bad Approach: −5² = +25 (Ignored BODMAS order of powers!)
✓ Good Approach:
• In −5², the square applies ONLY to 5, not the minus: −(5²) = −(25) = −25.
• In (−5)², the parentheses bind the minus with the 5: (−5) × (−5) = +25.

15 Rapid Revision Memory Bullet Points (Pre-Exam Checklist)

  1. Set of Integers (ℤ): Includes all whole numbers and their negatives: {..., −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...}. Has no fractions or decimals.
