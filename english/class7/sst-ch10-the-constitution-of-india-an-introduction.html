Chapter 10: The Constitution of India — An Introduction

The Supreme Rulebook of Our Republic: Constituent Assembly, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, The Preamble, Fundamental Rights, DPSP, and the Living Spirit of Democracy.

Theme: Governance & Democracy Reading Time: ~35 Mins Zero-Omission Notes & Solved Crossword

1. What is a Constitution and Why Do We Need One?

Whenever a new President of India, Prime Minister, Chief Justice, or Union Minister takes office, they place their hand on a sacred text or lift their right hand to take an oath: "to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India as by law established." Why does everyone, from the highest leader to the ordinary citizen, submit to this single document?

A Constitution is the supreme legal document that spells out a nation's foundational principles, establishes the structure and limits of government, defines the rights and duties of citizens, and reflects the collective moral aspirations of the society.

The Kabaddi Match Analogy: Imagine your school playing the final state-level Kabaddi championship. A dispute erupts: did the raider cross the midline before being tackled? Without an official written rulebook and an impartial referee, the teams would fight endlessly and the game would collapse! The Constitution is the supreme Rulebook of the Nation. It ensures that governments cannot make up arbitrary rules as they go, and that no citizen can be pushed out unfairly!

Four Essential Reasons Why Every Nation Needs a Constitution

  1. Establishes the Framework of Government: Clearly lays down how the Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary are formed, who elects them, and what powers they hold.
  2. Enforces Checks and Balances: Prevents any single ruler or political party from becoming an absolute dictator by ensuring that each organ keeps a check on the others.
  3. Guarantees Fundamental Rights: Protects minorities and individual citizens against oppression by powerful groups or unjust state laws.
  4. Expresses National Values and Aspirations: Declares what kind of society we strive to build—one rooted in justice, liberty, equality, fraternity, and ecological harmony.

2. Drafting the Constitution: The Constituent Assembly

Writing a Constitution for a newly liberated subcontinent encompassing hundreds of languages, multiple religions, deep caste divisions, and princely states was one of the most formidable intellectual tasks in human history.

Timeline & Key Milestones

  • Formation (December 1946): The Constituent Assembly first convened on 9 December 1946. Initially comprised of 389 members, its strength became 299 members following the Partition of India.
  • Interim President: Dr. Sachidananda Sinha served as the temporary President during the inaugural session.
  • Permanent President: Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected as the permanent President of the Constituent Assembly.
  • Drafting Committee Chairman: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, renowned jurist, economist, and champion of social equality, served as the Chairman of the Drafting Committee and is revered as the "Father of the Indian Constitution".
  • Women Members (15 Visionaries): The Assembly included 15 distinguished women leaders, including Sarojini Naidu, Hansa Mehta, Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, Malati Choudhury, and Begum Aizaz Rasul, who vigorously championed women's equality in education, franchise, and inheritance.
  • Adoption & Enactment: After almost three years (exactly 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days) of intense line-by-line debate, the Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 (celebrated as Constitution Day / Samvidhan Divas).
  • Commencement (Republic Day): It came into full effect on 26 January 1950, commemorating the historic Purna Swaraj declaration of 1930, marking India's birth as a sovereign Republic!

A Masterpiece of Calligraphy and Fine Art

The original copies of the Constitution were not printed with mechanical typesets. They were magnificent works of handmade art:

  • Calligraphy: The entire original text was written by hand in flowing italic calligraphy by master calligrapher Prem Behari Narain Raizada using No. 303 nibs, taking no remuneration except his name written on every page and his grandfather's name on the last page!
  • Illustrations: Celebrated artist Nandalal Bose and his students from Kala Bhavana, Santiniketan, illustrated every chapter border with scenes representing 5,000 years of Indian history—including Harappan seals, Vedic gurukulas, Rama and Sita, Krishna delivering the Gita to Arjuna, Buddha, Mahavira, Nataraja, Emperor Ashoka, Mughal architecture, and Mahatma Gandhi's Dandi March.
  • Preservation: The original signed Hindi and English parchment manuscripts are preserved in hermetically sealed, temperature-controlled cases filled with helium gas inside the Library of the Parliament of India!

3. Foundations: Freedom Struggle, Civilisational Heritage & Global Learnings

Our Constitution was not drafted in an ivory tower. It drew life from three profound rivers of wisdom:

1. The Crucible of the Indian Freedom Struggle

Decades of resistance against colonial exploitation shaped our constitutional DNA. The British denied Indians civil liberties, banned peaceful assembly, and imprisoned freedom fighters without trial. Consequently, the freedom struggle demanded:

  • Absolute Equality before Law, banishing colonial racial supremacy.
  • Inalienable Freedom of Speech and Expression.
  • Universal Adult Franchise: India boldly granted equal voting rights to every adult woman and man on Day 1 of the Republic (1950), whereas countries like the USA took until 1965 and Switzerland until 1971 to grant full voting rights!

2. Ancient Civilisational Heritage

The framers integrated enduring Indian philosophies:

  • Vasudhaiva Kuṭumbakam ("The whole world is one family") and Sarve Bhavantu Sukhinaḥ ("May all beings be happy and free from disease").
  • Ancient republican spirit of Vajji and Chola Uttaramerur village assemblies.
  • Ethical principle of Rājadharma, which dictates that rulers exist only to serve the welfare of the poorest and weakest.
  • Reverence for nature embedded in Article 51A(g) (Fundamental Duty to protect forests, rivers, and wildlife).

Following the Vedic ideal "Ā no bhadrāḥ kratavo yantu viśvataḥ" ("Let noble thoughts come to us from every side"), the framers synthesised best practices worldwide:

4. Key Structural Features: Rights, Duties, and Directive Principles

The Constitution of India is the longest written constitution in the world. Originally containing 395 Articles, 22 Parts, and 8 Schedules, it has expanded through progressive amendments to over 448 Articles, 25 Parts, and 12 Schedules.

The Six Fundamental Rights (Articles 14–32)

Guaranteed in Part III, Fundamental Rights are justiciable—meaning that if the State or any person violates them, an aggrieved citizen can approach the Supreme Court (under Article 32) or High Court (under Article 226) for immediate legal protection!

Figure 10.2: The Six Fundamental Rights guaranteed to every Indian citizen under Part III of the Constitution.

Fundamental Rights vs Directive Principles (DPSP)

Feature Fundamental Rights (Part III) Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV)
Nature Strict legal guarantees given to individual citizens. Moral and socioeconomic guidelines for governments to achieve public welfare.
Enforceability Justiciable: Courts can directly punish violations and strike down conflicting laws. Non-justiciable: Cannot be directly sued for in court, but serve as foundational policy goals.
Examples Article 14 (Equality before law), Article 21 (Right to life and liberty), Article 21A (Free school education). Article 38 (Welfare state), Article 47 (Nutrition and public health), Article 48A (Wildlife protection).

Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

Rights cannot survive without responsibilities. Added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 (and expanded in 2002), Article 51A outlines 11 Fundamental Duties, including respecting the National Flag and Anthem, safeguarding public property, preserving composite culture, protecting the environment, developing scientific temper, and ensuring education for children aged 6–14.

5. The Preamble: The Guiding Soul of the Republic

The Preamble serves as the introductory preface embodying the philosophy, objectives, and foundational values of the Constitution. It is often celebrated as the "Identity Card of the Constitution".

THE PREAMBLE

WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:
JUSTICE, social, economic and political;
LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;
EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all
FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;
IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.

Key Preamble Value Precise Constitutional Meaning How We See It in Daily Life
WE, THE PEOPLE Popular Sovereignty: The Constitution originates from ordinary citizens, not a monarch or colonial ruler. Every citizen votes as an equal stakeholder in general elections.
SOVEREIGN India is completely independent; no foreign power can dictate our laws, borders, or policies. Parliament passes our own budget and laws without external interference.
SOCIALIST National wealth should be used to eliminate poverty and bridge socioeconomic disparities. Subsidised rations (PDS), free school midday meals, and government healthcare schemes.
SECULAR The State has no official religion; all religions are treated with equal respect and freedom. People celebrate Diwali, Eid, Christmas, and Gurpurab freely side-by-side.
DEMOCRATIC Government derives legitimacy from regular, free, fair, and competitive multi-party elections. Citizens can vote out non-performing governments after every five years.
REPUBLIC The Head of State (President) is an elected citizen for a 5-year term, not a hereditary king. Any humble citizen, regardless of humble birth, can aspire to become President of India.
JUSTICE Fair treatment across Social, Economic, and Political fields; elimination of unfair discrimination. Special scholarships and reservations for SC, ST, and OBC students.
LIBERTY Freedom of speech, creative thought, religion, and travel across India without unjust bans. Journalists publishing investigative reports; artists painting freely.
EQUALITY Every citizen is equal before the law; abolition of untouchability and royal titles. Rich business barons and poor street vendors stand in the very same voting line.
FRATERNITY A spirit of brotherhood assuring individual human dignity and national unity. Volunteers from Punjab or Gujarat rushing disaster aid to flood-affected Kerala or Assam.

6. The Living Constitution: Evolving with Time

A Constitution cannot remain frozen in stone. As society progresses and new challenges arise—such as digital privacy, environmental protection, or local self-rule—the Constitution must adapt. This capacity to grow makes it a Living Document.

Constitutional Amendments (Article 368)

Figure 10.4: The parliamentary amendment process under Article 368 that allows the Indian Constitution to remain a dynamic living document.

Changes to the Constitution are called Amendments. Unlike ordinary laws, constitutional amendments require rigorous national consensus: typically a special two-thirds majority in both Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha, and for federal matters, ratification by at least half of the State Legislative Assemblies!

Three Famous Examples of the Living Constitution:
  • Panchayati Raj (73rd & 74th Amendments, 1992): Empowered rural village panchayats and urban municipalities as the third tier of grassroots self-governance.
  • Right to Education (86th Amendment, 2002 / Article 21A): Made free and compulsory education for all children aged 6 to 14 a justiciable Fundamental Right!
  • Flying the Tricolour (2004 Supreme Court Verdict): In Union of India v. Naveen Jindal, the Supreme Court declared that hoisting the National Flag with dignity on private homes is part of the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Expression under Article 19(1)(a).

Let's Explore • Page 4 Kabaddi Rulebook Analogy: What if there was no rulebook or captains refused to agree?

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  1. If there was no official rulebook: Arguments would turn into physical shouting matches. The stronger or louder team would bully the referee, the match would be abandoned, and no genuine champion could be crowned.
  2. What is required for everyone to abide by the rules? Trust in the referee's neutrality, clear prior agreement by all parties to respect the rules, and mutual sportsmanship.
  3. Application to a Nation: If a country lacked a Constitution, politicians in power would act like partisan captains—jailing opponents, changing election rules overnight, and seizing citizens' lands. The Constitution keeps every government within legal boundaries.
Don't Miss Out • Page 4 Why have Parts and Schedules increased from 22 Parts & 8 Schedules to 25 Parts & 12 Schedules?

Reasons for Constitutional Expansion:

  • New Societal Needs: When India became independent, modern challenges like cyber laws, municipal corporations, panchayati raj institutions, and cooperative societies did not have dedicated frameworks.
  • Addition of Parts: Major additions include Part IV-A (Fundamental Duties in 1976), Part IX (Panchayats in 1992), Part IX-A (Municipalities in 1992), and Part IX-B (Cooperative Societies in 2011).
  • Addition of Schedules: Schedules increased from 8 to 12 to include land reform validations (9th Schedule), official languages expansion to 22 languages (8th Schedule), the Anti-Defection law (10th Schedule), Panchayati Raj powers (11th Schedule), and Municipality powers (12th Schedule).

Don't Miss Out • Page 13 Fundamental Rights vs Directive Principles: Why both were created

Why Framers Created Both:

In 1947, newly independent India was impoverished following two centuries of colonial plunder. The framers realized that certain basic political rights (freedom of speech, equality before law, religious freedom) could be guaranteed immediately without vast state expenditures—these became Fundamental Rights (Part III), enforceable in courts.

However, major socioeconomic aspirations (free healthcare for all, living wages, modern pensions, full employment) required colossal economic investments that the young nation could not finance immediately. The framers wisely placed these goals under Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV) as a permanent moral compass for future governments to implement progressively as national wealth expanded.

Let's Explore • Page 14 Begum Aizaz Rasul's Speech: Articles referred to & Women's equality in Indian heritage

Analysis of Begum Aizaz Rasul's Famous Address (22 Nov 1949):

  1. Articles Referred To: She was referring primarily to Article 14 (Equality before the Law) and Article 15 (Prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth).
  2. Why is Women's Equality Not a New Concept for India? In ancient Vedic and civilisational heritage, women were revered scholars and philosophers (such as Gargi and Maitreyi participating in royal philosophical debates with Sage Yajnavalkya) and warriors (like Rani Lakshmibai and Kittur Chennamma). While regressive social distortions developed during medieval centuries, the Constitution restored womanhood to its rightful ancient dignity.
Let's Explore • Page 15 Amendments Made to the Constitution in the Past Ten Years

Key Recent Constitutional Amendments:

  • 101st Amendment Act (2016): Introduced the Goods and Services Tax (GST) across India, unifying national indirect taxation into "One Nation, One Tax".
  • 102nd Amendment Act (2018): Conferred constitutional status on the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC).
  • 103rd Amendment Act (2019): Provided 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) in education and government jobs.
  • 104th Amendment Act (2020): Extended reservation of seats for SCs and STs in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies for an additional 10 years.
  • 106th Amendment Act (2023 - Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam): Reserved one-third (33%) of all seats in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies for women!

8. Step-by-Step NCERT Exercise Solutions

NCERT Q1 Why was it important to have a diverse set of representatives in the Constituent Assembly?

Why Diversity in the Constituent Assembly was Essential:

  1. Reflecting the Entire Mosaic of India: India is a subcontinent with immense geographical, cultural, linguistic, and religious diversity. If only a single class, region, or gender had written the Constitution, the needs of marginalized communities would have been ignored.
  2. Protection of Marginalised Interests: The presence of social reformers like Dr. B.R. Ambedkar ensured strong safeguards against caste discrimination, while leaders like Jaipal Singh Munda championed the land and forest rights of Adivasi tribal communities.
  3. Women's Voice & Gender Equality: 15 female members ensured that women received equal political franchise, non-discrimination in employment, and legal dignity from Day 1.
  4. Legitimacy & National Acceptance: Because every state, religion, and social background had representatives in the Assembly, all Indian citizens accepted the Constitution as their very own.
NCERT Q2 Identify key features / values reflected in each given statement

Statement-by-Statement Constitutional Identification:

  • a. Sheena, Rajat, and Harsh are standing in a line excited to cast their first vote in general elections:
    Reflects Democracy, Universal Adult Franchise, and Political Equality (Article 326).
  • b. Radha, Imon, and Harpreet study in the same class in the same school:
    Reflects Secularism, Equality of Opportunity, Fraternity, and the Right to Education (Article 21A).
  • c. Parents must make arrangements to ensure their children's education:
    Reflects Fundamental Duty under Article 51A(k) (Duty of parents/guardians to provide education to children aged 6 to 14).
  • d. People of all castes, genders, and religions can use the village well:
    Reflects Right to Equality (Article 15: Prohibition of discrimination regarding access to public wells, tanks, and roads) and Social Justice.
NCERT Q3 "All citizens in India are equal before the law": Is this a fact? Formulate arguments.

Balanced Constitutional Evaluation:

  • In Law (De Jure) — YES, It is an Absolute Fact: Under Article 14, the Constitution guarantees that the law applies identically to everyone. Whether an accused person is a wealthy minister or an impoverished laborer, both are tried under the same Indian Penal Code in the same courts. Powerful politicians and corporate tycoons have been investigated, tried, and sentenced by Indian courts, demonstrating the rule of law.
  • In Practice (De Facto) — Challenges Remain: In daily reality, achieving equality before the law is hindered by socioeconomic disparities. Wealthy individuals can hire top lawyers, secure quick bails, and prolong cases, whereas poor citizens often languish as undertrials due to lack of resources for legal aid.
  • Conclusion: Equality before the law is an established constitutional fact and reality in statutory terms, but our society must constantly work—through free legal aid clinics, fast-track courts, and police reforms—to make legal equality equally accessible to the poorest citizen.
NCERT Q4 Why did India provide Universal Adult Franchise from the very beginning?

Why India Boldly Adopted Universal Adult Franchise in 1950:

  1. Trust in the Wisdom of the Common People: Leaders like Mahatma Gandhi, Jawaharlal Nehru, and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar firmly believed that illiterate and poor peasants possessed profound common sense and deserved equal democratic dignity as much as university graduates.
  2. Equal Sacrifice in the Freedom Movement: Women, farmers, tribal fighters, and factory workers had sacrificed their lives side-by-side during the freedom struggle. Denying them the right to vote would have betrayed the sacred promise of Swaraj.
  3. Tool for Social Transformation: Dr. Ambedkar realized that the ballot paper was the most powerful weapon for oppressed castes to elect their own representatives and dismantle centuries-old social discrimination.
NCERT Q5 How the freedom struggle and civilisational heritage inspired key features of the Constitution

Dual Inspirations Explored:

  • Influence of the Freedom Struggle:

    Because the British Raj practiced arbitrary detention (Rowlatt Act) and suppressed free speech, the framers insisted on justiciable Fundamental Rights (Articles 19–22). Because colonial taxation impoverished rural India, the framers introduced Directive Principles to establish a welfare state committed to public health, village industries, and land reforms.

  • Influence of Civilisational Heritage:

    The ancient Indian tradition of democratic assemblies (Sabhas, Samitis, Gana-Sanghas of Vajji, and Uttaramerur village councils) reinforced republican governance. Ethical ideals like Rājadharma (welfare of the ruled is the ruler's supreme duty) and Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam inspired the values of fraternity, social justice, and environmental duty (Article 51A(g)).

NCERT Q6 Have we achieved all ideals of the Constitution? What can citizens do?

Honest Assessment & Citizens' Action Plan:

No, while India has made historic strides as the world's most vibrant democracy, we have not yet fully achieved all constitutional ideals. Poverty, malnutrition, gender violence, caste prejudice, and educational gaps still persist.

What Every Citizen Can Do:

  • Practice Non-Discrimination: Treat all classmates, neighbors, domestic workers, and sanitation staff with genuine equality and respect.
  • Exercise the Vote Responsibly: Turn out on election day and vote for candidates based on integrity, public service, and constitutional values rather than money or caste appeals.
  • Perform Fundamental Duties: Protect our environment, conserve water, maintain public property, and respect the National Flag and Anthem.
  • Promote Communal Harmony: Stand against hate speech, verify news before sharing on social media, and protect the spirit of fraternity.

9. Practice Drills & Essential Knowledge Bank

Key Terms Flashcard Bank

Constituent Assembly
The sovereign elected body of 299 members that drafted and enacted the Constitution of India between 1946 and 1949.
Justiciable
A legal right that can be directly enforced by courts if violated (e.g., Fundamental Rights).
DPSP
Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV): Non-justiciable socioeconomic guidelines for establishing a welfare state.
Preamble
The introductory manifesto setting out the core philosophy, source of power, and aspirations of the Constitution.
Amendment
A formal change, addition, or repeal of a constitutional clause passed by Parliament under Article 368.
Republic
A political entity where supreme power is held by the people and their elected representatives, with an elected Head of State.
