3.1 The Need for Smaller Units (ପରିମାପରେ କ୍ଷୁଦ୍ରତର ଏକକର ଆବଶ୍ୟକତା)

Imagine you are trying to assemble a mechanical toy or fix a loose battery door. You grab a screw from the toolbox, but it wobbles and slips. You bring another screw that looks almost identical, yet this one fits perfectly! Why? Because in the real world of engineering, manufacturing, and science, tiny differences in length matter immensely.

Sonu's Toy Repair Discovery (NCERT Opening Activity)

Sonu watched his mother attempting to fix a toy by fastening two plastic pieces with a screw. When the first screw failed to hold, she replaced it with another screw that looked the same to Sonu. When Sonu inspected them side-by-side above a centimeter ruler, he noticed:

  • Screw A passed 2 cm and reached 7 small tick marks: length = 27/10 cm (or 2.7 cm).
  • Screw B passed 3 cm and reached 2 small tick marks: length = 32/10 cm (or 3.2 cm).

If Sonu only used whole centimeter numbers, both screws would simply be called "about 2 or 3 cm". That coarse description completely ignores the 0.5 cm difference that makes the toy work or break!

A standard school ruler provides whole centimeter divisions numbered 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ... up to 15. Between every two consecutive centimeter marks (e.g., between 2 cm and 3 cm), the 1 cm unit length is subdivided into 10 equal parts. Each small part is called a millimeter (mm), which is exactly one-tenth of a centimeter (1/10 cm).

3.2 A Tenth Part (ଏକ-ଦଶମାଂଶ: 1/10 = 0.1)

When any unit length (or whole quantity) is cut into 10 equal pieces, each individual piece is called one-tenth (1/10).

1 Whole Unit = 10 × ≤ft(1/10) = 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 = 10/10 = 1

Consider measuring an everyday classroom pencil. If the pencil extends past 3 whole units and reaches 4 small tenth divisions, we can describe its length in two mathematically identical ways:

  1. Mixed Fraction Form: 34/10 units (Read as: "Three units and four-tenths"). Here, (3 × 1) + ≤ft(4 × 1/10).
  2. Improper Fraction Form: Since 1 unit contains 10 tenths, 3 units contain 3 × 10 = 30 tenths. Adding the extra 4 tenths gives 34/10 units (Read as: "Thirty-four tenths").
Reading and Writing Fractional Tenths
  • 41/10 → Read as "Four and one-tenth" (Value = 4.1)
  • 4/10 → Read as "Four tenths" or "Four one-tenths" (Value = 0.4)
  • 41/10 → Read as "Forty-one tenths" (Value = 4.1, identical to 41/10)
  • 411/10 → Read as "Forty-one and one-tenth" (Value = 41.1)

Real-World Biology: Insect & Body Measurements in Tenths

In the NCERT textbook, Sonu measures his arm and insect body parts to practice combining and comparing tenths:

3.3 A Hundredth Part (ଏକ-ଶତାଂଶ: 1/100 = 0.01)

What if an object falls between two tenth markings? For instance, suppose a folded paper strip is longer than 44/10 units but shorter than 45/10 units. How do we measure it accurately?

We repeat the exact same division principle! We take one-tenth and subdivide it into 10 equal micro-parts. Since the whole unit had 10 tenths, dividing each tenth into 10 parts creates:

10 × 10 = 100 Equal Parts in 1 Whole Unit!

Each tiny division is called one-hundredth (1/100).

Fundamental Place Value Equivalences of Hundredths
  • 1 Tenth = 10 Hundredths: 1/10 = 10/100 = 0.1 = 0.10
  • 1 Whole Unit = 100 Hundredths: 1 = 100/100 = 1.00
  • Converting Tenths to Hundredths: Multiply numerator and denominator by 10. For example, 3/10 = 30/100.

3.4 Decimal Place Value & Historical Evolution (ଦଶମିକ ସ୍ଥାନମାନ ପ୍ରଣାଳୀ ଓ ଏହାର ଇତିହାସ)

The Indian place value system is based on the magic number 10 (which is why it is called the decimal system, from the Latin word decem, meaning ten).

  • When moving one step to the left, the place value is multiplied by 10:
    1 (Ones) × 10 = 10 (Tens) × 10 = 100 (Hundreds) × 10 = 1000 (Thousands).
  • Conversely, when moving one step to the right, the place value is divided by 10:
    1000 ÷ 10 = 100 ÷ 10 = 10 ÷ 10 = 1 (Ones).

What happens if we dare to take one more step to the right of the Ones place? We divide by 10 again!

1 ÷ 10 = 1/10 (Tenths) →{÷ 10} 1/100 (Hundredths) →{÷ 10} 1/1000 (Thousandths)

The Fascinating Historical Journey of Decimal Notation

How did humanity arrive at this elegant dot ('.') on paper? The NCERT textbook highlights an inspiring historical trajectory:

  • Ancient India: The foundation of the Base-10 system was invented by ancient Indian mathematicians and astronomers. In the 8th century CE, the great mathematician Śhrīdharāchārya wrote seminal treatises on arithmetic and algebra that utilized positional decimal thinking.
  • Abūl Ḥassan al-Uqlīdisī (c. 950 CE): In Damascus, Arab mathematician al-Uqlīdisī authored Kitāb al-Fuṣūl fī al-Ḥisāb al Hindī ("The Book of Chapters on Indian Arithmetic"). He pioneered writing decimals using a vertical stroke above the units place: he wrote the number 0.059375 as 0'059375.
  • The 15th-Century Transition: Mathematicians experimented with various separator marks:
    • Vertical bar or stroke on the units digit.
    • Writing decimal parts in different ink colors.
    • Superscript notation indicating fractional places: for example, 0.36 was written as 36^2 (meaning 36 to the second fractional place).
  • 16th Century to Modern Day: Scottish mathematician John Napier (1616) and German Jesuit mathematician Christopher Clavius popularized the period/dot ('.') to separate the integer from the fractional part. French mathematician François Viète championed the comma (','). Today, many European nations write 1 000,5, while India, the UK, the US, and international science adopt the decimal point 1,000.5.

Equivalent Decimals and the Mystery of Trailing Zeros

The Trailing Zero Rule (ଶେଷ ଶୂନ୍ୟ ନିୟମ)

In the textbook discussion, Sonu claims that 0.2 is identical to 0.20 and 0.200. Zara wonders if adding zeros creates a larger number, just like writing 2, 20, 200 in whole numbers. Who is right?

Sonu is 100% correct!

0.2 = 2/10 = 2 × 10/10 × 10 = 20/100 = 0.20 = 20 × 10/100 × 10 = 200/1000 = 0.200

Key Difference:
• In whole numbers, placing a zero on the right shifts every digit to a 10× higher place value (2 → 20).
• In decimals, appending trailing zeros to the extreme right of the decimal part simply partitions existing parts into smaller slices without adding any new amount!
• Beware: A zero placed between the decimal point and a digit is a placeholder that divides the value by 10! Thus, 0.02 = 2/100, which is ten times smaller than 0.2 = 20/100.

The 4-Step Left-to-Right Decimal Comparison Algorithm

To compare any two decimals (e.g., comparing 6.456 and 6.465), follow this strict step-by-step algorithm:

  1. Step 1: Compare the Whole Number Part (Highest Place Value):
    The number with the greater whole number is strictly greater.
    Example: In 13.800 vs 3.81, since 13 > 3, 13.800 > 3.81 immediately. (Decimal digits do not matter!).
  2. Step 2: If Whole Numbers are Equal, Compare the Tenths Place:
    Example: In 1.32 vs 1.23, both have whole number 1. Comparing tenths: 3 tenths > 2 tenths. Hence, 1.32 > 1.23.
  3. Step 3: If Tenths are Equal, Compare the Hundredths Place:
    Example: In 6.456 vs 6.465, whole numbers (6) and tenths (4) are identical. Comparing hundredths: 5 hundredths < 6 hundredths. Therefore, 6.465 > 6.456.
  4. Step 4: If Hundredths are Equal, Compare the Thousandths Place:
    Example: In 1.090 vs 1.009, whole numbers (1) and tenths (0) match. Hundredths: 9 vs 0. Since 9 > 0, 1.090 > 1.009.
Deadly Trap: More Digits Does NOT Mean Greater Value!

Students often falsely assume that 0.12345 is greater than 0.9 because "12345 is bigger than 9".
In reality, 0.9 = 0.90000 = 90000/100000, whereas 0.12345 = 12345/100000.
Because 9 tenths is vastly greater than 1 tenth, 0.9 > 0.12345! Always pad with zeros to compare like place values.

3.7 Addition and Subtraction of Decimals (ଦଶମିକ ସଂଖ୍ୟାର ଯୋଗ ଓ ବିୟୋଗ)

The golden rule for decimal arithmetic is universal and straightforward:

★ THE GOLDEN RULE: ALWAYS ALIGN THE DECIMAL POINTS VERTICALLY! ★

Why must the points align? Because in arithmetic, you can only combine like quantities. You cannot add tenths to hundredths or ones to tenths. Lining up the decimal points guarantees that:

  • Ones add to Ones
  • Tenths add to Tenths
  • Hundredths add to Hundredths
  • Thousandths add to Thousandths

3.8 Real-World Decimal & Measurement Disasters (ଦଶମିକ ତ୍ରୁଟି ଓ ବାସ୍ତବ ଦୁର୍ଘଟଣା)

In middle school math, an arithmetic mistake might cost you 1 mark on a test. But in aviation, space exploration, banking, and civil engineering, a misplaced decimal point or unit mismatch can cause catastrophic loss of life and billions of dollars!

Disaster 1: The Gimli Glider (Air Canada Flight 143, 1983)

In July 1983, a brand new Boeing 767 passenger jet flying at 41,000 feet ran completely out of jet fuel mid-air over central Canada.
What went wrong? Canada had just transitioned to the metric system. Ground fueling crew calculated fuel density using 1.77 pounds/litre instead of 0.8 kg/litre. As a result, they loaded 22,300 pounds of fuel instead of 22,300 kilograms—less than half the fuel required for the journey!
Miraculously, Captain Robert Pearson, an experienced glider pilot, managed to glide the 132-ton engineless jet safely to an abandoned airstrip in Gimli, saving all 69 souls on board.

Disaster 2: The Amsterdam City Council €188 Million Bug (2013)

In 2013, the municipal finance office of Amsterdam, Netherlands, was disbursing welfare housing subsidies. Due to a software coding error, a decimal point was mistakenly omitted or shifted by two places.
Instead of disbursing a total of €1.88 million, the computer system transferred €188 million directly into the bank accounts of surprised citizens! The city had to spend millions in legal and administrative efforts to recover the overpaid funds.

Disaster 3: NASA Mars Climate Orbiter ($125 Million Space Loss, 1999)

In 1999, NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter approached the Red Planet after a 10-month interplanetary voyage. Upon entering orbit, the spacecraft plunged too deep into Mars' dense upper atmosphere and disintegrated.
The Cause: The aerospace engineering team at Lockheed Martin produced thruster performance data in English imperial units (pound-force seconds), while NASA's navigation team at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory assumed the data was in metric units (newton-seconds). One pound-force equals 4.45 newtons. That single unit conversion mismatch burned up a $125 million spacecraft.

The Cricket Scoreboard Illusion: Why 4.2 Overs is NOT 4.2 in Base-10!

If you watch cricket, scoreboards display overs as 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5, 5.0. Young students often make the mistake of thinking:

"4.2 overs + 0.5 overs = 4.7 overs" --- THIS IS ABSOLUTELY FALSE!

Why? In cricket, 1 complete over consists of 6 legal balls, NOT 10 balls!

  • The notation "4.2 overs" means 4 completed overs and 2 balls.
  • In fractional arithmetic: 4 overs + 2/6 of an over = 41/3 ≈ 4.333... overs.
  • If a bowler bowls 4.5 overs and then bowls 2 more balls:
    The 1st ball completes the over (making 5.0 overs).
    The 2nd ball starts the next over (making 5.1 overs).
    It does never become "4.7 overs"!

This is a brilliant lesson: decimal points should only be used when units follow a base-10 structure! When units are based on 6 (cricket balls), 12 (inches/dozen), or 60 (minutes/seconds), decimal notation will mislead you unless converted properly.

Step 5: Final Concluding Statement

No, a decimal with more digits is not necessarily greater. The value is strictly determined by comparing digits from highest to lowest place value (left to right).

12.5 + 3.125 (WRONG! Aligning 5 under 5 adds tenths to thousandths!) ------- 4.375 (DISASTROUS ERROR!)

Why Students Fall For It: In Grades 1 to 5, students spent years aligning whole numbers to the right margin. Habit takes over in exam stress.
The Pro-Fix: Never look at the right edge! Draw a vertical dotted guide line through the decimal points first, pad empty spaces with zeros (12.500 + 3.125), and compute:

12.500 + 3.125 -------- 15.625 (CORRECT!)
Trap 2: The "More Digits is Bigger" Fallacy

The Fatal Blunder: Claiming that 0.0899 > 0.8 or that 0.795 > 0.8 because 795 is bigger than 8.
Why Students Fall For It: Whole-number intuition assumes longer numbers have greater magnitude.
The Pro-Fix: Always pad both numbers to the same number of decimal places before comparing:
0.8 = 0.800.
Now compare 800 thousandths with 795 thousandths: 800 > 795 ⇒ 0.8 > 0.795!

Trap 3: The Missing Zero in Metric Conversions

The Fatal Blunder: Converting 4 m 6 cm into meters and writing 4.6 m.
Why Students Fall For It: Students forget that 1 meter has 100 centimeters (2 decimal places), not 10!
The Pro-Fix:
6 cm = 6/100 m = 0.06 m.
Therefore, 4 m 6 cm = 4 + 0.06 = 4.06 m. (4.6 m would be 4 m 60 cm!).

Trap 4: The "Invisible Decimal Point" in Whole Number Subtraction

The Fatal Blunder: When solving 15 - 3.45, a student writes 12.45 or 11.45.
Why Students Fall For It: The whole number 15 does not have a visible decimal point, so students just subtract 15 − 3 = 12 and tack on the .45!
The Pro-Fix: Every whole number has an invisible decimal point immediately after the ones digit! Always write 15 as 15.00, then borrow across the zeros:

15.00 - 3.45 ------- 11.55 (CORRECT!)
Trap 5: The Cricket Overs Decimal Trap

The Fatal Blunder: In a cricket problem, adding 4.5 overs + 2 balls and writing 4.7 overs.
Why Students Fall For It: Confusing cricket notation (which is base 6) with mathematical decimals (which are strictly base 10).
The Pro-Fix: In cricket, 6 balls make an over.
4.5 overs + 1 ball = 5.0 overs!
5.0 overs + 1 ball = 5.1 overs! (It never reaches 4.6 or 4.7).

15 Master Revision Points (Last-Minute Golden Nuggets)

Memorize These 15 Flash Points Before Stepping Into the Exam Hall
  1. Need for Smaller Units: Continuous measurements require subdivisions because whole numbers leave unmeasured gaps between consecutive units.
  2. One-Tenth (1/10 = 0.1): 1 whole unit divided into 10 equal parts gives tenths. Exactly 10 tenths make 1 whole unit.
  3. One-Hundredth (1/100 = 0.01): 1 tenth divided into 10 micro-parts gives hundredths. 10 hundredths make 1 tenth; 100 hundredths make 1 whole unit.
  4. One-Thousandth (1/1000 = 0.001): 1 hundredth divided into 10 parts gives thousandths. 1,000 thousandths make 1 whole unit.
  5. Base-10 Multiplier Rule: Moving one position left multiplies place value by 10; moving one position right divides place value by 10.
  6. Decimal Point Pivot: The dot ('.') is a fixed boundary stone separating whole numbers (left) from fractional parts (right).
  7. Historical Pedigree: Ancient Indian mathematicians (Śhrīdharāchārya) pioneered place-value arithmetic; al-Uqlīdisī used vertical strokes; Napier & Clavius established the decimal point.
  8. Metric Length Hierarchy: 1 mm = 0.1 cm = 0.001 m = 0.000001 km. Exactly 1 million mm make 1 km.
  9. Metric Mass & Capacity: 1 g = 0.001 kg and 1 mL = 0.001 L. Always divide by 1,000 when converting to kilo/litre.
  10. Currency Decimalization: 1 paisa = ₹0.01. Always ensure 2 decimal places when writing rupees and paise (e.g. ₹5.04, not ₹5.4).
  11. Trailing Zeros Invariance: Appending or removing zeros at the extreme right of the decimal part does not change the value (0.2 = 0.20 = 0.200).
  12. Left-to-Right Comparison Rule: First compare whole numbers. If equal, compare tenths; if tenths are equal, compare hundredths. The first different digit determines the larger number.
  13. Digit Count Deception: A decimal with more digits is NOT necessarily greater (0.9 > 0.12345). Always equalize lengths with zeros before comparing.
  14. Vertical Point Alignment: In addition and subtraction, always align the decimal points on a single straight vertical line so like place values combine.
  15. Whole Number Point Padding: When subtracting from a whole number (e.g., 20 - 4.075), write 20 as 20.000 before performing borrowing across zeros.
