🌲 Chapter 10: The Tunnel (Ruskin Bond)

1. Narrative Synopsis & The Jungle Cutting

Ranji’s Exploration: Ranji, a young boy on school holidays, cycles along a forest track near the Himalayan foothills. He discovers a dramatic railway cutting where the tracks pierce straight through the heart of a granite hill through Tunnel No. 13.

Sunder Singh, the Solitary Guardian: At the tunnel entrance stands a small stone hut inhabited by Sunder Singh, the railway watchman. Sunder Singh's lonely job is to inspect the tracks, clear fallen boulders, and wave the kerosene signal lamp to guide the trains safely through the pitch-black tunnel.

The Presence of the Beast: Sunder Singh casually informs Ranji that a wild leopard lives in the dense shisham and sal forest above the cutting. The leopard regularly uses the tunnel as a cool shelter during scorching afternoons and as a convenient shortcut across the hill. When Ranji asks if he feels afraid, Sunder Singh replies serenely: “I am used to him. He does not bother me if I do not bother him. The tunnel belongs to the leopard as much as to the train.”

The Breath-stopping Climax: As dusk gathers, the distant hoot of the evening mail train vibrates along the rails. Sunder Singh grabs his lit kerosene lamp and enters the cutting. Suddenly, Ranji spots the luminous green eyes and sleek golden silhouette of the leopard inside the tunnel mouth! The train roars closer, its giant front headlight piercing the darkness like a dragon's eye. Sunder Singh stands rock-still, raising his lantern high. At the very last moment, before the rushing engine enters the archway, the leopard leaps effortlessly up the sheer rock cutting and vanishes into the dark forest! The train thunders through without incident, its coaches glowing like a ribbon of fire.

🚂 Chapter 11: Travel (Poem by Edna St. Vincent Millay)

1. Complete Poem Text

The railroad track is miles away,
    And the day is loud with voices speaking,
Yet there isn’t a train goes by all day
    But I hear its whistle shrieking.

All night there isn’t a train goes by,
    Though the night is still for sleep and dreaming,
But I see its cinders red on the sky,
    And hear its engine steaming.

My heart is warm with the friends I make,
    And better friends I’ll not be knowing;
Yet there isn’t a train I wouldn’t take,
    No matter where it’s going.
— Edna St. Vincent Millay

2. Stanza-by-Stanza Master Explanation

  • Stanza 1 (The Day & The Audible Call): Although the railroad track is located many miles away across the countryside, and the daytime is packed with human conversations and chores, the speaker's mind remains transfixed by the distant railway. Every single train that passes makes its whistle echo in her heart as if it were shrieking directly to her.
  • Stanza 2 (The Night & The Visual Call): When stillness falls over the countryside and ordinary people sleep and dream, the speaker stays awake watching the horizon. In her imagination and memory, she sees the train's glowing red coal cinders spewing into the dark starry sky and hears the heavy chug and hiss of the steam engine.
  • Stanza 3 (The Paradox of Wanderlust): In the final poignant quatrain, the poet clarifies that she is not running away from unhappiness. She cherishes her warm, wonderful companions and knows she could never find better friends. Yet, the irresistible urge to travel, explore the unknown world, and seek adventure is so overwhelming that she would board any train, without even asking its final destination!
💡 Poetic Devices Decoded

• Rhyme Scheme: Strict ABAB (away/day, speaking/shrieking; by/sky, dreaming/steaming; make/take, knowing/going).
• Personification: "whistle shrieking" (attributing a sharp human cry of longing to a mechanical train whistle).
• Sensory Imagery: Visual (cinders red on the sky), Auditory (voices speaking, whistle shrieking, engine steaming), Tactile/Emotional (heart is warm).
• Paradox: Deep love for home friends combined with an unstoppable desire to leave on any train.

🏔️ Chapter 12: Conquering the Summit (Bachendri Pal & Arunima Sinha)

1. Two Himalayan Legends & Their Historic Ascents

Bachendri Pal (The Trailblazer): Born in a simple village in Uttarakhand, Bachendri Pal defied societal discouragement to pursue mountaineering. On May 23, 1984, braving sub-zero blizzards, vertical ice-walls, and life-threatening avalanches, she reached the 8,848-metre summit of Mount Everest, etching her name as the first Indian woman to conquer the world’s roof.

Arunima Sinha (Triumph Over Tragedy): A talented national volleyball champion from Uttar Pradesh, Arunima's life was shattered in April 2011 when armed robbers threw her out of a moving train for resisting a robbery. A passing train on the opposite track crushed her left leg. Surgeons had to amputate her leg below the knee to save her life.

The Decision to Climb: Lying in her hospital bed at AIIMS, surrounded by pity, Arunima refused to be treated as a victim. She made a vow: "People are pitying me. I will not live on pity. I will climb the highest mountain in the world!"

The Mentorship of Bachendri Pal: When Arunima met Bachendri Pal at the Tata Steel Adventure Foundation, Pal looked at her with tears of admiration and said: “My child, you have already conquered your inner Everest the day you made this decision with a prosthetic limb! Now you only need to show the world the date!”

The Summit Triumph (May 21, 2013): Fitted with a carbon-fibre prosthetic limb, Arunima trained through agony. When blood oozed from her amputated stump, she refused to stop. On May 21, 2013, at 10:55 AM, Arunima Sinha stood on the summit of Mount Everest—the first female amputee in human history to do so! She subsequently went on to scale the Seven Summits (the highest peak of every continent: Kilimanjaro, Elbrus, Kosciuszko, Aconcagua, Denali, and Mount Vinson in Antarctica).

2. Grammar Topic A: Punctuation Masterclass (Colons, Semicolons & Dashes)

Punctuation Mark Primary Grammatical Function Rule & Textbook Example
Colon ( : ) Introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation that elaborates on the first clause. The mountaineer packed three vital tools: crampons, an ice axe, and oxygen cylinders.
Semicolon ( ; ) Connects two independent clauses that are closely related in thought without a conjunction. The day was loud with speaking voices; the night was quiet for sleep and dreaming.
Em Dash ( — ) Creates an emphatic pause or adds a sudden parenthetical thought. The wild beast—a sleek golden leopard—leaped up the rocky wall.

3. Grammar Topic B: Expressing Past Habits (Used to vs Would vs Simple Past)

💡 Past Habit Rules Decoded
  • Used to: Expresses past habits OR past states that are no longer true (We used to live in a mountain village [State]; He used to cycle every morning [Action]).
  • Would: Expresses repeated past actions only, NEVER past states (Every evening, the watchman would light his lantern [Correct]; He would live near the forest [Incorrect!]).
  • Simple Past: Used for specific single completed events in past time (Yesterday, Ranji visited Tunnel 13).

4. Poetic Form: The Diamante Poem

A Diamante poem is a seven-line diamond-shaped poem that contrasts or compares two opposing concepts using specific parts of speech:

Line 1: Beginning Topic (Noun)
Line 2: Two Adjectives describing Topic 1
Line 3: Three -ing Participles describing Topic 1
Line 4: Four Nouns (Two for Topic 1, Two for Topic 2)
Line 5: Three -ing Participles describing Topic 2
Line 6: Two Adjectives describing Topic 2
Line 7: Ending Opposite Topic (Noun)

🏔️ NCERT Solutions: Conquering the Summit
Let Us Think and Reflect (Page 172) Bachendri Pal's Words to Arunima

Extract: Bachendri Pal instilled confidence in Arunima and said, “My child, you have already conquered your inner Everest the day you decided to climb with a prosthetic limb!”

View Step-by-Step Teacher Answer

(i) What is the "inner Everest"? The inner Everest refers to overcoming fear, despair, social pity, self-doubt, and physical trauma after a catastrophic accident.

(ii) Why did Bachendri Pal say she had already conquered it? Because the greatest battle is fought in the human mind. Most people would have surrendered to despair after losing a leg, but Arunima resolved to climb the world's most perilous mountain. Having conquered her mind, the physical mountain was merely a matter of time and discipline.

3-Mark SAQ Why is the ending of Edna Millay's 'Travel' considered a paradox?
View Model Answer

A paradox is a seemingly self-contradictory statement that reveals a deeper truth:

  • The speaker sincerely treasures the warm, loving friendships she has at home, admitting she could never know better friends.
  • Yet, in the very next breath, she confesses she would board any passing train regardless of its destination.
  • This reveals that wanderlust is not driven by unhappiness with one's companions, but by an innate human thirst to explore the vast unknown.

Part C: Long Answer / Competency Value-Based Questions (5 Marks)

5-Mark Value Question "True disability exists only in a defeated mind." Elaborate with reference to Arunima Sinha's journey in 'Conquering the Summit'.
View CBSE Topper Answer

Introduction: Arunima Sinha’s journey from a hospital bed to the pinnacle of Mount Everest is one of the most heroic chapters in modern human history.

Overcoming Unimaginable Adversity:

  • Refusing Victimhood: After being thrown from a train by robbers and losing her leg, society looked upon Arunima with pity. Instead of accepting a life of dependence, she transformed her tragedy into an audacious challenge.
  • Mental Resolve Preceding Physical Victory: As mentor Bachendri Pal remarked, Arunima conquered her "inner Everest" the moment she made the decision to climb with a prosthetic limb. True courage is won in the mind before it is manifested in action.
  • Physical Endurance: During the grueling ascent through the Khumbu Icefall and the Death Zone, her artificial leg slipped repeatedly, causing agonizing friction and bleeding on her stump. Yet, fueled by national pride and sheer willpower, she pressed forward.

Conclusion: Arunima proved to the world that physical limitations cannot shackle the human spirit. She went on to scale the Seven Summits of all seven continents, reminding millions of students that faith and perseverance can conquer any summit.

⚡ "Exam Hall Mistakes" & 5-Minute Rapid Revision Cheat Sheet

⚠️ Common Student Mistakes in Exams & How to Avoid Them

Common Student Error Why It Loses Marks CBSE Correct Method
Using would to describe past physical states (e.g., He would be an amputee). 'Would' expresses repeated past actions, never past states or conditions. Use used to be or was for states of being.
Claiming Arunima Sinha was the first Indian woman on Everest. Bachendri Pal was the first Indian woman (1984); Arunima was the world's first female amputee (2013). Distinguish clearly between Bachendri Pal (1984) and Arunima Sinha (2013).
Using a semicolon before introducing a list of items. Semicolons connect independent clauses; colons introduce lists. Use a colon ( : ) to introduce lists after a complete thought.
Assuming the leopard in Ruskin Bond's story was shot or captured. The story is a celebration of non-violent wildlife coexistence. Describe how the leopard bounded safely up the cutting into the forest.
