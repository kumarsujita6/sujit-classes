Chapter 9: From the Rulers to the Ruled: Types of Governments

Exploring the Evolution of Political Authority: Monarchies, Republics, Democracies, Dictatorships, Theocracies, Oligarchies, and Citizen Vigilance.

Theme: Governance & Democracy Reading Time: ~35 Mins Complete Notes & Zero-Omission Solutions

1. What is Government and What are Its Functions?

Have you ever wondered who decides where roads should be laid, how water and electricity reach our homes, who protects our borders, and who settles disputes when people disagree? All these complex responsibilities are managed by the Government.

A Government is the governing body or system that makes, implements, and enforces laws, provides essential civic services, maintains internal order, and protects the safety and welfare of all people residing within a nation.

Teacher's Perspective: Think of a country as a massive, lively school with 1.4 billion students! Without a clear timetable, rules for the playground, and teachers to coordinate lunch and exams, there would be total chaos. The government acts as that organizing body for the entire nation!

Seven Primary Roles of Government

  1. Maintaining Law and Order: Enforcing police systems to keep citizens safe from crime and violence.
  2. Ensuring Peace, Stability, and Security: Resolving internal communal tensions and preventing civil strife.
  3. Managing International Relations: Conducting diplomacy, trade treaties, and cultural exchange with other nations.
  4. National Defence: Maintaining armed forces (Army, Navy, Air Force) to guard national borders and sovereignty.
  5. Delivering Public Goods and Services: Building schools, hospitals, drinking water grids, highways, and railway networks.
  6. Managing the Economy: Collecting taxes, controlling inflation, generating employment, and issuing national currency.
  7. Welfare and Social Justice: Providing rations, pensions, disaster relief, and affirmative action for disadvantaged groups.

2. What Makes Governments Different?

Across world history and in contemporary international politics, nations are organized under remarkably diverse governmental systems. A government's nature depends on four fundamental structural questions:

Defining Question Democratic Government (e.g., India) Non-Democratic Government (e.g., Monarchy / Dictatorship)
1. Who decides "This is the government"? (Source of Authority) The Citizens: Power originates from the people through the sovereign ballot box. The Ruler / Faith / Military: Power originates from royal inheritance, military force, or religious decree.
2. How is the government formed? (Mode of Succession) Periodic Elections: Universal adult franchise at fixed intervals (e.g., every 5 years in India). Hereditary Bloodline / Coup: Eldest royal son ascends the throne, or military general seizes control by arms.
3. What are the parts and what do they do? (Structure) Separation of Powers: Clearly demarcated independent Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary bound by a written Constitution. Concentration of Power: The monarch or dictator acts as maker, executor, and judge of all laws simultaneously.
4. Who is the government working for? (Ultimate Goal) Universal Welfare: Equality, fundamental rights, and prosperity for all citizens without discrimination. Regime Survival / Elites: Enrichment of the royal clan, aristocratic elite, or a single religious orthodoxy.

3. Democratic Governments Around the World

Today, democracy is the most widely adopted and admired form of governance on Earth. However, democratic practice manifests in two primary models:

Direct Democracy vs Representative Democracy

Direct Democracy

All eligible citizens gather directly to propose, debate, and vote upon every single legislation and policy. While practiced in ancient Greek city-states (Athens) and in modern Swiss cantons via referendums, it is impractical for large, populous modern nations.

Representative Democracy

Citizens elect trusted representatives through periodic secret ballot voting. These elected representatives sit in parliaments or assemblies to draft laws and run the administration on behalf of the people. This is the model followed by India.

4. A Peek into History: Early Republics and Indian Democratic Traditions

Democracy is often portrayed as an exclusive modern Western invention. However, archaeological and literary evidence proves that India has deep, venerable democratic and republican roots stretching back over 2,500 years!

The Gana-Sangha Republics (600 BCE)

In Chapter 4, we learned that during the Second Urbanisation, several Mahājanapadas operated as republics (Gaṇa-Saṅghas) rather than hereditary monarchies. The foremost example was the Vajji (Vṛji) confederacy centered at Vaishali:

  • Power was vested in an assembly of clan elders (the Lichchhavis) rather than a single autocratic king.
  • Leaders and delegates were chosen based on merit, debate, and character.
  • Decisions on defence, taxation, and treaties were resolved through open collective discussions (Santhāgāra) until consensus was achieved.

The Chola Uttaramerur Inscriptions (10th Century CE)

Found on the granite walls of the Vaikuntha Perumal Temple at Uttaramerur (Kanchipuram district, Tamil Nadu) from the reign of Chola King Parantaka I (907–955 CE), these inscriptions describe a breathtakingly sophisticated, corruption-free grassroots democracy:

The Kuda Olai (Pot-Voting) System:

The village was partitioned into 30 wards (kudumbus). Candidates qualified only if they owned tax-paying land, built a house on their own site, were aged between 35 and 70, possessed clean character, and understood Vedic laws and administration.

On election day, names of eligible candidates were inscribed on dried palm-leaf tickets (olai). The tickets were placed inside a large clay pot (kuda). In front of the assembled village elders, an innocent young child was asked to draw one ticket per ward. The drawn name became the committee member for one year! Anyone who failed to submit public accounts or committed bribery was immediately disqualified along with their family.

Figure 9.3: The Uttaramerur Kuda Olai secret pot-lottery election system in Chola-era Tamil Nadu.

Contrast this with ancient Athenian democracy in Greece (5th century BCE): while Athenian citizens could vote, women, foreigners, working laborers, and enslaved people (who made up the majority) were completely excluded from voting!

5. Non-Democratic Governments: Monarchy, Theocracy, Dictatorship & Oligarchy

1. Monarchy & Rājadharma (Indian Ideal vs Absolute Tyranny)

A Monarchy is ruled by a king or queen (monarch) who inherits power hereditarily through royal family succession.

  • Western Absolute Divine Right: In European history, many kings claimed "Divine Right of Kings," asserting their decrees were infallible God's word and subjects had zero rights.
  • Indian Rājadharma: In ancient India, kingship was strictly bounded by Dharma. As Bhīṣhma instructs Yudhiṣṭhira from his bed of arrows in the Shānti Parva of the Mahābhārata, the monarch's supreme duty is the safety and joy of his subjects. A king who oppresses his subjects violates Rājadharma and loses legitimacy.
  • The Cobbler and King Chandrāpīḍa: In Kalhaṇa's 12th-century historical chronicle Rājataraṅgiṇī, the Kashmiri King Chandrāpīḍa sought to build a sacred temple, but a humble cobbler's hut stood on the land. Instead of bulldozing the hut, the king personally went on foot to the cobbler, acknowledged that a humble hut is as precious to a poor man as a palace is to a monarch, and respectfully requested permission while paying handsome voluntary compensation!

2. Theocracy

A Theocracy is a government ruled according to religious doctrines where religious clerics hold supreme state authority.

  • Islamic Republic of Iran: Ultimate authority rests with the Supreme Leader (an Islamic cleric chosen for life by a body of scholars), while an elected President and Parliament manage day-to-day administration under Islamic law.
  • Vatican City: Governed directly by the Bishop of Rome (The Pope) as absolute head of the Roman Catholic Church.

3. Dictatorship

A Dictatorship is an authoritarian system where a single individual or a military junta holds unchecked, absolute power, unfettered by constitutions or independent courts.

Historical Atrocities of Dictators:
  • Adolf Hitler (Nazi Germany, 1933–1945): Banned all opposition, triggered World War II, and orchestrated the industrial murder of 6 million Jews and hundreds of thousands of Romani people in the Holocaust.
  • Idi Amin (Uganda, 1971–1979): Military dictator responsible for the massacre of over 300,000 citizens and the brutal expulsion of 60,000 people of Indian origin.
  • North Korea (Shane's Case Study): Citizens endure total state surveillance, mandatory long-term military conscription, zero access to the world internet, and strict decrees prescribing approved hairstyles and clothing.

4. Oligarchy

Derived from the Greek words oligos (few) and arkho (to rule), an Oligarchy is governance by a tiny, self-serving clique of ultra-wealthy corporate tycoons, land barons, or royal aristocrats who pull the strings of state power for personal enrichment.

6. Why Democracy Matters & Guarding Against Challenges

Why do more than half the nations on Earth embrace democracy? Because only in a democracy are the rulers directly answerable to the ruled!

Hurdles Faced by Modern Democracies

Even the strongest democracies require constant maintenance and citizen alertness. Major hurdles include:

  • Corruption & Money Power: Massive illicit spending during elections can lead politicians to favor wealthy donors over ordinary citizens.
  • Wealth Inequality: When economic gains concentrate in fewer hands, poorer citizens struggle to exercise real political influence.
  • Disinformation & Fake News: Algorithms and media propaganda polarizing citizens and distorting truth.
  • Threats to Judicial Independence: Attempts by powerful political executives to influence court appointments.
  • Voter Cynicism & Low Turnout: When educated citizens refuse to vote, corrupt interests easily dominate.
The Eternal Golden Rule of Democracy: "Eternal vigilance is the price of liberty." A democracy remains strong only when citizens actively read news from diverse sources, question officials respectfully, cast votes conscientiously, and defend the constitutional rights of their neighbors!

NCERT Q2 Which type of Government does India have? And why is it called that type?

India has a Sovereign, Democratic, Parliamentary Republic.

Why It Is Called So:

  • Representative Democracy: Indian citizens elect their representatives to Parliament and State Assemblies via Universal Adult Suffrage (every citizen aged 18+ has one equal vote).
  • Parliamentary Form: The executive government (Prime Minister and Council of Ministers) is drawn from within the elected Parliament and remains accountable to the Lok Sabha. If they lose majority support, they must resign.
  • Republic: The Head of State (the President of India) is an elected figure serving a 5-year term, not a hereditary king or queen.
NCERT Q3 Three reasons why it is important for the judiciary to be independent in a democracy

Three Critical Reasons for an Independent Judiciary:

  1. Protection of Fundamental Rights: If the government passes an unjust law or the police unlawfully arrests an innocent citizen, an independent court can strike down the law and order the citizen's immediate release without political interference.
  2. Checks and Balances on Government Power: It prevents the Legislature and Executive from acting like dictators. An independent judiciary ensures that every minister, prime minister, and bureaucrat obeys the Constitution.
  3. Fair and Impartial Settlement of Disputes: An independent judiciary ensures that in legal conflicts—whether between rich corporations and poor farmers, or between State governments and the Central government—verdicts are based strictly on evidence and law, not political pressure.
NCERT Q4 Do you think democratic government is better than other forms of government? Why?

Yes, democracy is universally recognized as superior to other forms of government for the following reasons:

  • Protects Human Dignity & Rights: It guarantees freedom of speech, religion, association, and equality before law to every citizen.
  • Accountability & Peaceful Change: If rulers become corrupt, incompetent, or neglectful, citizens can vote them out peacefully through scheduled elections without bloodshed.
  • Encourages Debate & Better Decision-Making: Laws undergo transparent parliamentary scrutiny and public feedback, reducing reckless mistakes.
  • Accommodates Social Diversity: In vast multi-cultural nations like India, democracy provides mechanisms for all linguistic, regional, and religious communities to share power peacefully.
  • Self-Correcting Mechanism: Free courts, investigative journalism, and civil society protests ensure that governmental errors can be exposed and rectified.
