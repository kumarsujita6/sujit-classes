2.1 Simple Expressions, Values, and Equality

In mathematics, numbers alone are like single words, but when we connect numbers using operation signs such as addition (+), subtraction (−), multiplication (×), and division (÷), we form mathematical phrases. These phrases are known as arithmetic expressions (ପାଟୀଗାଣିତିକ ରାଶି).

What is an Arithmetic Expression?

An arithmetic expression is a meaningful combination of numbers and one or more arithmetic operations (+, −, ×, ÷) that does not contain an equals sign or inequality sign. Examples include:

  • 13 + 2 (read as "13 plus 2" or "the sum of 13 and 2")
  • 20 − 4 (read as "20 minus 4" or "the difference between 20 and 4")
  • 12 × 5 (read as "12 multiplied by 5" or "the product of 12 and 5")
  • 18 ÷ 3 (read as "18 divided by 3" or "the quotient of 18 and 3")

Every arithmetic expression has a unique Value: The value of an arithmetic expression is the single number that results when all indicated calculations are correctly carried out. For instance, the value of the expression 13 + 2 is the number 15. We use the equality symbol (=) to express this relationship:

13 + 2 = 15
One Value, Countless Expressions!

Just like a person can be called by different nicknames or wear different clothes while remaining the exact same person, a single numerical value can be expressed in infinitely many ways! Consider the number 12:

  • Using addition: 10 + 2 = 12 or 7 + 5 = 12
  • Using subtraction: 15 − 3 = 12 or 20 − 8 = 12
  • Using multiplication: 3 × 4 = 12 or 2 × 6 = 12 or 1 × 12 = 12
  • Using division: 24 ÷ 2 = 12 or 36 ÷ 3 = 12 or 48 ÷ 4 = 12

Because they all yield 12, we can link them together as equal expressions: 10 + 2 = 15 − 3 = 3 × 4 = 24 ÷ 2.

Comparing Expressions: The Power of Mathematical Reasoning

When asked to compare two expressions such as 1023 + 125 and 1022 + 128, a beginner immediately reaches for pencil and paper to perform long addition. However, a skilled mathematician uses structural comparison without computing the values at all!

Let us visualize this using the textbook's delightful marble scenario:

  • Raja's Marbles: Raja started with 1,023 marbles and received 125 more today. Total = 1023 + 125.
  • Joy's Marbles: Joy started with 1,022 marbles and received 128 more today. Total = 1022 + 128.

Let us compare their starting amounts and daily gifts:

  1. At the beginning, Raja had 1 more marble than Joy (1023 − 1022 = 1).
  2. However, during the day, Joy received 3 more marbles than Raja (128 − 125 = 3).
  3. Since Joy gained 3 extra marbles while Raja had an advantage of only 1 marble, Joy ends up with 3 − 1 = 2 marbles more than Raja!
1023 + 125 < 1022 + 128   (Joy has 2 marbles more)

Similarly, consider the subtraction comparison: 113 − 25 versus 112 − 24.

  • Raja had 113 marbles and lost 25. Joy had 112 marbles and lost 24.
  • Raja started with 1 marble more than Joy (113 − 112 = 1).
  • But Raja also lost exactly 1 marble more than Joy (25 − 24 = 1).
  • The initial advantage of +1 was perfectly balanced by the extra loss of −1. Therefore, both boys are left with the exact same number of marbles!
113 − 25 = 112 − 24 = 88

2.2 Reading and Evaluating Complex Expressions: The Ambiguity Dilemma

When an expression contains more than one operation, confusion easily arises if the expression is written without context. In language, punctuation marks like commas determine meaning:

  • "Shalini sat next to a friend with toys." → The friend has toys, and Shalini sat next to her.
  • "Shalini sat next to a friend, with toys." → Shalini has the toys, and she sat with them next to her friend.

Without punctuation, a reader cannot tell who owns the toys! Mathematics faces the exact same dilemma. Consider the story of Mallesh and Purna from the textbook:

The Classic Ambiguity: Mallesh vs Purna

Mallesh brought 30 marbles to the playground. Arun brought 5 bags of marbles with 4 marbles in each bag. To find the total number of marbles brought to the playground, Mallesh wrote:

30 + 5 × 4

Two students evaluated this raw expression:

  • Purna's Evaluation: Purna added first: 30 + 5 = 35, then multiplied: 35 × 4 = 140 marbles.
  • Mallesh's Evaluation: Mallesh multiplied first: 5 × 4 = 20, then added: 30 + 20 = 50 marbles.

Who was right? Mallesh was completely right because Arun's 5 bags had 4 marbles each, giving 20 marbles, plus Mallesh's 30 marbles = 50 marbles. Purna got it wrong because he blindly performed addition from left to right! To eliminate all ambiguity, mathematics uses two powerful tools: Brackets and the Notion of Terms.

2.3 Terms in Expressions: The Atomic Building Blocks (ପଦ)

When an expression does not have brackets specifying the order, how do we know what to calculate first? The NCERT Ganita Prakash curriculum introduces the foundational concept of Terms.

Formal Definition of a Term (ପଦ)

Terms are the parts of an arithmetic expression separated strictly by a '+' sign. When an expression has no brackets, we evaluate each term independently first, and then add the resulting values of all terms together.

For example, in the simple expression 12 + 7, there are two terms: 12 and 7. Each term is an independent quantity joined by addition.

How Do We Handle Subtraction? The Additive Inverse Revolution

What if an expression contains a minus sign, such as 83 − 14? Are there two terms, or does subtraction break our rule?

Here lies one of the most beautiful insights of mathematics: Subtracting a number is identical to adding its opposite (additive inverse)!

  • The additive inverse of +14 is −14.
  • The additive inverse of −14 is +14.
  • Therefore, subtracting 14 from 83 is rewritten as: 83 − 14 = 83 + (−14).

Now, the expression is strictly a sum of two terms: the first term is 83, and the second term is −14!

Single Terms Containing Multiplications and Divisions

Notice that phrases like 6 × 5, 4 × 6, or 16 ÷ 2 do not contain any '+' sign. Therefore, each of them forms a single indivisible term!

  • In 5 + 6 × 3, the terms are 5 and 6 × 3. (Term 2 evaluates to 18, so total = 5 + 18 = 23).
  • In 23 − 2 × 4 + 16, we rewrite as 23 + (−(2 × 4)) + 16. The terms are 23, −8, and 16.
  • In 48 − 10 × 2 + 16 ÷ 2, the terms are 48, −20, and 8.

2.4 Fundamental Laws of Addition of Terms

Once an expression is understood as a sum of terms, two monumental mathematical properties guarantee complete computational freedom:

1. The Commutative Property of Addition (ଯୋଗର କ୍ରମବିନିମୟ ନିୟମ)

Swapping terms does not change the sum. For any two terms Term 1 and Term 2:

Term 1 + Term 2 = Term 2 + Term 1

Consider Madhu flying a drone from a terrace (Example 6 in NCERT):

  • The drone first climbs 6 m up, then descends 4 m down: 6 + (−4) = 2 m above the terrace.
  • What if we swap the sequence? The drone first descends 4 m, then ascends 6 m: (−4) + 6 = 2 m above the terrace!

The final position is identical regardless of which movement occurred first.

2. The Associative Property of Addition (ଯୋଗର ସହଯୋଗୀ ନିୟମ)

Grouping terms does not change the sum. For any three terms:

[ Term 1 + Term 2 ] + Term 3 = Term 1 + [ Term 2 + Term 3 ]

Consider adding three terms: (−7) + 10 + (−11):

  • Grouping A: [ (−7) + 10 ] + (−11) = 3 + (−11) = −8
  • Grouping B: (−7) + [ 10 + (−11) ] = (−7) + (−1) = −8

Everyday Life Analogy: Hat & Shoes vs Socks & Shoes

Mathematics connects directly to our physical world:

  • Commutative Operation (Order doesn't matter): Putting on your hat and putting on your shoes. You can wear the hat first, then shoes; or shoes first, then hat. You look and feel identical!
  • Non-Commutative Operation (Order strictly matters!): Putting on your socks and putting on your shoes. If you wear socks first, then shoes, you are ready for school. But if you put on shoes first and then pull socks over them, you look ridiculous and feel awful! In math, subtraction and division are non-commutative, whereas addition of terms is always commutative.

2.5 Removing Brackets - Part I: The Sign Inversion Gate

Brackets ( ) are used to group numbers that belong together. When simplifying expressions, we often wish to remove brackets. What happens to the terms inside?

Rule 1: Brackets Preceded by a Plus Sign (+)

When brackets are preceded by a positive sign, the brackets simply dissolve without changing any sign inside:

a + (b + c) = a + b + c      a + (b − c) = a + b − c

Example (Hira's Coins): Hira has 28 coins in one pouch and 35 in another. She gifts 10 coins from the second pouch: 28 + (35 − 10) = 28 + 35 − 10 = 63 − 10 = 53 coins.

Rule 2: Brackets Preceded by a Minus Sign (−)

When brackets preceded by a negative sign are removed, EVERY term inside the bracket inverts its sign:

  • A positive term inside turns negative: a − (b + c) = a − b − c
  • A negative term inside turns positive: a − (b − c) = a − b + c
Diagram 5: Algorithmic Logic of the Bracket Unpacking and Sign Inversion Mechanism
Why does a − (b − c) become a − b + c? (The Shopkeeper Logic)

Let us understand this with two crystal-clear everyday examples:

  1. Irfan's Grocery Bill: Irfan buys biscuits for ₹15 and dal for ₹56, paying with a ₹100 note. Change = 100 − (15 + 56). The shopkeeper can subtract both items one after the other: first take away ₹15 (100 − 15 = 85), then take away ₹56 (85 − 56 = 29). Hence, 100 − (15 + 56) = 100 − 15 − 56 = 29. Both signs become negative!
  2. Correcting an Over-Deduction: Consider 500 − (250 − 100). The amount to subtract is 250 − 100 = 150, so 500 − 150 = 350. If you blindly subtract 250 from 500 (500 − 250 = 250), you subtracted 100 too much! To correct this over-subtraction, you must add back the 100: 500 − 250 + 100 = 350! That is why the minus sign in front of (−100) inverted into +100!

2.6 Removing Brackets - Part II: The Distributive Property (ବଣ୍ଟନ ନିୟମ)

When a multiplier sits outside parentheses containing a sum or difference, how do we evaluate it? This is governed by the famous Distributive Property of Multiplication over Addition and Subtraction.

The Distributive Law (ବଣ୍ଟନ ନିୟମ)

The multiple of a sum (or difference) is equal to the sum (or difference) of the individual multiples:

a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c
a × (b − c) = a × b − a × c

Look at Example 15 from the textbook: Lhamo and Norbu go to a hotel. Each orders 1 vegetable cutlet (₹43) and 1 rasgulla (₹24). How much must they pay altogether?

  • Method 1 (Total per person first): Each person owes (43 + 24) = 67. For 2 friends, total = 2 × (43 + 24) = 2 × 67 = 134.
  • Method 2 (By food item): They ordered 2 cutlets (2 × 43 = 86) and 2 rasgullas (2 × 24 = 48). Total = 86 + 48 = 134.

Both methods yield ₹134! Hence, 2 × (43 + 24) = 2 × 43 + 2 × 24.

Mental Math Superpower: Lightning Multiplication

The distributive property is the secret weapon used by human calculators:

  • Calculate 97 × 25: We notice that 97 is 100 − 3. Therefore:
    97 × 25 = (100 − 3) × 25 = 100 × 25 − 3 × 25 = 2500 − 75 = 2425
  • Calculate 104 × 15: We express 104 as 100 + 4.
    104 × 15 = (100 + 4) × 15 = 100 × 15 + 4 × 15 = 1500 + 60 = 1560
  • Calculate 49 × 50: We express 49 as 50 − 1.
    49 × 50 = (50 − 1) × 50 = 50 × 50 − 1 × 50 = 2500 − 50 = 2450

The 5 Fatal Exam Hall Traps (And How to Avoid Them)

Every year, thousands of students lose 5 to 10 marks in Class 7 mathematics examinations due to recurring mechanical mistakes. Here are the five most dangerous traps and how you can sidestep them effortlessly:

Trap 1: The Blind Left-to-Right Add/Multiply Trap (Purna's Blunder)

Why students fall into the trap: In everyday English, we read from left to right. When students see 30 + 5 × 4, their eye naturally encounters "30 + 5" first, and they add them to get 35, then multiply by 4 to get 140!

WRONG APPROACH:
30 + 5 × 4 = 35 × 4 = 140
(Blindly adding before multiplying breaks the integrity of terms!)
CORRECT APPROACH:
30 + (5 × 4) = 30 + 20 = 50
(Multiplication forms a single term that must be evaluated first!)

Teacher's Pro-Tip: Draw a mental boundary box around any multiplication or division expression. Treat 5 × 4 as a single package labeled "20".

Trap 2: The Bracket Sign-Inversion Blind Spot

Why students fall into the trap: When expanding 100 − (15 + 56) or 500 − (250 − 100), students only apply the minus sign to the first number inside and forget to flip the sign of the second number!

WRONG APPROACH:
100 − (15 + 56) = 100 − 15 + 56 = 141
500 − (250 − 100) = 500 − 250 − 100 = 150
CORRECT APPROACH:
100 − (15 + 56) = 100 − 15 − 56 = 29
500 − (250 − 100) = 500 − 250 + 100 = 350

Teacher's Pro-Tip: Remember the Inversion Gate: removing parentheses preceded by '−' inverts EVERY single sign inside (+ flips to −, and − flips to +).

Trap 3: The Half-Hearted Distributive Expansion

Why students fall into the trap: When expanding 5 × (4 + 3), students multiply the 5 by the 4, but simply copy the +3 without multiplying it by 5!

WRONG APPROACH:
5 × (4 + 3) = 5 × 4 + 3 = 20 + 3 = 23
(Forgetting to distribute the multiplier to the second addend!)
CORRECT APPROACH:
5 × (4 + 3) = 5 × 4 + 5 × 3 = 20 + 15 = 35
(The multiplier distributes fairly to BOTH terms inside!)

Teacher's Pro-Tip: Think of the multiplier as a teacher handing out chocolates: every student inside the classroom must receive a chocolate, not just the one sitting in the front row!

Trap 4: Illegally Swapping Numbers in Subtraction

Why students fall into the trap: Students confuse the commutativity of addition with subtraction. When they see 14 − 20, they mentally flip it to 20 − 14 = 6 because they are uncomfortable with negative numbers.

WRONG APPROACH:
14 − 20 = 20 − 14 = 6
(Subtraction is NOT commutative: a − b ≠ b − a!)
CORRECT APPROACH:
14 − 20 = 14 + (−20) = −6
(If you swap terms, you must carry the negative sign: (−20) + 14 = −6!)

Teacher's Pro-Tip: Never swap numbers across a minus sign without attaching the sign to the number as an additive inverse: a − b = (−b) + a.

Trap 5: The Climbing Snail Endpoint Fallacy

Why students fall into the trap: In climbing rate puzzles (climbs 3 cm day, slips 2 cm night on a 10 cm pole), students calculate the net rate (3 − 2 = 1 cm/day) and divide 10 ÷ 1 = 10 days.

WRONG APPROACH:
Net Rate = 1 cm/day ⇒ 10 cm ÷ 1 = 10 days
(Assumes the snail slips down even after already reaching the treat!)
CORRECT APPROACH:
At end of Day 7, snail is at 7 × 1 = 7 cm. On Day 8 morning, it climbs 3 cm: 7 + 3 = 10 cm! It touches the treat on Day 8 and stops climbing!

Teacher's Pro-Tip: In all rate problems with nightly drops, subtract the single daytime climb from the total height first (10 − 3 = 7 cm). Calculate days to reach that milestone, then add 1 day for the final climb!

15 Rapid Revision Points for Exam Eve

  1. An arithmetic expression is a meaningful combination of numbers and basic operators (+, −, ×, ÷) without an equals or inequality sign.
  2. Every arithmetic expression evaluates to a single unique numerical value.
  3. The equality sign (=) signifies that two expressions have identical numerical values.
  4. Terms are the building blocks of an arithmetic expression, defined strictly as parts separated by '+' signs.
  5. Subtraction is universally treated as the addition of an additive inverse: a − b = a + (−b).
  6. The Commutative Property of Addition states that swapping terms does not alter the sum: Term 1 + Term 2 = Term 2 + Term 1.
  7. The Associative Property of Addition guarantees that grouping terms in any order preserves the sum: (a + b) + c = a + (b + c).
  8. Subtraction and division are neither commutative nor associative: a − b ≠ b − a and a ÷ b ≠ b ÷ a.
  9. When an expression has no brackets, operations inside terms (multiplication and division) are performed first, followed by addition of terms.
  10. When removing brackets preceded by a positive sign (+), all internal signs remain strictly unchanged: a + (b − c) = a + b − c.
  11. When removing brackets preceded by a negative sign (−), every sign inside the bracket inverts: a − (b + c) = a − b − c and a − (b − c) = a − b + c.
  12. The Distributive Property over Addition states: a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c.
  13. The Distributive Property over Subtraction states: a × (b − c) = a × b − a × c.
  14. Mental multiplication hacks utilize the distributive property by decomposing numbers near powers of 10 (e.g., 97 × 25 = (100 − 3) × 25 = 2500 − 75 = 2425).
  15. In alternating arithmetic series 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + ... + (2n − 1) − 2n, the sum is always equal to −n.
