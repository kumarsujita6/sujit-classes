PART A (Prose 1): The Day the River Spoke – by Kamala Nair

1. Pre-Reading Context ("Before We Read" - Page 1)

  • Background of Jahnavi: Jahnavi was a bright young girl (nearly ten years old) who lived with her parents and three brothers in a coastal village in India. Her parents worked on the farm, and she had a deep dream of going to school and learning.

2. Section-by-Section Story Breakdown & Key Details

  • Scene I: Jahnavi's Tears by the Riverbank & The Speaking River (Pages 2–3):
    • Sitting by the river, a big bright tear splashed down Jahnavi's nose. Around her, nature was vibrant: a kingfisher swept down with wings like an "arrow of blue in the sunlight" to catch a fish and eat it in a thicket of bamboo, while a green lizard slithered down to the river's edge to bask in the sun.
    • Suddenly, a sleepy, murmuring voice asked, "Dear, dear! What's the matter?"
    • Jahnavi was startled. She knew it couldn't be the lizard, the kingfisher, or the parrots (because parrots shrieked in a high-pitched voice, whereas this voice was sleepy).
    • The voice revealed itself to be the River (capitalised as a proper noun because it is personified as a living friend!). The River comforted her, noting she visited it almost every day, and asked her to share her trouble quickly before it hurried to reach the sea.
  • Why Jahnavi Couldn't Go to School (Page 3):
    • Jahnavi explained that her family wouldn't let her go to school. When she was younger, she asked her mother why she couldn't go to school like Ettan (her elder brother, whose real name was Gopi; 'Ettan' means 'Elder brother') and Meena.
    • Her mother had replied, "You are too small, baby. Maybe later."
    • When Jahnavi turned five, her younger brother little Ramu was born, and her mother told her to mind her little brother while she went to the fields.
    • Now, Jahnavi was nearly ten, and she had to mind Little Appu (the smallest brother), while even little Ramu had started going to school!
  • 🔥 Jahnavi's Eagerness & Scientific Curiosity (Page 3):
    • Jahnavi wanted to learn to read like Ettan and Meena and find answers to her endless questions about nature:
      1. Why spiders are yellow in yellow flowers? (Camouflage)
      2. Why bamboo trees rustle?
      3. Why the moon always comes from behind the hills, never the other way?
      4. Why the baby fish (tadpoles) in the field water become frogs?
    • The River chuckled and told her conspiratorially (secretly) that it knew where the moon goes: "It goes down towards the sea... over the mountains and down to the sea, like me!"
  • Scene II: The River's Advice, Ships vs. Catamarans & Overcoming Fear (Pages 4–6):
    • The River believed in gender equality: "Seems to me little girls can do as much as little boys—they swim as fast as little boys."
    • The River's Advice: It advised Jahnavi to just slip along (move quietly) one morning, sit in the school, and listen to what was going on, hoping the teacher would let her stay.
    • When Jahnavi gasped, "I couldn't! I couldn't! They'd scare me! They'd chase me out," the River laughed and reminded her how brave she actually was: she wasn't afraid of the green lizard, the snake in the bamboo clump (which startled Jahnavi as she didn't know a snake lived there!), or the big noisy trains rattling past the bridge.
    • Trains, Ships & Chandu's Catamaran: The River remarked that trains are noisy and it preferred ships — "big boats, so big that they can take hundreds of people, and they sail along the sea with lights that shine all night." Ships couldn't enter the shallow river, so the River was happy with Chandu's catamaran (a boat made of tied wood) and said Chandu could take her to see a ship someday if she tried going to school first: "Remember—it's up to you!"
    • The Classroom Visit (Page 5): The next day, carrying sleeping Little Appu on her shoulder, Jahnavi gathered courage, crept into the back row, and squatted on the earthen floor listening to the teacher read a story about a prince called Ashoka who became a great king.
    • When the teacher noticed her, the boys identified her as Gopi's little sister, Jahnavi. The kind teacher assured her: "If you really, really want to come to my school, Jahnavi, we'll talk to your father about it. Don't you worry. We'll find a way."
    • A Happy Ending & Noble Ambition (Pages 5–6): The next evening, while Jahnavi was lighting the lamp, the teacher visited her father. Her mother happily supported her, revealing that when she was Jahnavi's age, she also wanted to go to school, but Jahnavi's grandmother had said 'No'. Now she declared: "Girls should learn as much as they want."
    • Jahnavi's Future Vow: Jahnavi promised her mother: "Mother, when I grow up, I'll be a teacher and I'll go from house to house in our village and ask all the little girls to come to my school. And I'll teach them all that I'm going to learn."
    • The next morning before school, she proudly told the River, "I did it! I was scared, but I did it!" She was excited to learn to write her name, do sums, and find out why fishes in the rice fields turn into frogs.
🧪 In-Box & "Let us explore" Activities Decoded (Pages 14–15)
  • 1. Sacred Rivers in India (Page 14): In India, rivers are revered as sacred life-givers (providing water for drinking, farming, and civilization) and are mostly referred to as female / mothers (e.g., Ganga, Yamuna, Saraswati, Narmada, Godavari, Mahanadi, Kaveri). However, a few rivers are referred to as male, such as the Brahmaputra (literally 'Son of Brahma') and the Sone.
  • 2. Poster Analysis – "The Happiness of a Nation lies in the Dignity of its Daughters" (Page 15):
    • Observation: Four young schoolgirls are smiling brightly while reading and writing together in a classroom.
    • Why they look happy: They are enjoying the freedom, confidence, and joy of receiving an education alongside their friends.
    • Relation between 'Dignity' (self-respect) and 'Happiness': True happiness comes when an individual is treated with equal respect, valued for their potential, and given the freedom to learn and grow with self-respect.
    • How a Nation prospers: Educated, empowered daughters become teachers, scientists, doctors, leaders, and informed citizens who uplift families, eradicate poverty, and drive national progress.
  • 3. Government Schemes for Girl Child Education (Page 15):
    • Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (Save the Daughter, Educate the Daughter)
    • Balika Samridhi Yojana
    • Samagra Shiksha Scheme – Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalayas (KGBVs) (residential schools for girls from disadvantaged groups)
    • Other Major Schemes: Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana, CBSE Udaan Scheme, and Pragati Scholarship for Girls.
PART B (Poem): Try Again – by Eliza Cook

1. Complete Stanza-by-Stanza Explanation (Pages 17–18)

  • Stanzas 1 to 3 (King Bruce's Despair):
    • King Bruce of Scotland (a monarch who wore a crown) flung himself down in a lonely, sad mood with a sinking heart.
    • He had been trying repeatedly to perform a great deed to make his people happy (glad), but despite trying again and again, he could not succeed.
    • Overwhelmed by "low despair" (deep hopelessness) and grief, he pondered (thought deeply) and decided to quit: "I'll give it all up," said he.
  • Stanzas 4 & 5 (The Spider Appears):
    • At that very moment, a tiny spider dropped down on its "silken, filmy clue" (a very delicate, soft silk thread).
    • King Bruce stopped his sad thoughts to watch what the spider would do. Its cobweb home was high up on the ceiling dome, and the king could not divine (guess/predict) how the tiny creature would ever climb back up such a fine, long thread.
  • Stanzas 6 to 9 (Nine Brave Attempts & Falls):
    • The spider immediately began to cling and crawl straight up with strong endeavour (hard effort), but slipped and sprawled right back down near the ground.
    • Without waiting a second or uttering a single complaint, it ran "Up, up" again, only to fall even lower and lie there dizzy and faint.
    • Once its head grew steady, it climbed a half-yard higher along the delicate thread where its feet would tire, falling and mounting repeatedly until nine brave attempts were counted!
  • Stanzas 10 to 12 (The Anxious 10th Climb & Victory):
    • After 9 failures, King Bruce thought the "foolish thing" would surely stop striving since it tumbled every time.
    • Yet the spider went up once more! In an anxious minute, when it was just a foot away from its cobweb door, it climbed "Steadily, steadily, inch by inch, / Higher and higher" and made a bold little run at the very last pinch to reach its "native cot" (its home/web).
  • Stanzas 13 & 14 (King Bruce's Inspiration & Success):
    • King Bruce cried out, "Bravo, bravo! All honour to those who try; / The spider up there, defied despair; / He conquered, and why shouldn't I?"
    • King Bruce braced his mind (prepared himself with determination), tried once more just as he had tried before, and that time he did not fail!
Visual Arc of the Poem "Try Again": From Low Despair to Perseverance and Victory.
💡 Poetic Devices in "Try Again" (Pages 19–20)
  • Rhyme Scheme: ABAB throughout the poem (e.g., think/crown/sink/down or deed/glad/succeed/sad).
  • Metaphor: The spider's struggle to climb back to its web is an extended metaphor for King Bruce's (and humanity's) struggle to overcome failure through perseverance. Also, "native cot" and "cobweb door" are metaphors for the spider's web.
  • Alliteration (Repetition of initial consonant sounds):
    • "slippery sprawl" (/s/ sound)
    • "cling and crawl" (/k/ sound)
    • "defied despair" (/d/ sound)
    • "tells the tale" (/t/ sound)
    • "filmy clue" / "braced his mind"
  • Repetition: Phrases like "tried and tried", "up, up", "steadily, steadily", "higher and higher", and "Bravo, bravo!" are repeated to emphasise continuous, tireless effort and rising determination.
  • Archaic Contractions for Rhythm: 'Tis (It is) and 'Twas (It was) are used to maintain the musical rhythm (meter) of the lines.
  • Interjection: "Bravo!" and "Ah me!" express sudden strong emotion (admiration and anxiety).
🧪 Science Connection ("Let us explore" - Page 26): Spiders vs. Insects (Ants)
  • 🧪 Fact: Spiders are NOT insects! They belong to a class of animals called arachnids.
  • 👀 Key Differences Between a Spider (Arachnid) and an Ant (Insect):
    1. Number of Legs: A spider has 8 legs (4 pairs), whereas an ant (insect) has 6 legs (3 pairs).
    2. Body Parts: A spider's body is divided into 2 main parts (cephalothorax and abdomen), whereas an ant's body has 3 parts (head, thorax, and abdomen).
    3. Antennae & Wings: Ants have two antennae (feelers) on their heads, whereas spiders have no antennae and spin silk webs using spinnerets.
PART C (Prose 2): Three Days to See – by Helen Keller

1. How Helen Keller Experiences the World Through Touch (Page 29)

  • Although Helen Keller is blind (and deaf), she finds hundreds of things to interest her through mere touch (tactile sense):
    1. She feels the delicate symmetry (evenness) of a leaf.
    2. She passes her hands lovingly over the smooth skin of a silver birch tree or the rough, shaggy bark of a pine tree.
    3. In spring, she touches tree branches hopefully in search of a bud — "the first sign of awakening Nature after her winter's sleep."
    4. Occasionally, she places her hand gently on a small tree and feels the happy quiver (shiver/vibration) of a bird in full song.
    5. She recognises her friends from the feel of their faces with her fingertips, detecting laughter, sorrow, and other obvious emotions.

2. 🔥 Helen Keller's Imaginary 3-Day Plan of Sight (Pages 29–31)

Day Primary Theme / Focus Specific Things Helen Keller Wants to See
Day 1 People, Kindness & Companionship
  • She wants to see the people whose kindness and companionship have made her life worth living.
  • She wants to look into the heart of a friend through that 'window of the soul', the eye (instead of just feeling the outline of their faces with her fingertips).
Day 2 Dawn & The Pageant of Earth's Past and Present (Museums)
  • Arise with the dawn to see the thrilling miracle by which night is transformed into day and behold with awe the magnificent panorama of light with which the sun awakens the sleeping earth.
  • Devote the day to a hasty glimpse of the world, past and present, by visiting museums to see the condensed history of the earth: animals and races of men in their native environment, gigantic carcasses of dinosaurs and mastodons, and how man with his tiny stature and powerful brain conquered the animal kingdom.
Day 3 The Workaday World & Daily Human Life in the City
  • Greet the dawn again to discover new revelations of beauty, then spend the third day in the workaday world amid the haunts of men going about the business of life in the city.
  • Stand at a busy street corner merely looking at people to understand their daily lives:
    • She sees smiles → and feels happy.
    • She sees serious determination → and feels proud.
    • She sees suffering → and feels compassionate.
  • At midnight, permanent night closes in on her again, leaving many things unseen.

3. 🔥 Helen Keller's Golden Advice to Those Who Can See & Hear (Page 31)

"Use your eyes as if tomorrow you would be stricken blind. And the same method can be applied to your other senses. Hear the music of voice, the song of a bird, the mighty strains of an orchestra, as if you would be stricken deaf tomorrow. Touch each object as if tomorrow your tactile sense would fail. Smell the perfume of flowers, taste with relish each morsel, as if tomorrow you could never smell and taste again. Make the most of every sense... But of all the senses, I am sure that sight must be the most delightful."
🧪 In-Box & "Let us explore" Activities Decoded (Pages 37–42): Braille, ISL & Digital India
  • 1. Louis Braille & The Braille Code (Pages 37–38):
    • Persons with visual challenges read and write through touch using Braille, a tactile code of raised dots (arranged in a 6-dot cell: 2 columns of 3 dots) representing letters of the alphabet and numbers.
    • It was developed over a period of nine years by Louis Braille (France), who became blind at a very young age due to an accident.
  • 2. International Day of Persons with Disabilities (Page 38):
    • Observed globally and across India every year on December 3 (proclaimed by the United Nations in 1992).
    • In India, schools, NGOs, and the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment organise awareness drives, assistive device camps, inclusive sports, and cultural events to promote dignity, equal rights, and accessibility (Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan) for Divyangjan.
  • 3. Digital India & Assistive Technology (Listening Transcript - Pages 41–42):
    • The Digital India Initiative promotes inclusion for the visually impaired through assistive technologies like screen readers (software applications that read out text displayed on a computer/smartphone screen with audio feedback) and voice-over-enabled apps, opening independent careers in IT, banking, and beyond.
    • As Anuj's mother concludes: "Inclusion is not just about having the right tools, but also about having the right mindset."

2. Grammar Rulebook (Covered in Unit 1)

📐 3 Core Grammar Rules in Unit 1
  1. Preposition vs. Adverb (Page 11):
    • Preposition: Shows relationship (direction, location, time) between a noun/pronoun and another part of the sentence. Rule: A preposition is always followed by an object (noun/pronoun).
      Example: "She ran up the stairs." (Here 'up' is followed by the noun 'the stairs' → Preposition).
    • Adverb: Tells us more about the action (verb) — how, where, or when it happened — and has no noun object following it.
      Example: "A kingfisher swept down." / "The children played outside." (No noun object after 'down' or 'outside' → Adverb).
  2. Simile vs. Metaphor (Page 20):
    • Simile (S): Directly compares two unlike things using the words 'like' or 'as...as' (e.g., "He ran as fast as a cheetah"; "The baby slept like a log").
    • Metaphor (M): Makes an implied comparison by stating that one thing IS another, without using 'like' or 'as' (e.g., "The world is a stage"; "The classroom was a zoo").
  3. Modal Verbs (Pages 34–35):
    • Definition: Auxiliary (helping) verbs placed before the main verb to express ability, possibility, permission, necessity/obligation, advice, or polite request.
    • Key Functions:
      • can = ability / informal suggestion | may = formal permission / possibility
      • might = weak possibility (e.g., It might rain) | could / would = polite request
      • must = strong necessity / obligation | should = advice / suggestion
      • ought to = moral obligation / duty (e.g., You ought to apologise).

Page 3 Discussion Questions:

  • Q1. What was Jahnavi's dream? Was it important to her? Why?
    Ans: Jahnavi's dream was to go to school and learn to read and write like her elder brother Ettan (Gopi) and Meena.     Ans: Yes, the River helps her not by carrying her physically, but by acting as a wise, encouraging friend. It removes her self-doubt, reminds her of her natural bravery, and gives her the practical idea to slip quietly into the classroom and listen to the teacher.

Let us think and reflect – II. Short & Long Answer Questions (Page 8)
View CBSE Topper-Style Answers (Questions 1 to 7)
  1. Why does the writer describe different aspects of nature in great detail at the beginning of the story? Why do you think the writer does this?
    Ans: The writer vividly describes the kingfisher's blue wings, the green lizard basking in the sun, the bamboo thicket, and the murmuring river to create a peaceful, lifelike rural setting. Moreover, it highlights how deeply connected and observant Jahnavi is towards nature, preparing the reader for the magical moment when nature (the River) speaks back to her.
  2. How did the River know so much about Jahnavi?
    Ans: The River knew everything about Jahnavi—including her fearlessness around lizards, snakes, and noisy trains—because Jahnavi visited the riverbank almost every day to sit by its edge, and the River had been silently watching and listening to her grow up.
  3. Jahnavi says, "And I'm so old now, they'll never let me go." What can you infer about Jahnavi and the school from this line?
    Ans: We can infer that Jahnavi (who is nearly ten) feels she has missed the normal age of starting school (around age 5 or 6, like her younger brother Ramu). She innocently worries that she is now too old to sit with beginners and that her parents will permanently keep her at home to babysit her youngest brother, Appu.
  4. Why did the River laugh when Jahnavi said she would be scared at school?
    Ans: The River laughed at the irony that a brave girl who was not afraid of creeping green lizards, venomous snakes hiding in the bamboo clump, or loud, rattling trains passing over the bridge was afraid of sitting in a harmless classroom.
  5. Why should Jahnavi follow the River's advice?
    Ans: Jahnavi should follow the River's advice because her parents had been postponing her schooling year after year without realising how badly she wanted to learn. Taking the initiative to visit the school herself was the only way to show the teacher and her parents her genuine eagerness to study.
  6. How does the conversation between Jahnavi and the River make the story more appealing?
    Ans: Personifying the River as a sleepy, wise, and humorous friend turns an ordinary story into an enchanting fable. Instead of a lonely girl crying to herself, the lively dialogue brings warmth, gentle humour, and encouragement, making the message of girl-child education engaging for young readers.
  7. What is the main message that the writer intends to convey?
    Ans: The writer conveys the powerful message of gender equality and the right to education for every girl child. Girls are just as capable and curious as boys, and with a little self-belief and family/teacher support, an educated girl can uplift her entire village.

I. Capital letter for 'River' (Page 8): The author uses a capital 'R' for the 'River' because of personification—the river has been given human qualities (speaking, laughing, advising, and acting as a named character and friend to Jahnavi), so it is treated as a Proper Noun.

⚡ "Exam Hall Mistakes" & 5-Minute Rapid Revision Cheat Sheet

⚠️ 5 Common "Exam Hall Mistakes" & How to Avoid Them
  1. Mistake 1: Confusing the names and ages of Jahnavi's brothers.
    Fix: Jahnavi (nearly 10 years old) has three brothers: (1) Ettan (elder brother, real name Gopi), (2) Ramu (born when Jahnavi was 5; now goes to school), and (3) Little Appu (the smallest, whom Jahnavi carries on her shoulder).
  2. Mistake 2: Writing that the spider succeeded on its 9th attempt.
    Fix: The spider made 9 failed attempts ("Nine brave attempts were counted") and succeeded on its 10th attempt!
  3. Mistake 3: Mixing up Helen Keller's Day 2 and Day 3 plans.
    Fix: Remember F-M-C: Day 1 = Friends & loved ones; Day 2 = Museums (past & present history, dinosaurs, mastodons); Day 3 = City (workaday world & busy street corner).
  4. Mistake 4: Confusing Prepositions and Adverbs for words like up, down, inside, outside, around.
    Fix: Check if there is a noun after the word! If a noun follows it (e.g., inside the room), it is a Preposition. If no noun follows it (e.g., he stepped inside), it is an Adverb.
  5. Mistake 5: Forgetting to change 'like/as' when transforming a Simile into a Metaphor.
    Fix: When converting a Simile to a Metaphor, completely remove 'like' or 'as...as' (e.g., "The night was like a velvet cloak" → "The night was a velvet cloak").
