TOPIC 2.1: What Is a Civilisation? & The Road from Village to City

In earlier chapters, we learned how human beings began as hunter-gatherers, learned to tame wild fire, invented stone tools, and eventually started settling down in small farming villages during the Neolithic period (New Stone Age). But living in a small village is very different from living in an advanced civilisation!

1. What Exactly Is a "Civilisation"?

In everyday language, we use the word civilisation to describe an advanced stage of human society. In our NCERT textbook (page 86), historians define a civilisation as a society that possesses at least the following five core characteristics:

  1. 1. Central Government & Civic Administration:
    In a village of 50 people, elders can solve disputes easily. But in a city of 30,000 citizens, you need an organized system of rules, public administrators, tax collection, city managers, and supervisors to keep streets clean, maintain grain reserves, and build public walls.
  2. 2. Urbanism (Growth of Planned Cities):
    A civilisation is marked by true cities, complete with organized town planning, wide streets, public brick buildings, fresh water management, and safe underground drainage systems.
  3. 3. Specialised Crafts & Technology:
    Not everyone has to farm! Because farmers produce a food surplus, other citizens can become full-time specialists — metalworkers (working with copper and bronze), potters, bead-makers, weavers, stone carvers, and architects.
  4. 4. Long-Distance Trade Networks:
    Cities cannot survive on local stones or timber alone. A civilisation creates bustling trade networks over land and sea to exchange raw materials (like lapis lazuli, copper, gold, and tin) and finished luxury goods over hundreds and thousands of kilometres.
  5. 5. A Writing System (Script):
    To manage trade records, seal storage jars, write contracts, and communicate messages across distant regions, a civilisation develops a written script.

2. The Global Timeline: When Did Civilisations Begin?

  • Civilisation did not begin everywhere at the same time:
    • Mesopotamia (Modern Iraq and Syria): Began around 5,000 years ago (~3000 BCE) along the Tigris and Euphrates rivers.
    • Ancient Egypt: Flourished along the Nile River a few centuries later (around ~2800 BCE).
    • The Indian Subcontinent: Our earliest urban civilisation — the Indus-Sarasvati Civilisation — stepped into its mature city-building phase around 2600 BCE (4,600 years ago)!

3. The Road from Village to City: From Mehrgarh to Harappa

  • Mehrgarh (~7000 BCE): Located near the Bolan Pass in Balochistan (Pakistan), Mehrgarh is the earliest known agricultural village in South Asia. Here, our ancestors first cultivated wheat and barley, domesticated humped cattle, sheep, and goats, built rectangular mud houses, and used dental flint drills!
  • Early Agricultural Towns (~3500 BCE to 2600 BCE): Over the next few millennia, farming spread east into the fertile river plains of the Punjab, Sindh, Rajasthan, and Haryana. Small hamlets grew into fortified towns with pottery kilns and copper metallurgy.
  • The Mature Urban Phase (~2600 BCE to 1900 BCE): Around 2600 BCE, with expanding river trade and farming wealth, these towns blossomed into magnificent, planned brick metropolises like Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Rakhigarhi, Kalibangan, and Dholavira. This mature urban phase lasted for over 700 years!

4. Why Does This Civilisation Have Three Different Names?

Students often get confused when teachers use different names for the same civilisation. Here is why archaeologists use three names:

  1. The "Indus Valley Civilisation": When British and Indian archaeologists first began digging in the 1920s, the first major sites (Harappa and Mohenjo-daro) were discovered near the Indus River (Sindhu). So, historians originally named it the 'Indus Valley Civilisation'.
  2. The "Harappan Civilisation": In archaeology, there is a universal rule: whenever a new ancient culture is discovered, it is named after the very first site where its pottery and tools were found. Because Harappa was the first city excavated in 1920–1921 by Daya Ram Sahni, the entire culture is officially named the 'Harappan Civilisation'.
  3. The "Indus-Sarasvati" or "Sindhu-Sarasvati Civilisation": Over the past 70 years, archaeologists have discovered over 1,500 Harappan sites. Surprisingly, the vast majority (over 1,000 sites!) were not along the Indus River at all, but along the dried-up valley of the sacred Sarasvati River (in Haryana, Punjab, Rajasthan, and Cholistan in Pakistan). Therefore, modern scholars prefer the more accurate term: Indus-Sarasvati Civilisation.

5. The Sacred Sarasvati River (The Ghaggar-Hakra Basin)

  • Where did it flow? A few millennia ago, the mighty Sarasvati River flowed from the foothills of the Shivalik Himalayas, across Haryana, Punjab, northern Rajasthan, and through Sindh and Gujarat into the Rann of Kutch and the Arabian Sea.
  • What is it called today? Today, the old riverbed is known as the Ghaggar River in India and the Hakra River in Pakistan (together called the Ghaggar-Hakra River). It is now a seasonal stream that flows only during heavy monsoon rains because its ancient snowmelt sources were diverted by geological earthquakes.
  • Mention in the Rig Veda: The Sarasvati is mentioned extensively in the Rig Veda, where it is praised as the greatest, purest, and most nourishing of all rivers (nadītame — 'best of mothers, best of rivers').
  • Remarkable Density of Cities: The Sarasvati basin cradled massive metropolises like Rakhigarhi (Haryana) and Ganweriwala (Cholistan), prominent cities like Kalibangan (Rajasthan) and Farmana (Haryana), and thriving craft towns like Bhirrana and Banawali.

TOPIC 2.2: Town Planning, Architecture & The Drainage Miracle

The greatest wonder of the Harappan Civilisation was its town planning. More than 4,500 years ago, Harappan engineers built cities with a level of sanitation, cleanliness, and geometric precision that Europe did not achieve until the 18th century CE!

1. The Twin Layout: Western Citadel vs. Eastern Lower Town

Most Harappan cities were divided into two distinct, fortified sections:

  • The Western Citadel (Upper Town):
    • Built on a towering, artificial mud-brick platform to raise it high above river flood levels.
    • Heavily fortified with thick perimeter walls and watchtowers.
    • Contained the city's grand public, religious, and administrative buildings: the Great Bath, the Great Granary, and spacious Assembly Halls.
    • Used for public gatherings, religious rituals, and civic governance.
  • The Eastern Lower Town (Residential City):
    • Located at a lower ground level to the east, covering a much larger area.
    • Divided into square or rectangular housing blocks by straight, wide streets.
    • This was the residential and commercial quarter where ordinary citizens — merchants, craftspeople, potters, weavers, and labourers — lived and worked.
  • The Unique Exception — Dholavira (Three Towns!):
    While most cities had two divisions, Dholavira in Gujarat had three distinct zones: the Castle (Upper Citadel), the Middle Town, and the Lower Town! Furthermore, while most cities were built of baked clay bricks, Dholavira's massive walls, gateways, and building foundations were constructed with beautiful cut stone masonry!

2. The Grid Street System

  • Harappan streets were laid out like a giant chessboard — running strictly from North to South and East to West, cutting each other at perfect 90° right angles (the grid system).
  • Main streets were surprisingly wide (up to 10 metres wide, like a modern four-lane road!), allowing two bullock carts to pass each other comfortably without traffic jams.
  • Side alleys branched off perpendicular to the main avenues.
  • Ingenious House Entrances: The doors of private houses did not open directly onto the noisy, dusty main avenue. Instead, they opened into quiet side lanes. This kept the homes peaceful, dust-free, and safe for young children!

3. Standardised Baked Bricks (The 1:2:4 Ratio)

  • One of the most astonishing facts discovered by archaeologists is that across an area of over 1 million square kilometres — from northern Afghanistan to Gujarat — Harappans used bricks made to the exact same proportional ratio:
    Brick Ratio = 1 : 2 : 4 (Thickness : Width : Length)
    For example: 7 cm thick, 14 cm wide, and 28 cm long.
  • Bricks were baked in high-temperature kilns, making them waterproof, rock-hard, and immune to decay. When British railway engineers laid railway tracks in the 1850s, they dug up thousands of Harappan bricks and found them as strong as brand new bricks after 4,000 years!

4. The Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro (Fig. 6.6)

  • The Great Bath on the Citadel of Mohenjo-daro is an architectural masterpiece of ancient hydraulic engineering:
    • It is a large rectangular swimming pool/tank measuring roughly 12 metres north-south, 7 metres east-west, and 2.4 metres deep.
    • Two wide flights of steps lead down into the water — one from the north and one from the south.
    • Watertight Waterproofing: The tank was built with precision-fitted bricks laid on edge with gypsum mortar, and sealed behind a thick layer of natural bitumen (mineral tar) so that not a single drop of water leaked out!
    • Surrounded by pillared corridors and small dressing rooms on three sides. One room contained a large brick well to fill the bath with fresh water.
    • In one corner of the floor, an enormous corbelled brick drain was installed to empty the dirty water periodically and refill it with clean water!
  • What was its purpose? Archaeologists agree that it was not an ordinary public swimming pool (because every house had its own private bath). Instead, it was a sacred tank for ritual purification, where kings, priests, or citizens took a holy bath before religious ceremonies — very much like temple tanks (Kalyanis or Pushkarinis) used across India today!

5. The Drainage System: Ancient India's Sanitation Miracle

  • Every private house had a designated, paved brick bathroom with gently sloping floors.
  • Wastewater flowed through terracotta wall pipes into small private cesspits, which trapped solid waste so it wouldn't choke the sewer.
  • The water then emptied into covered brick drains running along every street.
  • These street drains were covered with flat stone slabs or baked bricks that could be lifted easily by municipal workers to inspect and clean them.
  • The covered sewer network flowed gently outside the city walls, ensuring that living quarters remained clean, odor-free, and healthy. No other ancient civilization on Earth — not Egypt, not Mesopotamia — paid such fanatical attention to public hygiene!

6. Dholavira's Giant Water Reservoirs (Pages 95–96)

  • Dholavira is located on an island in the arid Rann of Kutch (Gujarat), where fresh river water was scarce and rainfall was unpredictable.
  • To conquer drought, Harappan hydraulic engineers created an astonishing rainwater harvesting network:
    • They built at least 16 giant reservoirs, carved directly into the bedrock or built with cut stone masonry.
    • The largest reservoir discovered measures an incredible 73 metres long, over 10 metres wide, and up to 7 metres deep!
    • They built stone dams across two seasonal streams (the Manhar and Mansar) and directed floodwater through covered underground canals into this interconnected chain of reservoirs.
    • These reservoirs held enough fresh drinking water to support thousands of people, gardens, and livestock for years, even if rains failed!

TOPIC 2.3: Daily Life, Food, Occupations & Crafts

What did ordinary people do from sunrise to sunset in a Harappan city? What did they eat, what games did their children play, and what amazing crafts did they master?

1. Rural-Urban Interdependence: Feeding the Cities

  • Harappan cities did not grow food inside their city walls. They depended completely on hundreds of surrounding rural farming villages that supplied fresh grain, vegetables, dairy, and meat on a daily basis.
  • In return, cities supplied villagers with fine bronze tools, carnelian ornaments, painted pottery, and trade goods.

2. Agriculture & What Harappans Ate

  • Farming Techniques: At Kalibangan (Rajasthan), archaeologists unearthed the world's earliest preserved ploughed field, featuring two sets of criss-cross furrows at right angles. This allowed farmers to grow two different crops (such as mustard and gram) simultaneously in the same field! At Banawali (Haryana), they discovered a realistic terracotta toy model of a plough.
  • Crops Grown: Wheat, barley (excellent varieties found at Banawali), several types of millets (ragi, jowar, bajra in Gujarat), sesame, mustard, lentils, chickpeas, peas, and dates.
  • Pioneers of Cotton: Harappans were the first people in the world to grow and spin cotton (called Sindon by the Greeks, derived from 'Sindhu')!
  • Domesticated Animals: Humped cattle (the Zebu bull), water buffalo, sheep, goats, pigs, and dogs. They also fished extensively in rivers and the Arabian Sea with bronze fishhooks.
  • Surprising Secrets from Ancient Cooking Pots:
    Modern scientists extracted organic residue from Harappan clay cooking pots and made astonishing discoveries:
    • Pots contained traces of dairy products (milk, butter, curd, ghee).
    • Pots contained traces of spices like turmeric and ginger!
    • They even found traces of banana cooked with grains!
    • This proves that more than 4,500 years ago, Harappans were already preparing rich, aromatic, spiced curries that resemble our beloved Indian cooking today!

3. Metallurgy & The Bronze Age

📘 DON'T MISS OUT: The Secret of Bronze (Textbook Page 98)

The Harappans were skilled metallurgists. Copper is a soft metal that bends easily. By smelting copper and mixing it with a small proportion of tin (approx. 10%), they created bronze — an alloy that is significantly harder, sharper, and more durable than pure copper. They cast bronze into knives, axes, chisels, mirrors, cooking pans, fishhooks, and breathtaking statues like the 'Dancing Girl'.

4. The Famed Craft of Bead-Making

  • Harappan craftspeople were world-famous for making jewellery, especially from carnelian (a beautiful translucent red gemstone found in Gujarat), agate, jasper, lapis lazuli (deep blue stone from Afghanistan), turquoise, steatite, gold, silver, and sea shells.
  • At Lothal and Chanhudaro, archaeologists discovered complete bead-making factories with specialized stone kilns to bake carnelian to its rich red colour, and micro-drills made of hardened stone (ernestite) to bore tiny holes through long beads without cracking them!

5. Standard Weights and Measures

  • Harappan merchants were strictly honest and accurate. They used polished cubical stone weights made of chert.
  • The Binary System: The smaller weights followed a binary system: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32... up to 64, with the basic unit weighing about 13.7 grams. Larger weights followed a decimal system (100, 200, 500).
  • At Lothal, archaeologists found an ivory measuring ruler with tiny, precise graduations — the smallest division measuring just 1.7 millimetres!
TOPIC 2.4: Trade, Maritime Networks & The Undeciphered Script

1. The World's Earliest Tidal Dockyard at Lothal (Fig. 6.12)

  • Located near the Gulf of Khambhat in Gujarat, Lothal was India's premier international port city.
  • Archaeologists excavated an enormous rectangular basin made of baked bricks, measuring approximately 216 metres long and 37 metres wide.
  • Engineering Wonder: It was built along a tidal tributary connected to the Sabarmati and Bhogavo rivers. A sophisticated sluice gate controlled the water level during high and low tides, allowing seafaring cargo ships to float safely into the basin, load and unload goods, and sail back out to the Arabian Sea!

2. International Trade Routes: Meluhha, Magan & Dilmun

  • Harappan merchant ships braved the open waters of the Arabian Sea and the Persian Gulf:
    • Oman (Ancient Magan): Harappans traded with Oman to obtain copper in exchange for Indian cotton textiles, ivory, and grain. An ancient Harappan ivory comb (Fig. 6.11) was found on the coast of Oman!
    • Bahrain (Ancient Dilmun): Acted as a mid-way trading post in the Persian Gulf.
    • Mesopotamia (Modern Iraq / Sumer): Mesopotamian clay cuneiform tablets record brisk trade with a wealthy eastern maritime land called Meluhha — the Mesopotamian name for the Harappan Civilisation!
    • Mesopotamian kings imported carnelian beads, lapis lazuli, pearls, timber, gold, ivory, and exotic birds from Meluhha. Harappan carnelian beads and seals have been unearthed at famous Mesopotamian cities like Ur, Kish, and Susa (in Iran, Fig. 6.10).

3. Harappan Seals and the Mystery Script

  • More than 3,500 seals have been unearthed from Harappan sites. Most are small square or rectangular tablets made of steatite (soapstone), carved with artistic mastery and hardened in fire.
  • Animal Motifs: The seals depict magnificent animals: the one-horned mythical creature (often called the 'unicorn', appearing on over 60% of seals), the humped Zebu bull, the horned tiger, the elephant, the rhinoceros, and the water buffalo.
  • The Script: Above the animal figure runs a line of pictographic signs. The Harappan script consists of roughly 400 distinct signs and was written from right to left.
  • Why is it still undeciphered? Because no bilingual inscription (like the Rosetta Stone of Egypt, which had Greek alongside hieroglyphs) has ever been found for Harappan writing! Despite hundreds of attempts by scholars worldwide, the Indus script remains a teasing mystery waiting for a brilliant young student like you to decode it!
  • How Were Seals Used? Seals were used like modern identity stamps and security locks on trade packages. A merchant wrapped goods in cloth, tied them with rope, put a patch of wet clay on the knot, and pressed his seal into it. If the clay seal arrived unbroken, the buyer knew the goods had not been opened or stolen on the journey!
TOPIC 2.5: Art, Social Life, Spiritual Beliefs & The Peaceful Society

Harappan culture was filled with joy, creativity, music, games, and spiritual wonder. Let us explore the artistic treasures shown in your textbook (pages 100–102):

1. Toys, Games & Daily Fun (Fig. 6.14)

  • Stone Gameboards: At Dholavira, archaeologists found a polished stone gameboard (25 cm long) with carved square grids — ancient India's ancestors of chess and chaupar!
  • Terracotta Toys: Children played with miniature toy bullock carts with rolling wheels, climbing monkeys on strings, bird-shaped terracotta whistles (like the 4 cm whistle from Karanpura, Rajasthan), and terracotta animals with moving heads!
  • Bronze Mirrors: Elegant circular mirrors made of polished reflective bronze plates with wooden or bone handles.

2. Cultural and Spiritual Masterpieces (Fig. 6.15)

  • The 'Priest King' (Mohenjo-daro): A dignified soapstone statuette of a bearded man with neatly combed hair, a headband, and an embroidered shawl with trefoil (three-leaf clover) floral patterns draped over his left shoulder, leaving his right arm bare — exactly the way sacred shawls are worn in Indian ceremonies today!
  • The Bronze 'Dancing Girl' (Mohenjo-daro): A tiny 10.8 cm tall bronze statuette made using the sophisticated lost-wax casting technique. She stands with confident pride, right hand resting casually on her hip, slightly bent knee, and her left arm covered from wrist to shoulder in 25 bangles! (This traditional style of wearing bangles all the way up the arm is still proudly worn by Rabari and Banjara women in Rajasthan and Gujarat today!).
  • The Pashupati Seal: Depicts a three-faced figure seated cross-legged on a low throne in a yogic posture (mulabandhasana), wearing a horned headdress. He is surrounded by wild animals: an elephant, a tiger, a rhinoceros, and a water buffalo, with two deer resting below his seat. Historians view this as an early representation of Lord Shiva as Pashupati ('Lord of Animals') and the historic origin of Yoga in India!
  • The 'Namaste' Figurine: A small terracotta human figurine sitting with palms pressed together in the respectful Indian greeting of Namaste, showing that our most universal gesture of hospitality is at least 4,500 years old!
  • The 'Thirsty Crow' Pot from Lothal: A painted ceramic jar from Lothal depicts a tree with birds and a deer, and a crow perched near a pitcher of water — an ancient illustration of the famous fable of the thirsty crow dropping pebbles into a jar, recorded thousands of years before Aesop's fables!

3. Prof. B.B. Lal's Insight: A Peaceful, Well-Balanced Society

📘 The Nature of Harappan Society: Prof. B.B. Lal's Perspective

In the opening quote of our chapter, the eminent archaeologist Prof. B.B. Lal pointed out a profound truth: Harappan society was not built on war, royal conquest, or cruelty. Archaeologists have not found giant golden palaces for greedy kings, nor mass graves of enslaved warriors, nor weapons designed for battle (no swords, heavy armor, or siege weapons). Instead, cities were built for everyone — with clean drains, fresh water, and comfortable homes for all citizens. It was a society based on civic cooperation, peaceful trade, and mutual accommodation.

TOPIC 2.6: The Mystery of the Decline & The Living Legacy

Around 1900 BCE, after flourishing for over seven centuries, the great cities of the Indus-Sarasvati Civilisation began to empty. People stopped maintaining the brick drains, foreign trade with Mesopotamia halted, writing faded away, and families packed their belongings. What happened? Did an army attack them?

1. Debunking the Invasion Myth

  • In the early 20th century, some European colonial historians guessed that foreign invaders (such as the Aryans) swept down from Central Asia and violently destroyed the cities.
  • Modern archaeological science has completely disproven this theory! Excavations across dozens of sites show no trace of warfare, no battlefield slaughter, no burned-out forts, and no weapons of conquest. The Harappans were not conquered; their environment changed!

2. The Two Real Factors: Climate Change and the Drying of the Sarasvati

  1. 1. Global Climate Change & Weaker Monsoons:
    Scientific climate data (from tree rings, cave stalagmites, and lake sediments) shows that around 2000 BCE, a prolonged dry climate phase struck the ancient world (affecting Egypt, Mesopotamia, and India). Monsoon rainfall weakened significantly, causing frequent droughts. When crops failed year after year, the huge cities could no longer feed their dense populations.
  2. 2. The Drying Up of the Central Sarasvati Basin:
    Tectonic plate movements and earthquakes in the Himalayas tilted the landscape. As a result, major snow-fed tributaries that previously flowed into the Sarasvati (the Sutlej and Yamuna) shifted their courses — the Sutlej joined the Indus, and the Yamuna joined the Ganga! Deprived of glacial meltwater, the mighty Sarasvati River dried up in its central basin. Cities like Kalibangan, Banawali, and Ganweriwala suddenly had no drinking water, forcing their citizens to abandon their homes.

3. The End or a New Beginning? (Migration & Living Legacy)

The Harappan Civilisation did not perish — it transformed and moved!

  • Eastward and Southward Migration: Families migrated eastward into the lush, rain-rich Ganga-Yamuna river plains (modern Uttar Pradesh and Bihar), and southward into the fertile valleys of Gujarat and Maharashtra.
  • The Living Legacy That Survives Today: The Harappans passed down their timeless traditions to subsequent generations:
    • The 'Namaste' Greeting: The universal gesture of joined palms.
    • Yoga Postures: The seated yogic pose seen on the Pashupati seal.
    • Bangles and Ornaments: Women wearing bangles up the arm in Rajasthan and Gujarat.

1. What is a civilisation?

A civilisation is an advanced stage of human society characterized by five fundamental pillars: (a) a central government and administration, (b) planned cities with water management and drainage (urbanism), (c) specialised crafts and metallurgy, (d) long-distance trade networks, and (e) a writing system (script).

2. What was the earliest civilisation of the Indian Subcontinent?

The earliest civilisation was the Indus-Sarasvati (or Harappan) Civilisation, which flourished between 2600 BCE and 1900 BCE across the fertile valleys of the Indus and Sarasvati river systems in modern India and Pakistan.

  • International Maritime Trade: The world's earliest engineered tidal dockyard at Lothal, trading with Oman and Mesopotamia.
  • Fine Craftsmanship: Famed carnelian beads, steatite seals, and bronze sculptures like the 'Dancing Girl'.

Q1. Why does the civilisation studied in this chapter have several names? Discuss their significance.
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

This civilisation is known by three different names, each reflecting a specific stage of archaeological discovery and geographical understanding:

  • 1. Indus Valley Civilisation:
    Reason & Significance: In the early 1920s, the first major excavated cities (Harappa and Mohenjo-daro) were discovered along the Indus River (Sindhu) and its tributaries. Early British and Indian archaeologists assumed the culture was confined to the Indus valley, hence the name.
  • 2. Harappan Civilisation:
    Reason & Significance: In archaeology, the standard international scientific convention is to name an entire ancient culture after the type-site — the very first archaeological site where its distinctive pottery, tools, and architecture were identified. Because Harappa was the first site excavated in 1920–1921, archaeologists officially call it the 'Harappan Civilisation'.
  • 3. Indus-Sarasvati (or Sindhu-Sarasvati) Civilisation:
    Reason & Significance: Post-independence excavations revealed that more than 1,000 settlements (the vast majority of all known sites!) were situated along the dried-up basin of the sacred Sarasvati River (modern Ghaggar-Hakra) in Haryana, Rajasthan, Punjab, and Cholistan. Therefore, scholars today use 'Indus-Sarasvati Civilisation' as the most geographically accurate name.
Q2. Write a brief report (100 to 150 words) summing up some of the achievements of the Indus-Sarasvati civilisation.
View Complete Model Answer

Model Report: Achievements of the Indus-Sarasvati Civilisation

The Indus-Sarasvati Civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) was one of the most advanced urban societies of the ancient world. Its greatest achievement was scientific town planning: cities were built on a geometric grid with wide streets cutting at right angles and constructed from kiln-baked bricks of a uniform 1:2:4 ratio.

Their sanitation system was unparalleled: private bathrooms drained into covered underground street sewers with inspection manholes. Hydraulic wonders included the watertight Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro and Dholavira's 16 rock-cut reservoirs (up to 73 m long). In maritime trade, they constructed the world's earliest tidal dockyard at Lothal, sailing to Oman and Mesopotamia. Harappans pioneered cotton cultivation, binary stone weights, carnelian bead-making, and bronze casting, living in a remarkably peaceful society built on civic welfare and mutual accommodation.

Answer:

Harappan town planning was the most sophisticated urban achievement of the ancient world. It was defined by five stellar architectural pillars:

  • 1. The Geometric Grid Pattern:
    Cities were laid out on a planned grid with broad cardinal streets (up to 10 metres wide) running strictly North-South and East-West, intersecting at perfect 90° right angles. This divided cities into neat, organized rectangular residential sectors.
  • 2. Standardised Kiln-Baked Bricks:
    Across hundreds of settlements, builders used bricks manufactured to the universal 1:2:4 ratio (thickness : width : length). Baking in kilns made them waterproof, rock-solid, and interlocking.
  • 3. The Fortified Citadel & Lower Town:
    The western Citadel, built on high mud-brick platforms, housed public administrative halls, granaries, and the sacred Great Bath. The eastern Lower Town provided spacious two-storey residential quarters with central courtyards.
  • 4. Covered Underground Drainage:
    Every home had a paved bathroom draining into covered street sewers. Slabs could be lifted for inspection, and settling pits collected silt, preventing epidemics — an achievement that mirrors modern municipal sewerage.
  • 5. Quiet Residential Entrances:
    House doors opened into quiet side lanes rather than noisy main avenues, shielding families from dust, noise, and street traffic.
5-Mark LAQ Q2. Describe the agricultural practices, craft specialisations, and trade networks of the Harappans.
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

  • 1. Advanced Agriculture:
    Harappans farmed fertile alluvial plains using wooden ploughs (evidenced by the ploughed field at Kalibangan and toy plough at Banawali). They practiced double-cropping, growing wheat, barley, millets, sesame, mustard, lentils, chickpeas, and were the world's first producers of cotton. Domesticated cattle, buffalo, sheep, and goats provided milk and meat.
  • 2. Specialised Crafts & Metallurgy:
    Harappans alloyed copper with tin to produce bronze for chisels, axes, and statues like the 'Dancing Girl'. They excelled in carnelian bead-making at Lothal, cutting and drilling gemstones with diamond micro-drills. They manufactured wheel-thrown painted pottery and carved steatite seals.
  • 3. Standardised Weights & Measures:
    Trade was regulated using polished cubical chert weights following a binary system (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32...) for smaller units, and decimal systems for larger units, alongside graduated ivory measuring rulers.
  • 4. Long-Distance Maritime & Overland Trade:
    Harappans operated caravans along river routes and sailed across the Persian Gulf. They exchanged carnelian beads, cotton textiles, and ivory with Oman (Magan) and Mesopotamia (Meluhha), receiving copper and silver in return.
5-Mark LAQ Q3. What led to the decline and transformation of the great Indus-Sarasvati cities around 1900 BCE? Explain why this was not an abrupt end but a new beginning.
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

Around 1900 BCE, the mature urban phase of the Harappan Civilisation came to a gradual close. Modern science reveals that this was caused by ecological changes rather than human warfare:

  • 1. Global Climate Change:
    Geological studies show that around 2000 BCE, a widespread arid phase reduced monsoon rainfall across Asia. Prolonged droughts made large-scale agriculture difficult, leading to food shortages in densely populated cities.
  • 2. The Drying Up of the Sarasvati River:
    Tectonic plate movements in the Himalayas shifted the courses of the Yamuna and Sutlej rivers away from the Sarasvati basin. Deprived of glacial meltwater, the Sarasvati dried up in its central valley, forcing residents of Kalibangan, Banawali, and Ganweriwala to abandon their cities.
  • 3. Collapse of International Trade:
    With political crises in Mesopotamia, overseas demand for luxury beads and cotton declined, leading to the collapse of craft guilds and dockyard activity.
  • 4. Peaceful Eastward Migration (A New Beginning):
    The population did not vanish. Families migrated eastward into the rain-rich Ganga-Yamuna plains and southward into Gujarat and Maharashtra, establishing farming settlements.
  • 5. Unbroken Cultural Survival:
    Harappan traditions — the 'Namaste' greeting, yoga postures (Pashupati), armfuls of bangles, sindoor, pottery shapes, weights, and reverence for sacred trees and rivers — lived on, forming the enduring foundation of later Indian civilization!

⚡ Common Exam Hall Mistakes & 15-Point Rapid Revision

1. Six Common "Exam Hall Traps" & How to Avoid Them

⚠️ Common Student Mistakes in Chapter 6
  1. Mistake 1: Writing that the Harappans were conquered by foreign Aryan armies.
    Correct Fact: Modern archaeology has completely disproven the invasion myth. There are no signs of battlefields, war destruction, or weapons. The civilization transformed due to climate change and the drying of the Sarasvati River.
  2. Mistake 2: Confusing the ploughed field site with the toy plough site.
    Correct Fact: The actual ploughed field was found at Kalibangan (Rajasthan). The terracotta toy model of a plough was found at Banawali (Haryana).
  3. Mistake 3: Stating that Dholavira was built of baked clay bricks.
    Correct Fact: Dholavira was unique because its massive walls, gateways, and reservoirs were built of cut stone masonry, not baked clay bricks!
  4. Mistake 4: Believing the Great Bath was an everyday public swimming pool.
    Correct Fact: Every house in Mohenjo-daro had its own bathroom. The Great Bath was on the Citadel and was used for special religious and ritual purification baths.
  5. Mistake 5: Writing that Harappans used iron tools.
    Correct Fact: The Harappan civilization was a Bronze Age civilization. Iron was completely unknown to Harappans; iron was introduced in India much later around 1000 BCE.
  6. Mistake 6: Thinking Harappans had no trade with other countries.
    Correct Fact: Harappans were master seafarers who traded extensively with Oman (Magan), Bahrain (Dilmun), and Mesopotamia (Meluhha) through the port of Lothal.

2. 15 Ultra-Crisp Rapid Revision One-Liners (5-Minute Exam Prep)

  1. ⚡ Definition of Civilisation: An advanced society characterized by government, urbanism, crafts, long-distance trade, and writing.
  2. ⚡ Neolithic Predecessor: Mehrgarh in Balochistan (~7000 BCE) is the earliest known farming and cattle-herding village in South Asia.
  3. ⚡ Mature Urban Phase: The Harappan civilization flourished in its mature urban form between 2600 BCE and 1900 BCE.
  4. ⚡ Three Names: Known as the Indus Valley (first sites), Harappan (first excavated site, 1920-21), and Indus-Sarasvati (vast majority of sites).
  5. ⚡ The Sarasvati River: Known today as the seasonal Ghaggar-Hakra River; over 1,000 Harappan sites are located along its ancient basin.
  6. ⚡ Town Planning Layout: Most cities had a western fortified Citadel on a raised platform and an eastern Lower Town for residences.
  7. ⚡ The Dholavira Exception: Dholavira (Gujarat) had three distinct zones and was built with dressed stone masonry.
  8. ⚡ Grid Street Network: Wide streets ran strictly North-South and East-West, intersecting at perfect 90° right angles.
  9. ⚡ Standardised Bricks: Kiln-baked bricks followed the uniform mathematical ratio of 1 : 2 : 4 across all settlements.
  10. ⚡ Sanitation Miracle: Private household bathrooms drained into covered underground street sewers with inspection manholes.
  11. ⚡ The Great Bath: A watertight ceremonial tank in Mohenjo-daro sealed with a thick lining of natural bitumen (mineral tar).
  12. ⚡ Water Harvesting at Dholavira: Featured 16 giant rock-cut reservoirs, the largest measuring an impressive 73 metres long!
  13. ⚡ Lothal Tidal Dockyard: World's earliest engineered maritime dockyard (216 m × 37 m) connected to the Gulf of Khambhat.
  14. ⚡ Mesopotamian Trade: Mesopotamian tablets record active maritime trade with Meluhha (the Harappan Civilisation).
  15. ⚡ Living Heritage: The 'Namaste' greeting, yoga poses (Pashupati), armfuls of bangles, and moral fables (Lothal pot) survive unbroken in India today!
