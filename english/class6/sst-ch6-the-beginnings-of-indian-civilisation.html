TOPIC 1: What Is a Civilisation? (The 7 Core Pillars)
"The most ancient civilisation of India, known variously as the Harappan, Indus or Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation, was indeed remarkable in many ways. ... [It showed how] a well-balanced community lives — in which the differences between the rich and the poor are not glaring. ... In essence, the Harappan societal scenario was not that of ‘exploitation’, but of mutual ‘accommodation’."
— Prof. B.B. Lal (Renowned Indian Archaeologist)

At an advanced stage of human development, societies organize beyond simple tribal villages and form a civilisation. In historical study, a society is recognized as a civilisation when it exhibits at least the following seven essential characteristics:

  1. Government & Administration: Formal political and administrative machinery to govern complex communities, enforce public civic rules, maintain municipal hygiene, and organize collective public works.
  2. Urbanism & Town Planning: Growth of planned cities with designated residential, administrative, and manufacturing quarters, accompanied by systematic water supply and drainage systems.
  3. Specialized Crafts & Industries: Efficient acquisition of raw materials (stones, timber, ores) and production of refined finished goods (beads, pottery, bronze tools, textiles).
  4. Internal & External Trade: Dynamic commerce exchanging surpluses within local regions and across distant overseas lands using standardized weights and measures.
  5. A System of Writing: Script used to record commercial transactions, ownership of goods, contracts, and communication.
  6. Cultural & Aesthetic Expressions: Shared values and worldviews expressed through architecture, sculpture, painting, games, oral traditions, and religious customs.
  7. Productive Agriculture: Generation of agricultural food surpluses by rural farmers to nourish non-farming urban populations (craftspeople, traders, administrators).
📘 Key Definition: Metallurgy

Metallurgy: The science and technology of extracting metals from their mineral ores, purifying them, combining them to create stronger alloys (e.g. Copper + Tin = Bronze), and shaping them into durable implements, vessels, and ornaments.

  • Analysis: Productive Agriculture is the foundational bedrock of all civilisation! Without an agricultural food surplus, people must spend all their daily hours foraging or hunting for immediate survival. A food surplus frees up a portion of the population to specialize as craftspersons, architects, traders, thinkers, and administrators, which directly enables urbanism, writing, metallurgy, and trade.

Global Timeline of Early Civilisations

  • Mesopotamia (Modern Iraq & Syria): Emerged around 6,000 years ago (~4000 BCE) between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers.
  • Ancient Egypt: Flourished along the Nile River around 3100 BCE.
  • Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation: Flourished in northwestern South Asia from roughly 2600 BCE to 1900 BCE (Mature Urban Phase).
TOPIC 2: From Village to City & The Geographical Landscape
  • The Fertile River Basins: The vast plains of Punjab and Sindh are enriched and watered by the mighty River Indus (Sindhu) and its five tributaries (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Sutlej).
  • The Mighty Sarasvatī River: A few millennia ago, another great perennial river, the Sarasvatī, flowed parallel to the Indus from the Himalayan foothills through Haryana, Punjab, Rajasthan, and Gujarat into the Rann of Kutch. Today, its dry paleochannel is identified as the seasonal Ghaggar in India and Hakra in Pakistan (the Ghaggar-Hakra River).
  • The Transition (~2600 BCE): Around 3500 BCE, farming villages grew into thriving market towns. Around 2600 BCE, these towns leaped into well-planned, fortified metropolitan cities — a development celebrated by historians as the 'First Urbanisation of India'.

TOPIC 3: Master Town-Planning & Architectural Marvels
  • Grid-Iron Street Pattern: Streets were wide, straight, and intersected at perfect right angles (cardinal directions: North-South and East-West), allowing refreshing prevailing winds to clean the streets naturally.
  • Standardized Bricks: Buildings were constructed using standardized kiln-burnt bricks and sun-dried mud bricks following a universal ratio of 1 : 2 : 4 (thickness : width : length), ensuring architectural stability across hundreds of settlements.
  • Dual Division of Cities:
    • Upper Town (Citadel): Built on raised mud-brick platforms to the west; fortified with massive walls; contained administrative buildings, grand public structures, and elite residences.
    • Lower Town: Situated to the east at a lower level; much larger residential area with residential housing, craft workshops, and markets for ordinary citizens.
    • Unique Exception — Dholavira: Unlike other two-part cities, Dholavira had THREE distinct fortified zones: the Castle (Citadel), the Middle Town, and the Lower Town. Furthermore, Dholavira extensively utilized finely dressed stone masonry instead of burnt bricks!
  • Equal Quality of Construction: Prof. B.B. Lal observed that small two-room houses and large multi-room houses were constructed with the exact same high-quality bricks and mortar, demonstrating an egalitarian society without glaring wealth disparities.

The Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro

  • Dimensions: An impressive rectangular tank measuring approximately 12 metres long, 7 metres wide, and 2.4 metres deep (Fig. 6.6).
  • Waterproofing Engineering: Built of finely fitted bricks set in gypsum mortar, sealed with a thick backing layer of natural bitumen (mineral tar), making it the earliest known waterproof tank in world history.
  • Water Supply & Drainage: Flight of brick steps led down into the water from northern and southern ends. Water was brought in from an adjacent room with a well, while a corbelled drain in the southwest corner allowed stale water to be completely emptied and replaced with clean freshwater.
  • Historical Purpose: Ruled out as a common public bath because almost every private Harappan home had its own bathroom. Scholars agree it was an exclusive sacred tank used for special religious purification rituals.
TOPIC 4: World-Class Water Management, Wells & Drainage Systems

1. The Covered Underground Drainage Network

  • Harappan civic hygiene was thousands of years ahead of any contemporary civilisation (and even medieval Europe).
  • Every private home had a sloping brick-paved bathroom floor draining into small wall channels.
  • These house drains connected into covered sewer lines running beneath street pavements. Drains were covered with brick slabs or stone slabs that could be lifted for periodic municipal inspection and desilting.

2. The ~700 Wells of Mohenjo-daro

  • In Mohenjo-daro alone, archaeologists discovered approximately 700 circular brick-lined wells.
  • Wells were strategically placed in private courtyards as well as public street corners, providing continuous clean drinking water to all inhabitants for over 700 years.

3. The Engineering Genius of Dholavira Reservoirs

  • Located in the arid, salt-encrusted Rann of Kutch (Gujarat), Dholavira received erratic seasonal rainfall.
  • Harappan engineers dammed two seasonal rivulets (Mansar and Manhar) and excavated at least six colossal reservoirs cut directly into bedrock and fortified with stone masonry (Fig. 6.8).
  • The largest reservoir measured an extraordinary 73 metres in length!
  • These reservoirs were linked via underground stone conduits to collect storm runoff, settle sediment, and store millions of litres of freshwater — an ancient masterpiece of rainwater harvesting.
📘 Key Definition: Reservoir

Reservoir: A large natural or artificial storage basin engineered to collect, retain, and manage water for irrigation, drinking, and municipal supply.

TOPIC 5: Agriculture, Food & Diverse Diet
  • Crops Cultivated: Barley, wheat (two varieties), millets (bajra at Gujarat sites), rice (found at Lothal and Rangpur), and pulses (peas, chickpeas, lentils).
  • First in Eurasia to Grow Cotton: The Harappans were the first civilization across the entire Eurasian landmass to domesticate, spin, and weave cotton into textiles!
  • Agricultural Implements: While wooden ploughs have decayed, a complete terracotta model of a plough was excavated at Banawali in Haryana (Fig. 6.9), proving the use of oxen-drawn furrowing ploughs.
  • Domestication of Animals: Reared humped zebu cattle, buffaloes, sheep, goats, and pigs for milk, draught labour, wool, and meat. They also caught freshwater river fish and marine fish using bronze hooks.
  • Scientific Residue Analysis of Cooking Pots: Chemical lipid analyses of ancient earthenware pots revealed startling culinary sophisticated: Harappans cooked with dairy products (ghee/milk) and seasoned their stews with turmeric, ginger, and banana!
📘 Key Definition: Pulses

Pulses: A category of leguminous crops harvested solely for their dry edible seeds, including beans, peas, and lentils (dal), providing vital protein in the Indian diet.

TOPIC 6: Crafts, Maritime Trade, Dockyard & Seals

1. Specialized Crafts & Bead Technology

  • Carnelian Beads: Craftspeople in Gujarat shaped vivid red carnelian gemstones into polished cylindrical beads (Fig. 6.10). They invented specialized micro-drills made of hard chert/stone to pierce narrow holes through the beads.
  • Shell Working: Coastal settlements harvested conch shells from the Arabian Sea, cutting them into gleaming white shell bangles and ladles.
  • Bronze Metallurgy: Blended copper with tin to cast tough bronze chisels, knives, mirrors, and artistic figurines.

2. The Lothal Dockyard & Overseas Commerce

  • The Lothal Dockyard Basin: Archaeologists excavated a gigantic brick-walled tidal basin at Lothal (Gujarat) measuring 217 metres in length by 36 metres in width (Fig. 6.12) — longer than two full football grounds!
  • A sluice-gate system regulated tidal water levels from the Bhogavo river, enabling sea-going transport ships to dock, load cargo, and sail across the Arabian Sea.
  • Global Trade Partners: Harappan products travelled to Mesopotamia (Susa, Ur), the Persian Gulf (Dilmun/Bahrain), and Oman (Magan). Evidence includes Harappan carnelian beads found in royal tombs at Susa and an etched Harappan ivory comb found in Oman (Fig. 6.11).

3. Steatite Seals & Undeciphered Script

  • Thousands of square and rectangular seals carved from steatite (soft talc stone) were excavated. After carving, seals were baked in kilns to white ceramic hardness.
  • Engravings: Feature masterfully carved animals — the mysterious one-horned unicorn (most frequent), majestic humped bull, horned tiger, rhinoceros, elephant, and buffalo.
  • Function: Merchants pressed seals into wet clay lumps (sealings) tied over the rope knots of merchandise sacks to guarantee authenticity and prevent tampering during transport.
  • Indus Script: Engraved at the top of seals; written from right to left; remains undeciphered to this day.
TOPIC 7: Art, Society, Daily Life & The Question of Decline

Iconic Masterpieces of Harappan Art

  • The 'Dancing Girl' of Mohenjo-daro: A world-famous 10.8 cm bronze statuette cast via the lost-wax technique (Fig. 6.15-4). She stands in a lively, confident posture with one hand on her hip, slightly bent knee, and her left arm completely covered with bangles.
  • The 'Priest-King' of Mohenjo-daro: A steatite bust of a bearded dignitary wearing a decorative trefoil-patterned shawl draped over his left shoulder, an ornate fillet headband, and an armlet.
  • The Pashupati Seal: A three-faced deity seated in a cross-legged yogic posture on a low platform, crowned with buffalo horns, surrounded by an elephant, a tiger, a rhinoceros, a water buffalo, and two deer beneath the seat (proto-Shiva).
  • Terracotta Figurines: Playful toys, wheeled bullock carts, birds that whistle, and a figurine seated in a reverent 'namaste' gesture.
  • Painted Pottery: Red pottery painted with black motifs, including a jar from Lothal depicting the fable of the clever thirsty crow dropping pebbles into a jar.

The End or a New Beginning? (Decline around 1900 BCE)

  • Around 1900 BCE, the great cities gradually emptied out. Public drainage fell into disuse, monumental buildings ceased to be built, and people scattered into small agrarian villages.
  • Dispelling the Invasion Myth: Early 20th-century theorists guessed that violent Aryan invasions destroyed Harappa. Modern archaeology has completely refuted this — excavations reveal zero traces of warfare, burning of cities, weapons of war, or mass graves. Harappan society was remarkably peaceful.
  • The Two Proven Environmental Factors:
    1. Global Climatic Aridity: Around 2200–1900 BCE, severe worldwide climate shifts caused weakening monsoon rainfall, prolonged droughts, and crop failure.
    2. The Drying Up of the Sarasvatī River: Tectonic shifts diverted headwaters (Yamuna and Sutlej) away from the Sarasvatī river basin. As the Sarasvatī dried up in its middle course, thriving cities like Kalibangan, Banawali, and Rakhigarhi lost their lifeline and were abandoned.
  • Cultural Continuity: The cities disappeared, but the people and their culture survived! Harappan ceramic shapes, binary weight systems, yogic postures, namaste greetings, bangles covering the arm, boat designs, and sacred motifs (swastika, pipal tree) directly fed into the subsequent historical stream of Indian civilisation.

Q1. Why does the civilisation studied in this chapter have several names? Discuss their significance.
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

The civilisation studied in this chapter is known by several names, each reflecting a specific phase of archaeological discovery and geographical understanding:

  • 1. Harappan Civilisation: In archaeology, when an ancient culture is discovered, it is standard scientific practice to name it after the type-site (the first site excavated). Since Harappa was the first settlement discovered in 1920–21 by Daya Ram Sahni, the entire culture is universally designated as the Harappan Civilisation.
  • 2. Indus Valley Civilisation: In the initial decades, major excavated cities like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro were located along the Indus River and its five tributaries. Therefore, early historians called it the Indus Valley Civilisation. However, modern archaeologists consider this term obsolete because hundreds of sites were later discovered far outside the Indus basin.
  • 3. Indus-Sarasvatī (Sindhu-Sarasvatī) Civilisation: Over the last fifty years, extensive explorations revealed that the densest cluster of settlements (including major cities like Rakhigarhi, Ganweriwala, Kalibangan, and Banawali) lies along the basin of the ancient Sarasvatī River (the modern seasonal Ghaggar-Hakra). Hence, modern Indian scholars prefer 'Indus-Sarasvatī' or 'Sindhu-Sarasvatī' as the most geographically accurate title.
Q2. Write a brief report (150 to 200 words) summing up some of the achievements of the Indus-Sarasvatī civilisation.
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

Report: Achievements of the Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation (~2600–1900 BCE)

The Indus-Sarasvatī Civilisation represents South Asia's first urban revolution, celebrated for extraordinary achievements in municipal planning, engineering, hygiene, and trade:

  • Precision Town Planning: Cities were constructed on strict grid layouts with wide streets meeting at right angles, standardized kiln-burnt bricks (1:2:4 ratio), and segregated functional zones (Citadel and Lower Town).
  • Hydraulic & Sanitary Engineering: Harappans pioneered advanced public health. They engineered underground covered street drainage, individual private bathrooms, over 700 brick wells in Mohenjo-daro, the bitumen-waterproofed Great Bath, and Dholavira's colossal rock-cut rainwater reservoirs.
  • Agricultural & Industrial Innovation: Harappans were the first in Eurasia to grow and weave cotton, mastered bronze metallurgy, and mass-produced precision carnelian beads and shell bangles.
  • Maritime Trade & Civic Harmony: Lothal's massive tidal dockyard (~217 × 36 m) supported flourishing maritime commerce with Mesopotamia and Oman, regulated by steatite seals and standardized cubical stone weights. Remarkably, this grand civilisation maintained public order without palaces, standing armies, or weapons of war, embodying a society founded on peaceful civic cooperation.
Q3. Imagine you have to travel from the city of Harappa to Kalibangan. What are your different options? Can you make a rough estimation of the amount of time each option might take?
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

Harappa is situated on the left bank of the Ravi River (in Punjab), while Kalibangan is located to the southeast on the banks of the Ghaggar/Sarasvatī River in northern Rajasthan. The straight-line overland distance is approximately 250 to 300 kilometres. In Harappan times (~2500 BCE), a traveller had three main options:

  1. Option A: Overland on Foot
    • Mode: Walking along established caravan tracks carrying personal provisions.
    • Speed: A brisk walking pace covers approximately 20–25 km per day.
    • Estimated Time: Approximately 12 to 15 days.
  2. Option B: Bullock Cart / Pack Animals (Oxen / Asses)
    • Mode: Wooden cart with solid wheels drawn by a pair of zebu oxen (similar to clay toy carts found in excavations), or pack bullocks carrying trade goods.
    • Speed: Carts move steadily at about 15–20 km per day over rough dirt tracks.
    • Estimated Time: Approximately 15 to 20 days (longer if travelling with heavy trade cargo).
  3. Option C: Combined Riverine & Land Route
    • Mode: Taking a flat-bottomed wooden boat down the Ravi River to the Chenab/Sutlej, and then travelling overland across the fertile divide to the Sarasvatī basin.
    • Speed: Downstream river boats travel faster (30–40 km per day), followed by 3–4 days of overland travel.
    • Estimated Time: Approximately 7 to 10 days.
Q4. Let us imagine a Harappan man or woman being transported to an average kitchen in today’s India. What are the four or five biggest surprises awaiting them?
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

A Harappan visitor transported to a modern 21st-century Indian kitchen would find some familiar staples (wheat flour, barley, turmeric, ginger, and lentils), but would be astonished by five revolutionary modern surprises:

  1. Piped Gas Stove & Instant Fire: Instead of laboriously rubbing fire-sticks or blowing on wood and dried cow-dung hearths (chulhas), touching a knob and clicking a lighter creates instant, smoke-free blue flame.
  2. Stainless Steel, Non-Stick & Plastic Utensils: Harappans cooked in earthen terracotta pots or heavy cast bronze/copper pans. Gleaming, lightweight stainless steel pressure cookers and colourful plastic containers would look like magic.
  3. Electric Refrigerator & Mixers: An electric ice-cold cupboard that preserves cooked dairy, vegetables, and water fresh for days without spoiling would be totally mind-boggling.
  4. Running Tap Water: Rather than hoisting water buckets from brick-lined courtyard wells or city reservoirs, turning a small metal tap instantly releases continuous flowing water at the kitchen sink.
  5. New World Vegetables (Potatoes, Tomatoes & Chillies): Harappans seasoned food with mustard, black pepper, and ginger. Potatoes, tomatoes, green chillies, and refined white sugar were completely unknown in ancient India (introduced from the Americas only a few centuries ago).

Q6. What mindset does the system of reservoirs at Dholavira reflect?
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

The remarkable network of at least six massive rock-cut and stone-built reservoirs at Dholavira (measuring up to 73 metres in length) reflects a sophisticated, forward-looking civilisational mindset:

  • Deep Environmental Awareness & Foresight: Located on the arid island of Khadir Bet where freshwater was scarce and rainfall erratic, Harappan planners did not rely on chance. They anticipated droughts and planned years in advance.
  • Scientific Water Conservation (Every Drop Counts): They engineered stone dams across the seasonal Mansar and Manhar nullahs to divert storm runoff into tiered settling tanks, demonstrating master-level hydraulic engineering.
  • Supreme Collective Civic Governance: Quarrying huge bedrock trenches and lifting tons of stone blocks required tremendous community organization, disciplined labor, and municipal oversight working for the common welfare of all citizens rather than personal royal luxury.
Q7. In Mohenjo-daro, about 700 wells built with bricks have been counted. They seem to have been regularly maintained and used for several centuries. Discuss the implications.
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

The existence of roughly 700 brick-built wells in Mohenjo-daro, maintained continuously over centuries, carries profound implications:

  • Universal Access to Clean Water: With ~700 wells, almost every neighborhood or private courtyard had immediate access to fresh underground water. Drinking water was not monopolized by an elite ruler.
  • Enduring Municipal Administration: Wells in flood-prone alluvial plains silt up rapidly unless cleaned and relined. That these wells functioned for 700+ years proves that Mohenjo-daro possessed an efficient, continuous municipal body that maintained public utilities uninterrupted across generations.
  • Hydrological Knowledge: The Harappans mastered the art of trapezoidal wedge-shaped bricks to line circular well walls so that the inward soil pressure locked the bricks tight instead of collapsing the well shaft!
Q8. It is often said that the Harappans had a high civic sense. Discuss the significance of this statement. Do you agree with it? Compare with citizens in a large city of India today.
View Complete Model Answer

Answer:

Yes, I wholeheartedly agree with this statement. The archaeological evidence unequivocally proves that Harappans possessed an extraordinarily high level of civic sense and public discipline:

  • Strict Adherence to Building Bylaws: For over 600 years, no private home encroached upon the wide public streets or clogged the lanes. Houses strictly conformed to grid alignments.
  • Sanitation Responsibility: Every homeowner maintained a private bath connected to an enclosed soak-pit, ensuring wastewater flowed into covered street sewers with inspection traps. Waste was never dumped haphazardly into public walkways.

Comparison with Citizens in a Large Indian City Today:

Civic Feature Ancient Harappan Cities (~2500 BCE) Modern Indian Metros (21st Century CE)
Drainage & Sewers Entirely covered street drains; regular desilting inspection traps. Open drains frequently choked with single-use plastic waste and construction debris.
Building Encroachment Zero illegal encroachment on public streets across 700 years. Frequent illegal extensions of shops and residences encroaching upon roads and footpaths.
Water Stewardship Every drop of rainwater harvested into community reservoirs (e.g. Dholavira). Widespread depletion of groundwater tables and wastage of municipal piped water.

Conclusion: Modern citizens can take profound inspiration from our Harappan ancestors' voluntary civic discipline, pride in public cleanliness, and sustainable urban living.

CBSE 2-Mark Short Answer How was the Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro made watertight?
View Answer & Explanation

Answer:

The Great Bath was made watertight through ingenious multi-layer engineering:

  1. Finely cut burnt bricks were laid on edge and mortared with gypsum cement.
  2. A thick backing layer of natural bitumen (mineral tar) was applied between the bricks and the outer wall, completely sealing the tank against any water leakage.
CBSE 3-Mark Analytical Why did the Harappans produce thousands of steatite seals? Mention two animals depicted on them.
View Answer & Explanation

Answer:

Purpose of Steatite Seals:

  • Trade Identification & Security: When merchants packed bags of goods for long-distance transit, they tied ropes around the neck of the bag and placed a lump of wet clay over the knot. Stamping the merchant's seal onto the wet clay left a clear impression (sealing). If the bag arrived with its sealing intact, the buyer knew the goods had not been opened or tampered with.
  • Identity Tokens: Seals also functioned as personal identity badges for traders and guild authorities.

Animals Depicted: The mythical one-horned unicorn (most common) and the humped zebu bull (as well as the horned tiger, elephant, and rhinoceros).

CBSE 5-Mark Case Study "The decline of the Harappan cities around 1900 BCE was not the end of Indian civilisation, but the beginning of a new phase." Elaborate on the causes of decline and the elements of cultural continuity.
View Answer & Explanation

Answer:

1. Scientific Causes of Decline:

  • Climatic Aridity: Paleoclimate research confirms a global arid phase starting around 2200 BCE, bringing weakened monsoons and severe droughts across South Asia.
  • Drying of the Sarasvatī River: Tectonic shifts diverted headwaters into the Yamuna and Indus, drying up the central Sarasvatī riverbed and forcing the abandonment of cities like Kalibangan, Banawali, and Ganweriwala.
  • Dispelling the Warfare Myth: Archaeology reveals zero weapons of war, massacres, or burnt layers. Harappans peacefully relocated when water and food supplies failed.

2. Cultural Continuity into Later Indian Civilisation:

  • Sacred Motifs: Worship of the pipal tree, reverent namaste folded-hands gesture, and auspicious Swastika symbols continued directly into later Hinduism and Buddhism.
  • Yogic Traditions: The meditative seated posture of the Pashupati seal prefigured Shiva as Mahayogi.
  • Daily Traditions: Women wearing bangles across the arm, bullock cart designs, and terracotta weights and measuring standards survived into later historic periods.

⚡ 5-Minute Rapid Revision & Exam Hall Traps

⚠️ 5 Common "Exam Hall Mistakes" & How to Avoid Them
  1. Mistake 1: Believing that the Harappan civilization was destroyed by an invading army.
    Fix: There is no archaeological evidence of warfare, military invasion, or destruction. The decline was caused by climate change (reduced rainfall) and the drying up of the Sarasvatī River.
  2. Mistake 2: Thinking Dholavira was divided into two parts like Mohenjo-daro.
    Fix: Most Harappan cities had 2 parts (Upper and Lower Town), but Dholavira had THREE distinct fortified divisions (Castle, Middle Town, and Lower Town) and used stone masonry!
  3. Mistake 3: Stating that the Harappans did not know about cotton.
    Fix: The Harappans were the first people in Eurasia to cultivate and weave cotton!
  4. Mistake 4: Confusing the Great Bath with a common public swimming pool.
    Fix: It was not a common bath (almost all private houses had individual bathrooms). It was an exclusive tank used for special religious or ritual purification baths.
  5. Mistake 5: Assuming the Indus script has been deciphered.
    Fix: The Indus script remains undeciphered to this day.
