Part A: "The Unlikely Best Friends" (Prose Story)

1. Pre-Reading Prompts Decoded

  • Why Friends are Important: Friends provide emotional warmth, companionship, and someone to share joy, secrets, and sorrows with. Without friends, even a palace feels lonely.
  • Meaning of 'Unlikely': 'Unlikely' means unexpected, improbable, or not expected to happen. "Unlikely best friends" refers to two individuals from completely different backgrounds, sizes, or species (like an elephant and a stray dog, or a cow and a leopard) who form a deeply loving bond.

2. Detailed Narrative Breakdown

1. Gajaraj in the Royal Stables: Gajaraj was the king's prize elephant, housed in the finest booth of the royal stables. The king had ordered the best caretakers to look after him. The mahout fed him sumptuous food and gave him daily refreshing baths in the elephant pond. Despite this lavish comfort, Gajaraj was sorrowful because he lacked a companion to play and converse with.

2. The Arrival of the Stray Dog: One evening, a scrawny, famished stray dog strayed into the stable smelling the delicious aroma of elephant food. Gajaraj noticed how exhausted and hungry the little dog was. Instead of chasing the intruder away, Gajaraj gently nudged some sweet rice balls and green fodder toward him. The dog ate eagerly, wagged his tail in gratitude, and slept curled near Gajaraj's massive legs.

3. The Flourishing Bond: Over subsequent weeks, the elephant and dog became inseparable companions. The mahout named the dog Buntee. Gajaraj would gently lift Buntee onto his broad back with his trunk, spray water playfully over him during pond baths, and share all his meals. The huge pachyderm and tiny canine proved that genuine affection needs no common language or size match.

4. The Separation: One afternoon, a visiting farmer saw Buntee doing playful acrobatics around Gajaraj. Wanting the intelligent dog to guard his rural farm, the farmer offered a handful of silver coins to the greedy mahout. Seeing quick money, the mahout sold Buntee and handed him over. Neither the farmer nor the mahout noticed the silent tears streaming down the faces of the two friends.

5. Gajaraj's Heartbreak: From the moment Buntee was dragged away, Gajaraj fell into profound depression. He refused sugarcane, sweet rice, and fresh leaves. He stood motionless with bowed head, refusing to enter the elephant pond for his bath. The mahout feared for his own job as the king's prized elephant grew thin and weak.

6. The Royal Proclamation & Reunion: When informed that Gajaraj was wasting away without any physical illness, the wise king immediately deduced that the elephant was grieving the sudden loss of his beloved companion. The king issued an urgent royal proclamation: "Whoever holds the dog that lived in the royal stable must release him immediately, or face royal punishment!" Upon hearing the proclamation, the frightened farmer untied Buntee. The loyal dog dashed across fields, racing miles back to the palace stables. Gajaraj trumpeted in ecstasy, lifting Buntee high in the air with his trunk, while Buntee licked Gajaraj's cheeks. Gajaraj immediately resumed eating, his heart healed by love!

Part B: "A Friend's Prayer" by Jill Wolf (Poem)

1. Pre-Reading Exploration: Wish vs. Hope vs. Prayer

  • Wish: A casual longing for something pleasant (e.g., "I wish my friend brings idli tomorrow.").
  • Hope: An expectation of a positive outcome with emotional investment (e.g., "I hope my friend and I win the race.").
  • Prayer: A solemn, heartfelt invocation seeking divine benevolence and spiritual well-being for another person (e.g., "May my friend get well soon."). Sentence 3 is closest to a true prayer because it invokes blessings for someone else's recovery without personal gain.

2. Stanza-by-Stanza Literary Breakdown

"May my friendships always be
The most important thing to me.
With special friends I feel I’m blessed,
So let me give my very best."

Explanation: The speaker begins with a solemn invocation: asking that the bond of friendship always remain the most cherished priority in life. Realizing that having true, special friends is an immense divine blessing, the speaker resolves to reciprocate by offering their genuine best effort, loyalty, and affection.

"I want to do much more than share
The hopes and plans of friends who care;
I’ll try all that a friend can do
To make their wishes come true."

Explanation: True friendship is not merely passive listening or casual conversation. The speaker wants to actively invest themselves into helping friends achieve their heartfelt ambitions, working selflessly to turn their dreams into reality.

"Let me use my heart to see,
To realise what friends can be,
And make no judgements from afar,
But love my friends the way they are."

Explanation: The closing stanza presents the poem's moral climax. The poet asks for the empathy to "use my heart to see"—to perceive character through love and understanding rather than superficial appearances. She resolves never to judge friends critically from a distance, but to embrace and cherish them unconditionally with all their unique qualities.

3. Poetic Devices & Analysis

  • Rhyme Scheme: Consistent AABB CCDD EEFF rhyming couplets (be/me, blessed/best; share/care, do/true; see/be, afar/are). This creates a soothing, melodious, prayerful rhythm.
  • Metaphor: "Let me use my heart to see" — Comparing emotional empathy and intuition to physical vision (seeing with emotional understanding rather than physical eyes).
  • Alliteration: Repetition of consonant sounds, e.g., "friends who care", "make their wishes come true".
  • Tone: Devotional, humble, sincere, and deeply compassionate.

Part C: "The Chair" (Short Story)

1. Detailed Narrative Breakdown

1. Mario's Boasting & Grandpa's Bet: Mario was an outgoing schoolboy who took immense pride in his social popularity. He constantly bragged that he had dozens of best friends at school. His wise grandfather listened patiently and offered a friendly wager: "Mario, I bet you a fruit chaat that you don't have as many real friends as you think. Most of them are mere companions or casual playmates." Mario eagerly accepted the bet, confident of winning.

2. The Invisible "Magic Chair": When Mario asked how to prove who his true friends were, his grandfather fetched an unseen object from the attic. Holding out empty hands, Grandpa announced: "Take this very special invisible chair. Because it is invisible, it is tricky to sit on. But take it to school; if you manage to sit on it, its magic will instantly reveal who your real friends are!"

3. The Schoolyard Spectacle: Filled with determination, Mario took the invisible chair to school. During the recess break, he gathered his classmates in a large circle and declared dramatically: "Nobody move! You are about to witness something miraculous!" Mario bent his knees and attempted to sit back onto the empty air. Immediately, he lost balance and crashed flat onto the ground!

4. Mockery vs. Support: Everyone in the circle burst into uncontrollable laughter, pointing fingers and mocking him. Thinking it was just a "technical glitch", Mario got up and tried sitting again. Once more, he tumbled backwards. The crowd roared with delight, relishing his humiliation. But on his third attempt, just as he was about to hit the hard dirt, three classmates—Guneet, Asma, and Deepa—lunged forward, caught him firmly under the arms, and prevented him from getting hurt!

5. The Revelation of True Friendship: Mario looked around. The dozens of people he considered his "best friends" had done nothing but mock him. Only Guneet, Asma, and Deepa cared enough to save him from falling. Mario finally grasped his grandfather's profound wisdom: Real friends don't laugh at our embarrassments or take joy in our misfortunes; they step forward to support us when we stumble.

6. The Fruit Chaat Celebration: That evening, Mario conceded the bet and brought his three true friends to meet his grandfather. They shared stories, celebrated with plates of delicious fruit chaat, and agreed to use the "invisible chair test" to cherish true friends for life.

Section 3: Master Vocabulary, Literary Device & Grammar Bank

1. The Three Forms of Past Tense Decoded

Tense Form Grammatical Structure When to Use • Core Function Examples from Unit 2
Simple Past Tense Subject + Verb (2nd form / V2) [add -d, -ed, or irregular] Shows an action that happened and finished at a definite time in the past. Gajaraj looked sad. The king ordered. Buntee wagged his tail.
Past Progressive Tense Subject + was / were + (Verb + ing) Shows an action that was ongoing or continuous at a specific moment in the past. Gajaraj was looking out. The dog was munching sweet rice.
Past Perfect Tense Subject + had + Past Participle (V3) Shows an action that had already been completed BEFORE another action in the past. The farmer had tied the dog before Gajaraj stopped eating.

2. Correlative Conjunctions: 'Neither... Nor' vs. 'Either... Or'

  • Neither... Nor (Negative Pairing): Connects two negative alternatives (meaning not the first one, AND not the second one).
    Example: "Neither the farmer nor the mahout noticed the two friends were in tears." (The farmer did not notice, and the mahout also did not notice).
    Subject-Verb Agreement Rule: The verb agrees with the closer subject: "Neither the teacher nor the students were present."
  • Either... Or (Affirmative Choice): Connects two positive alternatives where one of the two will occur.
    Example: "Either Rohit or Anuj will win the race."

3. Morphology: Root Words & Suffixes

A root word is a primary base word. A suffix is a letter or group of letters added to the end of a root word to modify its meaning or part of speech.

Root Word Suffix New Form Part of Speech Change • Example Sentence
friend (noun) -ly friendly (adj) The elephant was friendly with the dog.
magic (noun) -al magical (adj) Grandpa gave him a magical invisible chair.
care (noun/verb) -ful careful (adj) Be careful while balancing on the chair.
celebrate (verb) -tion celebration (noun) They had a grand celebration with fruit chaat.
courage (noun) -ous courageous (adj) The courageous dog dashed miles back to the palace.
friend (noun) -ship friendship (noun) Their friendship withstood the test of time.

  3. Contrast with Human Greed and Superficiality (1 Mark): The mahout, despite being entrusted with Gajaraj's care, betrayed his duty for petty monetary gain by selling Buntee to a passing farmer. In contrast, the animals remained incorruptible and loyal to each other until reunited.
  4. Real-Life Parallel & Pedagogical Moral (1 Mark): The true story of Tarra (elephant) and Bella (dog) at the Tennessee sanctuary mirrors this exact bond, teaching young learners that genuine friendship is defined by pure loyalty, unconditional empathy, and standing by each other unconditionally.

So kind and thoughtful, good and true,
And when you need me, may I be
A friend who's always there to see..."

Questions:
1. What four core virtues does the poet pray to cultivate within herself? (1 Mark)
2. How does the line "A friend who's always there to see" connect with the deeper concept of 'seeing with the heart'? (2 Marks)
3. Identify the rhyme scheme and one poetic device used in the extract. (1 Mark)

View Case Study 2 Solutions
  1. Core Virtues (1 Mark): The speaker prays to be kind, thoughtful, good, and true.

Section 6: Common Exam Mistakes & 15 Rapid Revision Points

5 Common Student Mistakes in English Literature & Grammar Exams

❌ Mistake 1: Confusing Past Progressive with Past Perfect

Incorrect: Writing "Gajaraj was had eating food." or using 'had + ing'.
Correct: Past Progressive is was/were + verb-ing (was eating). Past Perfect is had + V3 (had eaten).

❌ Mistake 2: Mixing Correlative Conjunctions

Incorrect: "Neither Gajaraj or Buntee were happy."
Correct: Always pair neither with nor, and either with or!

❌ Mistake 3: Misinterpreting "Seeing with the Heart"

Incorrect: Thinking the poet is discussing a biological organ.
Correct: "Using the heart to see" is a metaphor meaning understanding someone through empathy, kindness, and love rather than surface appearances.

❌ Mistake 4: Believing Mario's Chair was an Actual Wooden Object

Incorrect: Writing that the carpenter built a transparent plastic chair.
Correct: The chair was entirely imaginary/invisible. Grandpa used it as a psychological test to see who would catch Mario when he pretended to sit and fell.

❌ Mistake 5: Misunderstanding the Moral of Gajaraj's Story

Incorrect: Stating the story teaches that elephants are good circus animals.
Correct: The story proves that love and true companionship transcend physical differences and that material luxury cannot heal emotional loneliness.
