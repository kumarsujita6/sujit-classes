8.1 Artwork & The Definition of a Circle

Every geometric construction begins with the simplest curved locus: the circle.

Definition: Circle & Radius

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that lie at a fixed, constant distance (called the radius, r) from a given fixed point (called the center, P).

When you fix the metal needle tip of a compass firmly at center point P and sweep the pencil tip through 360°, every point marked by the pencil maintains the exact same distance from P. If the distance set against a ruler is 4 cm, the traced boundary is a circle of radius 4 cm.

Constructing Semicircles and Wave Patterns

  • Semicircle: Exactly half of a circle, subtended by a diameter line passing through the center.
  • Continuous Wave Pattern: A wavy line is constructed on a central baseline by drawing alternating semicircles. For a central segment AX of length 4 cm, its midpoint M (at 2 cm from A) acts as the center, and a compass opened to radius 2 cm draws a semicircular crest above the line. The next semicircle of equal 2 cm radius is drawn below the baseline with center at M′, creating a smooth, repeating periodic wave.

8.2 Squares, Rectangles & Cyclic Naming Rules

Defining Properties of a Rectangle

A rectangle is a four-sided plane figure (quadrilateral) satisfying two strict geometric criteria:

  1. All four interior angles are right angles: ∠A = ∠B = ∠C = ∠D = 90°.
  2. Opposite sides are equal in length and parallel: AB = CD and BC = DA (with AB || CD and BC || DA).

Defining Properties of a Square

A square is a special regular rectangle where, in addition to having all four angles equal to 90°, all four sides are strictly equal in length: AB = BC = CD = DA.

Crucial Geometric Naming Rule: Cyclic Ordering

In geometry, a polygon must always be named by listing its vertices in continuous consecutive order around its boundary (either clockwise or counter-clockwise).
- Valid Names for Rectangle ABCD: ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC, ADCB, DCBA, CBAD, BADC.
- Invalid Names: ACBD or PQSR (where Q and S are diagonal opposite vertices). Crossing diagonally across the interior when naming a polygon violates universal mathematical nomenclature!

Dot Grid Analysis (Reasoning Without Instruments)

On a square dot grid where horizontal and vertical dots are separated by 1 unit:

  • A line segment spanning Δx horizontal units and Δy vertical units has a squared length given by the Pythagorean relation (Δx)² + (Δy)².
  • Two lines are perpendicular if their grid slopes are negative reciprocals: e.g., a step of (+2 horizontal, +1 vertical) is perpendicular to a step of (−1 horizontal, +2 vertical).
  • This enables students to construct rotated squares and rectangles on dot paper without protractors or rulers, purely by counting grid offsets!

8.3 Constructing Rectangles and Perpendicular Rays

Step-by-Step Compass & Straightedge Construction of a Rectangle (Length 6 cm, Breadth 4 cm):

  1. Step 1 (Base): Draw a horizontal line segment AB of length 6 cm using a ruler.
  2. Step 2 (Perpendiculars): At vertex A, construct a ray perpendicular to AB (at 90°) using a set-square or compass. Similarly, construct a perpendicular ray at vertex B.
  3. Step 3 (Breadth Arcs): Open the compass to 4 cm against a ruler. With A as center, cut an arc on the perpendicular ray to locate vertex D. With B as center and the same 4 cm radius, cut an arc on the perpendicular ray to locate vertex C.
  4. Step 4 (Top Edge): Join vertex C to vertex D with a straightedge. Check with a ruler that CD = 6 cm and ∠C = ∠D = 90°. Quadrilateral ABCD is the required rectangle.
Theorem: Quadrilaterals with Four 90° Angles

Question: Is it possible to construct a quadrilateral where all four angles are 90°, but opposite sides are NOT equal?
Answer: No! In Euclidean plane geometry, if all four angles are 90°, the lines containing opposite sides are both perpendicular to the same adjacent segments. Hence, opposite sides are strictly parallel. The perpendicular distance between two parallel lines is constant everywhere, which mathematically forces opposite sides to have identical lengths.

8.4 An Exploration in Rectangles & Partitioning into Squares

Moving Invariant Points Along Parallel Sides

Consider rectangle ABCD with horizontal sides AB and CD. If a point X is chosen on side AD at distance d from A, and a point Y is chosen on side BC at the exact same distance d from B:

  • The segment XY is parallel to AB and CD.
  • The length XY is strictly equal to AB: XY = AB.
  • The newly enclosed quadrilateral ABYX is also a perfect rectangle.

Partitioning Rectangles into Identical Squares

A rectangle of length L and breadth B can be cleanly partitioned into n identical squares of side B if and only if the length is an exact integer multiple of the breadth:

Partition Criterion: L = n × B

- Can be divided into 2 squares: Dimensions where L = 2B (e.g., 6 cm × 3 cm, or 8 cm × 4 cm).
- Cannot be divided into 2 squares: Any rectangle where L ≠ 2B (e.g., 4 cm × 2.5 cm, where 4/2.5 = 1.6 ≠ 2).
- Can be divided into 3 squares: Dimensions where L = 3B (e.g., 9 cm × 3 cm, or 12 cm × 4 cm).
- Cannot be divided into 3 squares: Any rectangle where L ≠ 3B (e.g., 7 cm × 2 cm, where 7/2 = 3.5 ≠ 3).

The "Square with a Hole" Symmetry Problem

To cut or drill a circular hole into a square sheet so that the remaining border is completely uniform in all directions, the center of the circle must be placed at the intersection of the square's two diagonals (Point O). Because diagonals of a square are equal and bisect each other, Point O is the unique center of symmetry equidistant from all four corners and all four edges.

8.5 Exploring Diagonals of Rectangles and Squares

A diagonal is a line segment connecting two opposite (non-adjacent) vertices of a polygon. In any quadrilateral ABCD, there are two diagonals: AC and BD.

Diagonal Properties Comparison:

Geometric Property Rectangle ABCD Square ABCD
Length of Diagonals Equal: AC = BD Equal: AC = BD
Mutual Bisection Bisect each other at midpoint O (OA = OB = OC = OD) Bisect each other at midpoint O (OA = OB = OC = OD)
Angle at Intersection Oblique (acute/obtuse pair, ≠ 90°) Perpendicular (intersect at 90°)
Bisection of Vertex Angles Do NOT bisect 90° (divided into unequal angles, e.g., 50° and 40°) Strictly bisect 90° into equal 45° and 45°

Constructing a Rectangle Given Side = 4 cm and Diagonal = 8 cm:

  1. Draw base segment AB = 4 cm using a ruler.
  2. At vertex A, construct a perpendicular line at 90°.
  3. Open compass to diagonal length 8 cm. Set needle at vertex B and swing an arc intersecting the perpendicular line. Mark this intersection point as D.
  4. With D as center and radius 4 cm (length AB), draw an arc to the right.
  5. With B as center and radius equal to length AD, draw an arc intersecting the previous arc at point C.
  6. Join BC and CD. Quadrilateral ABCD is the completed rectangle with side 4 cm and diagonal 8 cm.

8.6 Points Equidistant from Two Points & The Rhombus

The Equidistant Locus Theorem

The set of all points that are equidistant from two given points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of the line segment AB. Any point P on this line satisfies PA = PB.

Constructing the Perpendicular Bisector:

  1. Given line segment AB, open compass to any radius strictly greater than half the length of AB (> ½ AB).
  2. With needle at A, draw two arcs, one above AB and one below AB.
  3. With needle at B and keeping the exact same compass radius, draw two arcs intersecting the previous arcs at points P and Q.
  4. Join P and Q with a straightedge. Line PQ is the perpendicular bisector of AB: it cuts AB at 90° and divides it into two equal halves.

Constructing the "House":

A classic architectural construction in Ganita Prakash combines a square wall base with a triangular roof. To ensure the roof does not tilt to one side, the apex vertex (the ridge tip of the roof) must be equidistant from the two top wall corners. Hence, the apex is constructed by intersecting two equal arcs drawn from the top corners, naturally placing it on the perpendicular bisector!

The Rhombus: 4 Equal Sides Without 90° Angles

Can a 4-sided figure have all sides equal in length but NOT be a square?
Yes! That figure is a Rhombus.

  • In a rhombus, all 4 sides have equal length: AB = BC = CD = DA.
  • Opposite sides are parallel, but the interior angles are not 90° (it has two equal acute angles and two equal obtuse angles).
  • A square is simply a specialized rhombus whose angles happen to be right angles!

Section 8.2 — Naming Challenge (Page 193): Invalid Name for Square PQRS

Question: Which of the following is NOT a valid mathematical name for the square shown?
1. PQSR     2. SPQR     3. RSPQ     4. QRSP

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Given:
A square with consecutive vertices labeled P, Q, R, S in clockwise order around its boundary.
Options: (1) PQSR, (2) SPQR, (3) RSPQ, (4) QRSP.

Step 2: Formula / Geometric Naming Rule:
Polygons must be named by reading vertices continuously around the perimeter cycle (either clockwise: P → Q → R → S, or counter-clockwise: P → S → R → Q). Vertices cannot jump across an interior diagonal.

Step 3: Checking options:
- Option 1 (PQSR): Moves from P to Q, but then jumps diagonally from Q to S, and then from S to R. This is non-cyclic!
- Option 2 (SPQR): Continuous clockwise cycle starting at S: S → P → Q → R. (Valid)
- Option 3 (RSPQ): Continuous clockwise cycle starting at R: R → S → P → Q. (Valid)
- Option 4 (QRSP): Continuous clockwise cycle starting at Q: Q → R → S → P. (Valid)

Step 4: Final value:
Option 1: PQSR.

Step 5: Concluding statement:
PQSR is not a name for the given square because it fails the cyclic boundary ordering rule.

Section 8.3 — Construct Q3 (Page 197): The Four 90° Angles Non-Equal Sides Paradox

Question: Is it possible to construct a 4-sided figure in which all four angles are equal to 90°, but opposite sides are NOT equal?

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Given proposition:
Quadrilateral with ∠A = ∠B = ∠C = ∠D = 90°, but AB ≠ CD or AD ≠ BC.
To determine: Is such a construction possible in plane geometry?

Step 2: Geometric Theorem used:
Parallel Postulate & Invariant Distance: Lines perpendicular to the same transverse line are parallel. The perpendicular distance between two parallel lines is constant along their entire length.

Step 3: Mathematical proof by contradiction:
If ∠A = 90° and ∠B = 90°, then rays AD and BC are parallel (AD || BC).
If ∠D = 90° and ∠C = 90°, segment CD is perpendicular to both parallel lines.
The length of segment CD measures the perpendicular distance between AD and BC.
Similarly, segment AB measures the perpendicular distance between AD and BC.
Since the perpendicular distance between parallel lines is constant, AB must strictly equal CD.
By identical logic, AD must strictly equal BC.

Step 4: Final value / conclusion:
Impossible.

Step 5: Concluding statement:
No, it is mathematically impossible; any plane quadrilateral with four 90° angles must have equal opposite sides.

Section 8.5 — Construct Q1 & Q2 (Page 211): Rectangles with Given Diagonal Angles

Question: (Q1) Construct a rectangle in which one diagonal divides the opposite angles into 50° and 40°. (Q2) Construct a rectangle where the diagonal divides angles into 45° and 45°. What do you observe about the sides?

Construction for Q1 (50° and 40°):
1. Draw base segment AB of convenient length (e.g., 5 cm).
2. At A, draw a perpendicular ray AP (at 90° to AB).
3. At A, construct a ray AD forming an angle of 40° with ray AP, so that ∠BAD = 50°.
4. At B, construct an angle of 40° with AB such that ray BD intersects ray AD at diagonal point D.
5. Complete the rectangle by drawing perpendiculars. The diagonal divides 90° into 50° and 40°, yielding unequal sides (Length ≠ Breadth).

1. Draw base segment AB of length 5 cm.
2. At A, construct a 90° ray AP.
3. At A, construct ray AC at 45° to AB.
4. At B, construct ray BC perpendicular to AB (at 90°), intersecting ray AC at vertex C.
5. In ΔABC, ∠A = 45°, ∠B = 90°, so ∠C = 45°. Therefore, side BC = side AB = 5 cm.
6. Observation: All four sides are equal; the rectangle is a square.

Section 8.6 — Construct Q1 (Page 215): Constructing a Bigger House (Sides = 7 cm)

Question: Construct a bigger house in which all sides of the wall and roof are of length 7 cm.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Given:
Wall base: Square of side 7 cm. Roof: Equilateral triangle of side 7 cm sitting on top.

Step 2: Construction Protocol:
1. Draw horizontal ground baseline AB = 7 cm.
2. At A and B, construct perpendicular rays at 90°.

Step 3: Verification:
All 5 boundary sides (AB, BC, CE, ED, DA) measure exactly 7 cm.

Step 4: Final figure:
Symmetrical 5-sided house polygon constructed.

5.1 Multiple Choice Questions (5 MCQs with Trap Analysis)

MCQ 1: Cyclic Naming of a Quadrilateral

Question: For a rectangle whose vertices are situated at corners A, B, C, D in clockwise succession, which of the following is an INVALID name?

A) BCDA     B) DABC     C) ACBD     D) ADCB

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: C) ACBD

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Students often assume any permutation of the four letters {A, B, C, D} is acceptable. However, in mathematical naming conventions, vertices must follow the continuous perimeter boundary. In ACBD, the name jumps from A to C across the interior diagonal, making it invalid.

Verification: BCDA (clockwise from B), DABC (clockwise from D), and ADCB (counter-clockwise from A) are all continuous perimeter paths.

MCQ 2: Diagonal Angle Bisection Condition

Question: In a rectangle, the diagonals bisect the corner 90° angles into two equal 45° parts IF AND ONLY IF:

A) Length is twice the breadth     B) The rectangle is a square     C) The perimeter is 20 cm     D) The diagonals are perpendicular to the sides

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: B) The rectangle is a square

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Students frequently misremember that diagonals bisect vertex angles in ALL rectangles. In a non-square rectangle (e.g. 4 cm × 6 cm), the diagonal divides the 90° corner into unequal parts (such as 56° and 34°). Only when adjacent sides are equal (an isosceles right triangle) do the angles equal 45° and 45°.

MCQ 3: Locus of Points Equidistant from Endpoints

Question: The geometric set of all coplanar points that are equidistant from two distinct points P and Q is:

A) A circle with diameter PQ     B) A line segment parallel to PQ     C) The perpendicular bisector of PQ     D) An angle bisector

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: C) The perpendicular bisector of PQ

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Option A is a tempting distractor because circles involve "equidistant" concepts, but a circle is equidistant from ONE center point. Points equidistant from TWO points form an infinite straight line perpendicular to PQ passing through its midpoint.

MCQ 4: Four Equal Sides Without Right Angles

Question: A four-sided plane figure having all four sides equal in length, but whose interior angles are NOT 90°, is called a:

A) Trapezium     B) Kite     C) Rectangle     D) Rhombus

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: D) Rhombus

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Students often believe that "4 equal sides" automatically forces the shape to be a square. A rhombus has four equal sides, but its angles can be any acute/obtuse supplementary pair (such as 60° and 120°).

MCQ 5: Partitioning into Identical Squares

Question: Which of the following rectangular dimensions can be divided into exactly 3 identical squares in a single row?

A) 10 cm × 3 cm     B) 12 cm × 4 cm     C) 15 cm × 6 cm     D) 8 cm × 3 cm

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: B) 12 cm × 4 cm

Conceptual Trap Decoded: In Option A, 10 ÷ 3 = 3.33 ≠ 3. For 3 identical squares of side B, the length L must satisfy L = 3 × B. In Option B, 12 cm = 3 × 4 cm, yielding three 4 cm × 4 cm squares.

5.2 Assertion & Reasoning Questions (4 Questions)

Directions: Choose (A) if both A and R are true and R is correct explanation of A; (B) if both are true but R is NOT correct explanation; (C) if A is true but R is false; (D) if A is false but R is true.

A&R 1: Four 90° Angles Force Opposite Side Equality

Assertion (A): It is impossible to construct a plane quadrilateral with four 90° angles whose opposite sides have unequal lengths.
Reason (R): Lines perpendicular to the same transverse line are parallel, and the perpendicular distance between two parallel lines is constant throughout.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: In any quadrilateral with four right angles, opposite sides are parallel. The constant distance between parallel lines guarantees that opposite sides must be strictly equal in length.

A&R 2: Center of a Square Sheet

Assertion (A): The intersection point of the diagonals of a square is equidistant from all four of its vertices.
Reason (R): The diagonals of a square are equal in length and bisect each other.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: Since AC = BD and they bisect at O, OA = OC = ½ AC and OB = OD = ½ BD. Because AC = BD, OA = OB = OC = OD. Reason directly explains Assertion.

A&R 3: Perpendicular Bisector Compass Radius

Assertion (A): To construct the perpendicular bisector of a line segment AB of length 6 cm, the compass radius must be set to at least 3.5 cm.
Reason (R): If the compass radius is less than half the length of AB (< 3 cm), the arcs drawn from A and B will never intersect.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: By the Triangle Inequality, two circles with radii r1 and r2 centered at distance D can intersect if and only if r1 + r2 ≥ D. When r1 = r2 = r, we require 2r > D ⇒ r > D/2. For D = 6 cm, r must be strictly greater than 3 cm.

A&R 4: Diagonal Bisection in General Rectangles

Assertion (A): In any rectangle of dimensions 8 cm × 3 cm, the diagonal bisects the corner right angle into two 45° angles.
Reason (R): A diagonal connects opposite vertices of a quadrilateral.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (D) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) is true.

Explanation: In an 8 cm × 3 cm rectangle, the diagonal divides the right angle into unequal angles: tan(θ) = 3/8 ⇒ θ ≈ 20.6° and 69.4°, NOT 45°. Diagonals bisect corner angles only in squares.

[Part (b): Measurement of AD — 1 Mark]
[Part (c): Pythagorean verification — 1.5 Marks]

Question: On a square dot grid where adjacent dots are 1 cm apart, a student wants to draw a square whose sides are not horizontal or vertical.

Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 Rapid Revision One-Liners

Top 5 Exam Hall Traps in Geometric Constructions

⚠️ Trap 1: Non-Cyclic Quadrilateral Naming (The ACBD Fallacy)

A frequent error in geometry exams is writing names like ACBD or PQSR for a quadrilateral. In mathematical nomenclature, you must always travel around the continuous boundary perimeter (clockwise or counter-clockwise). Never jump across an interior diagonal!

⚠️ Trap 2: Believing Diagonals Bisect 90° Angles in ALL Rectangles

Students often incorrectly assume that diagonals divide the corner 90° angle into two 45° angles in any rectangle. This is TRUE only in a square! In a general rectangle (e.g., 6 cm × 4 cm), the diagonal splits the 90° angle into unequal portions (e.g., 56° and 34°).

⚠️ Trap 3: Insufficient Compass Radius in Perpendicular Bisector Construction

When constructing the perpendicular bisector of segment AB, opening the compass to a radius less than or equal to half of AB results in arcs that never intersect. Always ensure compass radius is strictly greater than ½ AB (e.g., about 2/3 of the line length).

⚠️ Trap 4: Assuming "Four Equal Sides" Means It Must Be a Square

A quadrilateral with four equal sides is NOT necessarily a square. It can be a Rhombus! A rhombus has four equal sides, but its interior angles are not 90° and its diagonals are unequal in length.

⚠️ Trap 5: Measuring from the Outer Plastic End of the Ruler

In precision constructions, measuring segments from the physical edge of a wooden or plastic ruler introduces a 2 to 4 mm parallax error. Always align pencil marks exactly with the '0' graduation line of the ruler.

15 Rapid Revision One-Liners (Pre-Exam Checklist)

  1. A circle is the geometric locus of all points in a plane equidistant from a fixed center point.
  2. The constant distance from the center to any point on the circle is called the radius.
  3. A semicircle is exactly half a circle, subtended by a diameter passing through the center.
  4. A rectangle is a quadrilateral with four 90° interior angles and opposite sides that are equal and parallel.
  5. A square is a regular rectangle with all four sides equal in length.
  6. Quadrilaterals must always be named cyclically along their perimeter (e.g., ABCD, not ACBD).
  7. If all four angles of a plane quadrilateral are 90°, opposite sides are mathematically guaranteed to be equal.
  8. A rectangle can be partitioned into n identical squares if and only if Length = n × Breadth.
  9. The exact center of symmetry of a square or rectangle is the intersection point of its two diagonals.
  10. The diagonals of a rectangle are equal in length and bisect each other.
  11. The diagonals of a square are equal, bisect each other at 90°, and bisect vertex angles into 45° and 45°.
  12. In a non-square rectangle, diagonals divide the 90° vertex angles into unequal parts.
  13. The locus of points equidistant from two points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of segment AB.
  14. A rhombus has four equal sides, but its angles are not 90° and its diagonals have unequal lengths.
  15. To construct a perpendicular bisector, always set the compass radius strictly greater than half the segment length.
