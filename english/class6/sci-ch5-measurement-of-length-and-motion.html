5.1 How Do We Measure?

When Deepa, an eleven-year-old girl in Haryana, grew taller before the new school academic session, her mother took her to a cloth shop. Deepa noticed something very interesting:

  • The shopkeeper measured the cloth using a straight metal measuring rod.
  • The tailor measured her chest, waist, and sleeve length using a flexible measuring tape.
  • Deepa’s classmates shared different experiences: Hardeep saw his grandmother measure cloth by the length of her forearm (cubit), while Padma saw a village farmer measure plots by counting his strides (walking steps) or foot lengths.

🧪 Activity 5.1 & Table 5.1: The Handspan Experiment

Setup: Five students (Deepa, Hardeep, Padma, Tasneem, Anish) measure the exact same teacher’s study table using their handspans (the distance from the tip of the thumb to the tip of the little finger when the hand is stretched wide open).

Observation: Each student recorded a different count! Deepa counted 8.5 handspans, Hardeep counted 7 handspans, while Padma counted 9 handspans.

Scientific Conclusion: The length of the table remained completely fixed. However, human body parts (handspan, cubit, stride) vary significantly from person to person. Therefore, body parts cannot serve as reliable or standard units of measurement.

📚 Historical Measurement in Ancient India

Ancient India possessed highly sophisticated measurement systems used in architecture, urban planning, and temple construction:

  • Angula: The width of a middle finger. Traditional carpenters and sculptors still use angula today.
  • Dhanusa: The length of an archer’s bow (roughly 4 cubits or ~6 feet).
  • Yojana: A long-distance unit used for distances between ancient cities and astronomical calculations (roughly 8 to 12 km).
  • Indus Valley Excavations: Archaeologists excavated ivory and shell scales at Lothal (Gujarat) and Mohenjo-daro, revealing precise, finely calibrated decimal divisions—proving that standardized measurement existed in India over 4,500 years ago!

5.2 Standard Units of Measurement (The SI System)

To eliminate confusion arising from varying personal measurements and to facilitate international trade and scientific communication, scientists around the globe adopted a uniform system called the International System of Units (Système International d'Unités – SI) in 1960.

Physical Quantity SI Base Unit Symbol Common Practical Sub-units & Multiples
Length / Distance Metre m Millimetre (mm), Centimetre (cm), Kilometre (km)
Mass / Weight Kilogram kg Gram (g), Milligram (mg), Quintal (100 kg), Tonne (1000 kg)
Time Second s Minute (min), Hour (h), Day, Year

🧠 The Metric Length Ladder: Conversions Made Simple

Memorize these universal relationships:

  • 1 kilometre (km) = 1,000 metres (m) → Used for distances between cities, highways, marathon routes.
  • 1 metre (m) = 100 centimetres (cm) → Used for room dimensions, cloth length, human height.
  • 1 centimetre (cm) = 10 millimetres (mm) → Used for pencils, erasers, notebooks.
  • 1 metre (m) = 1,000 millimetres (mm) → 1 mm is 0.001 m (the smallest division on your standard 15-cm school ruler).

5.3 The Correct Technique of Measuring Length

Having a standard ruler is not enough; one must use it with scientific accuracy. The NCERT Curiosity textbook highlights three crucial rules:

  1. Rule 1: Placement of the Scale: Always place the scale directly along the edge of the object to be measured, in close contact with it. If the ruler is placed tilted or diagonal, the measured length will be falsely larger than the true value.
  2. Rule 2: Dealing with Broken or Damaged Zero Marks: In many school rulers, the zero ('0') mark at the end is chipped, worn out, or broken.
    • Do not try to guess where the zero mark was.
    • Instead, place the object at any clear, fully visible integer mark, such as the 1.0 cm or 2.0 cm mark.
    • Take the reading at the other end, and then subtract the starting mark from the final reading:
      True Length = Final Reading − Initial Reading
      Example: If starting mark = 1.0 cm and ending mark = 14.3 cm, then True Length = 14.3 − 1.0 = 13.3 cm.
  3. Rule 3: Avoiding Parallax Error (Correct Eye Position): Your eye must be placed vertically directly above the calibration mark being read.
    • If you view the scale from an angled position (left or right), the reading appears shifted due to the thickness of the ruler. This error caused by looking from an angle is called parallax error.

5.4 How to Measure the Length of a Curved Line

A straight rigid ruler cannot bend around the rim of a round tumbler, the trunk of a tree, or an irregular river curve on a map. To measure a curved line:

  1. Take a piece of non-stretchable cotton thread or string.
  2. Tie a small knot at one end to serve as your starting point (Point A).
  3. Place the knot at one end of the curved line. Using your thumb and forefinger, carefully run small segments of the thread along the curvature of the line without stretching it.
  4. Make an ink mark on the thread where it touches the final endpoint (Point B) of the curved line.
  5. Now, stretch the thread straight along a standard metre scale, placing the knot at 0 cm and noting the reading at the ink mark. This reading gives the exact length of the curved line!

5.5 Describing Position: The Reference Point

Suppose you tell a guest, "Our school is located 2 kilometres away." The guest still cannot find the school because you haven't mentioned from where and in which direction!

📍 Reference Point (Origin)

A reference point is a fixed point or stationary object with respect to which the position or motion of another object is described. To state an object’s position completely, we need:

  • The reference point (e.g., the railway station or bus stop).
  • The distance from the reference point (e.g., 2 km).
  • The direction relative to the reference point (e.g., due North).

5.6 Rest and Motion

Look around your room right now: the walls, your study desk, and the blackboard are not changing their positions with respect to the floor as time passes. They are at rest.

On the other hand, a flying butterfly, a car driving down the street, or the moving hands of a clock continuously change their positions over time with respect to their surroundings. They are in motion.

🚀 Scientific Definition of Motion

An object is said to be in motion if its position continuously changes with respect to a chosen stationary reference point with the passage of time.

5.7 Types of Motion

Do all moving things move in the exact same manner? No! A train on a straight railway track moves differently from the blades of a ceiling fan or the swing in a children's playground. In Grade 6 Science, motion is classified into three fundamental types:

1. Linear Motion (Rectilinear Motion)

  • Definition: When an object moves along a straight line path, its motion is called linear or rectilinear motion.
  • NCERT Examples:
    • A car or bus travelling along a straight highway.
    • Soldiers marching in a straight row during a Republic Day parade.
    • A stone dropped from the roof of a building falling vertically downwards under gravity.
    • A sprinter running along a straight 100-metre racing track.
    • The striker sliding in a straight line on a carrom board after being flicked.

2. Circular Motion

  • Definition: When an object moves along a circular path such that its distance from a fixed central point remains constant, its motion is called circular motion.
  • NCERT Examples:
    • A stone tied to the end of a string whirled around in a horizontal circle by a student.
    • The tip of the minute or hour hand of a wall clock moving around the circular dial.
    • The blades of an electric ceiling fan rotating around its central motor axle.
    • The Earth revolving around the Sun in its orbit (approximately circular).
    • A bull moving in a circle around an ancient oil press (kolhu) or Persian water wheel.

3. Oscillatory Motion (Periodic / Vibratory Motion)

  • Definition: When an object moves to and fro (back and forth) about a fixed central position (called the mean position), its motion is called oscillatory motion.
  • NCERT Examples:
    • A child sitting on a swing in a park moving forwards and backwards.
    • The pendulum of an antique grandfather wall clock swinging left and right.
    • The stretched metallic string of a guitar or sitar vibrating when plucked.
    • The stretched membrane of a tabla or dholak vibrating when struck.
    • The branch of a tree swaying back and forth in a gust of wind.
    • The needle of a sewing machine moving up and down rapidly while stitching.

⚡ Combinations of Different Types of Motion (Exam Special!)

Many real-world objects exhibit more than one type of motion simultaneously:

  • Moving Bicycle: The wheels rotate on their axles (circular / rotational motion) while the whole bicycle travels forward along the road (linear motion).
  • Rolling Cricket Ball: The ball spins around its axis (rotational) and simultaneously rolls forward along the pitch (linear).
  • Sewing Machine: The wheel turns with circular motion, while its needle moves rapidly up and down with periodic oscillatory motion.
  • Earth: Rotates on its axis every 24 hours (rotational) while revolving around the Sun in its orbit (circular).

Learning Further 1 How to find the thickness of a single page of your textbook using a standard scale?

Scientific Method:

  1. A single page of paper is thinner than 0.1 mm, which is below the 1 mm least count of a school ruler. Thus, you cannot measure one page directly.
  2. Instead, take 100 pages of the textbook (excluding the thick cardboard covers).
  3. Press the pages firmly together so there is no trapped air between them.
  4. Measure the total thickness of the 100 pages using your 15-cm ruler (say it measures 10 mm or 1.0 cm).
  5. Calculate the thickness of a single page using the formula:
    Thickness of 1 page = Total measured thickness ÷ Number of pages
    Thickness = 10 mm ÷ 100 = 0.1 mm (or 0.01 cm).
Learning Further 2 Why do leaves collected from the same tree vary in length and breadth? (Table 5.7)

Leaves on the exact same tree exhibit natural variations in dimensions due to several biological and environmental factors:

  • Age & Maturity: Newly sprouted young leaves at branch tips are much smaller than fully mature leaves lower down.
  • Sunlight Exposure (Sun vs Shade Leaves): Leaves on the outer, sunlit canopy receive abundant light and develop thicker, different proportions compared to shaded inner leaves.
  • Water and Mineral Supply: Leaves closer to primary veins or lower branches receive water earlier during growth spurts.
Learning Further 3 Bicycle distance measurement using a spoke clicker and Jones Counter (Fig 5.23)

Principle: In one complete rotation of a bicycle wheel, the bicycle travels forward by a distance equal to the circumference of the wheel.

  • Step 1: Measure the circumference of the front wheel boundary using string (say C = 1.8 metres).
  • Step 2: Attach a flexible strip hitting the frame to count rotations (N clicks = N full turns).
  • Step 3: Total Distance = Circumference (C) × Number of turns (N).
    Example: If the wheel makes 200 turns, Distance = 1.8 m × 200 = 360 metres.
  • Jones Counter: A geared mechanical counting device mounted on a bicycle front hub used internationally by marathon and Olympic organizers to officially calibrate and certify road race courses with millimeter precision!

SA 1 Why is it necessary to have standard units of measurement? (3 Marks)

Standard units are essential because:

  1. Eliminates Personal Variation: Body units like cubits and strides differ between individuals, causing disputes and confusion.
  2. Facilitates Global Trade & Commerce: A merchant buying 1,000 metres of fabric in India and selling it in Europe receives the exact same length.
  3. Ensures Scientific Uniformity: Scientists worldwide can reproduce experiments and verify each other's data accurately using uniform SI units.

Definition: Parallax error is an error in scale reading that occurs when the observer's eye is positioned at an oblique angle to the scale mark rather than looking straight down.

Precaution: Always keep the eye positioned vertically directly above the point where the measurement is being taken.

Long Answer Questions (5 Marks)

LA 1 Explain the complete step-by-step procedure to measure the length of an irregular curved line using a thread. (5 Marks)

Aim: To accurately measure the length of an irregular curved line AB drawn on a sheet of paper.

Apparatus: A sheet of paper with curved line AB, a non-stretchable cotton thread, an ink marker pen, and a standard 30-cm ruler.

Step-by-step Procedure:

  1. Tie a knot: Tie a tiny knot at one extreme end of the thread to act as the reference zero mark (Mark A).
  2. Placement: Place the knot exactly over Point A of the curved line on paper.
  3. Segment tracing: Hold the knot firmly with your left thumb. With your right hand, lay a small 1 to 2 cm segment of the thread along the curvature of the line.
  4. Progressive pressing: Move your left finger to hold the new contact point, and gently lay down the next small segment along the curve. Repeat this carefully along the entire curved boundary until you reach Point B.
  5. Marking endpoint: With the marker pen, put a clear ink dot on the thread exactly at Point B.
  6. Measurement on ruler: Remove the thread and straighten it out along the standard 30-cm ruler, placing the knot at the 0 cm mark. Note the reading corresponding to the ink dot.

Precaution: The thread must never be stretched during tracing or measuring, as elastic stretching would give an artificially short reading.

LA 2 "Motion in real life is often complex and combines multiple types of motion." Justify this statement with three detailed real-life examples. (5 Marks)

In nature and everyday technology, bodies rarely move in a single isolated motion mode. Often, two or more types occur simultaneously:

  1. A Moving Bicycle on a Straight Road:
    • The wheels rotate around their central axles → Circular (Rotational) Motion.
    • The pedals move in circles around the chain wheel → Circular Motion.
    • The rider and the entire bicycle frame move forward along the road → Linear (Rectilinear) Motion.
  2. A Rolling Football / Cricket Ball:
    • The ball continuously spins about its central diameter axis → Rotational Motion.
    • Simultaneously, the center of mass of the ball travels across the ground → Linear Motion.
  3. The Earth in Space:
    • Earth rotates once every 24 hours around its own geographic axis → Rotational Motion (causing day and night).
    • Earth travels in a large elliptical orbit around the Sun once every 365.25 days → Circular / Orbital Periodic Motion (causing seasons).
LA 3 Describe the three major rules and precautions to follow while taking length measurements with a standard ruler. (5 Marks)

To avoid errors and obtain scientifically exact measurements, follow these three fundamental rules:

  1. Ruler Placement along the Object:
    • The ruler must be laid directly touching and strictly parallel to the edge being measured.
    • If the ruler is placed diagonally or slanted, the measured distance will be longer than the true edge length.
  2. Handling Broken Ends and Damaged Zero Marks:
    • Never guess the position of a chipped zero mark.
    • Start measurement from another prominent integer mark (such as 1.0 cm or 2.0 cm).
    • Calculate true length by subtracting the starting mark: True Length = Final Reading − Initial Mark.
  3. Correct Perpendicular Eye Placement (Avoiding Parallax):
    • The observer's eye must be positioned vertically right above the point where the measurement is being taken.
    • Viewing from the left causes an apparent shift towards a lower value; viewing from the right shifts it to a higher value.

📜 Case Study 1: The Tailor and the Carpenter

Context: Master Ramesh is a traditional carpenter who builds custom wooden dining tables, while Master Salman is a tailor who stitches school uniforms. During a community project, Ramesh used a 3-metre folding steel rule to measure plywood sheets. Meanwhile, Salman used a 1.5-metre flexible vinyl measuring tape to measure Deepa’s shoulder width and waist circumference. A young apprentice suggested replacing both tools with a single stretchy 2-metre elastic rubber strip to make the kit lighter and more versatile.

Questions:

  1. Why did Ramesh choose a rigid steel ruler rather than a flexible cloth tape for cutting plywood?
  2. Why did Salman prefer a flexible vinyl tape instead of a rigid steel metre rod for measuring waist circumference?
  3. Critically evaluate the apprentice's idea of replacing both tools with a stretchable rubber strip.
View Model Answers
  1. Plywood has straight, rigid edges. A rigid steel rule ensures perfectly straight lines and exact millimeter precision without bending or sagging.
  2. A human waist is curved and irregular. A rigid steel rod cannot bend around curves, whereas a flexible tape hugs the contour of the body smoothly without deformation.
  3. The apprentice's idea is scientifically flawed. A stretchable rubber strip will stretch under varying tension. Pulling it tightly will space the marks farther apart, giving false and wildly inconsistent readings. Measuring instruments must always be made of dimensionally stable materials!

🚨 Section 6: Exam Hall Mistakes & 15 Rapid Revision Points

❌ 5 Common Exam Hall Mistakes to Avoid

  • Mistake 1: Forgetting to subtract the broken zero mark: When starting from 1.0 cm, students frequently write down the final mark (e.g. 9.4 cm) as the answer instead of calculating 9.4 − 1.0 = 8.4 cm!
  • Mistake 2: Confusing 1 km with 100 m or 100 cm: Remember that 1 km = 1,000 m. Always double check powers of 10 during conversions!
  • Mistake 3: Stating motion without specifying the reference point: Saying an object is simply "at rest" or "in motion" without stating the reference frame loses marks in CBSE exams.
  • Mistake 4: Confusing Circular Motion with Rotational Motion: A point on the blade moves in a circle around the center (circular motion), while the fan as a whole spins about its central axis (rotational motion).
  • Mistake 5: Using stretchable string to measure curves: If the string stretches while tracing a curve, the resulting measurement on the ruler will be incorrect.

🚀 15 Ultra-Crisp Rapid Revision One-Liners

  1. Measurement is comparing an unknown quantity with a known standard quantity called a unit.
  2. Ancient units like handspan, cubit, foot, and stride vary from person to person and are non-standard.
  3. Angula, dhanusa, and yojana were ancient Indian units of measurement.
  4. The SI unit of length is the metre, designated by the lowercase symbol m.
  5. Standard conversions: 1 km = 1,000 m, 1 m = 100 cm, and 1 cm = 10 mm.
  6. On a standard 15-cm school ruler, the smallest measurable value (least count) is 1 mm (0.1 cm).
  7. To measure correctly, the scale must be kept strictly in contact along the edge of the object.
  8. If the zero mark is broken, start from an integer like 1.0 cm and subtract it from the final reading.
  9. Parallax error is avoided by keeping the eye vertically directly above the scale reading.
  10. The length of a curved line is measured using a non-stretchable thread and a straight ruler.
  11. A reference point is a stationary point chosen to describe the position or motion of an object.
  12. An object is in motion if its position changes relative to a reference point over time.
  13. Motion along a straight line path is called linear (rectilinear) motion.
  14. Motion along a circular circumference at a fixed distance from the center is called circular motion.
  15. Back-and-forth movement about a fixed rest position is called oscillatory motion.
