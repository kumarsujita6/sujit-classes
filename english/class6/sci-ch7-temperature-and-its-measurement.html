7.1 Hot or Cold? (The Fallacy of the Human Sense of Touch)

In our daily lives, we constantly describe objects as hot or cold — ice cream is cold, while freshly boiled milk is hot. But can we reliably tell how hot or how cold something is simply by touching it with our fingers?

🧪 Activity 7.1: The Three Bowls Experiment (Investigating Touch)

Setup: Take three bowls: Bowl A filled with ice-cold water, Bowl B filled with lukewarm water, and Bowl C filled with warm water (not too hot to burn fingers).

  1. Dip your left hand into cold water (Bowl A) and your right hand into warm water (Bowl C) for 2 to 3 minutes.
  2. Now, remove both hands simultaneously and plunge them together into the middle bowl of lukewarm water (Bowl B).

Observation: Your left hand feels that the lukewarm water is warm, while your right hand feels that the exact same water is cold!

Scientific Conclusion: The exact same bowl of water produces two conflicting sensations simultaneously! Our sense of touch is relative and subjective — it depends on what temperature our skin was exposed to immediately beforehand. Therefore, the sense of touch is not a reliable scientific measure of hotness or coldness.

7.2 What is Temperature?

To measure hotness or coldness reliably and objectively, science defines a specific physical quantity:

🌡️ Scientific Definition of Temperature

Temperature is the reliable, quantitative measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of an object or body.

  • A higher temperature indicates that a body is hotter.
  • A lower temperature indicates that a body is colder.
  • The device used to measure temperature accurately is called a thermometer (derived from Greek therme meaning heat, and metron meaning measure).

Temperature Scales and Units:

  • Degree Celsius (°C): Designed by Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius. On this scale, pure water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C under normal atmospheric pressure. It is written as °C (degree Celsius).
  • Degree Fahrenheit (°F): Designed by Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit. Pure water freezes at 32 °F and boils at 212 °F. Common clinical thermometers often display the Fahrenheit scale.
  • Kelvin (K): The SI base unit of thermodynamic temperature used in advanced scientific research. It has no negative values; 0 K is called absolute zero (−273.15 °C), where all atomic motion stops completely.
  • Normal Human Body Temperature: In a healthy human being, average body temperature is exactly 37.0 °C, which corresponds to 98.6 °F.

7.3 Types of Thermometers

Different situations require different temperature ranges and structural features. The two primary types studied in Grade 6 are:

7.3.1 Clinical Thermometer

A clinical thermometer is designed exclusively for measuring human body temperature.

  • Range: It has a narrow range from 35 °C to 42 °C (or 94 °F to 108 °F). Why? Because human body temperature never drops below 35 °C or rises above 42 °C while alive.
  • The Kink (Constriction): The defining anatomical feature of a clinical thermometer is a narrow, bent constriction or kink located in the capillary tube just above the bulb.
    • Function of the Kink: When the thermometer is placed under the tongue or in the armpit, mercury expands and pushes past the kink. When removed from the body into cooler room air, the liquid contracts. The kink prevents the mercury thread from falling back into the bulb on its own! This allows doctors and parents to read the temperature leisurely and accurately.
    • Shaking Down: Before taking the next measurement, the thermometer must be given a few sharp jerks (flicks) to force the mercury thread back down below the 35 °C mark.
  • Digital Clinical Thermometers: Traditional thermometers contain mercury, a toxic heavy metal that is dangerous if the glass tube breaks in a child's mouth and is difficult to dispose of safely. Modern digital thermometers use an electronic heat sensor (thermistor) to display temperature clearly on an LCD screen, completely mercury-free and shatterproof!
Figure 7.2: Structural Comparison — The crucial presence of the kink in a clinical thermometer vs the straight capillary in a laboratory thermometer.

7.3.2 Laboratory Thermometer

A laboratory thermometer is used in science experiments to measure the temperature of boiling water, chemical solutions, hot oil, and weather air.

  • Range: Typically spans from −10 °C to 110 °C.
  • Absence of Kink: A laboratory thermometer does NOT have a kink.
    Consequence: As soon as the thermometer is lifted out of the hot water into the room, its liquid level starts dropping immediately due to room temperature cooling. Therefore, the reading of a laboratory thermometer must be recorded while the bulb is still submerged in the liquid!
  • Precautions for Using a Laboratory Thermometer:
    1. Keep the thermometer strictly vertical (do not tilt).
    2. The bulb must be completely surrounded by the liquid substance being measured.
    3. The bulb must NOT touch the bottom or sides of the glass container (otherwise it measures the temperature of the heated glass rather than the liquid!).
    4. Position your eye horizontally at the level of the liquid meniscus to avoid parallax error.

7.4 How to Calculate the Least Count of a Thermometer

The least count is the smallest temperature change that can be measured accurately using a specific thermometer.

🧠 Universal Least Count Formula

Least Count = (Difference between two major marks) ÷ (Number of small divisions between them)

  • Example 1: On a lab thermometer, the difference between 0 °C and 10 °C is 10 °C. If there are 10 small divisions between them:
    Least Count = 10 ÷ 10 = 1 °C. Each small mark represents 1 °C.
  • Example 2: If there are 20 small divisions between 0 °C and 10 °C:
    Least Count = 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5 °C. Each small mark represents 0.5 °C.
  • Example 3 (Clinical Thermometer): The difference between 36 °C and 37 °C is 1 °C. If there are 10 small divisions:
    Least Count = 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1 °C. Each small tick represents 0.1 °C!

7.5 Maximum-Minimum Thermometer and Weather Air Temperature

Every evening, weather forecasts report the "Maximum temperature today was 38 °C and minimum temperature was 24 °C". How is this recorded continuously over 24 hours?

  • Meteorologists use a specialised U-shaped thermometer called the Maximum and Minimum Thermometer (often called Six's Thermometer).
  • It has small steel indicator dumbbells pushed by mercury. The maximum dumbbell stays at the highest temperature reached during the heat of the afternoon, while the minimum dumbbell records the lowest dip recorded in the early morning before sunrise.

Exercise 8 Why is a laboratory thermometer NOT used to measure human body temperature?

A laboratory thermometer cannot be used to measure human body temperature for two critical reasons:

  1. Absence of a Kink: A laboratory thermometer does not have a constriction (kink) above the bulb. As soon as it is removed from the mouth or armpit into cooler room air, the liquid level immediately drops down, giving an inaccurate, lower reading.
  2. Safety & Fragility: Laboratory thermometers are long and made of fragile thin glass containing toxic mercury. Putting it in a child's mouth poses a severe risk of breakage and poisoning.

Exercise 12 A lab thermometer has 50 divisions between 0 °C and 100 °C. What does each division measure?

Given:

  • Total temperature span = 100 °C − 0 °C = 100 °C.
  • Total number of divisions = 50.

Calculation:
Value of 1 division = 100 °C ÷ 50 = 2.0 °C.

Conclusion: Each division of this thermometer measures exactly 2 °C.

  • Total divisions required = 10 ÷ 0.5 = 20 divisions.

Exercise 14 Komal tells you she has a fever of 101 degrees. Does she mean Celsius or Fahrenheit?

Answer: She means the Fahrenheit scale (101 °F).

Scientific Proof:

  • Water boils at 100 °C. If her body were 101 °C, human blood would literally vaporize — a living human being cannot survive above 43 °C!
  • On the Fahrenheit scale, normal body temperature is 98.6 °F. A reading of 101 °F indicates a moderate fever (equivalent to approximately 38.3 °C), which is standard clinical terminology in India and worldwide.
Learning Further 1 Animal Body Temperatures: How are they measured?

Veterinary doctors use specialised digital or flexible rectal clinical thermometers:

  • Cat: ~38.1 °C to 39.2 °C
  • Dog: ~38.3 °C to 39.2 °C
  • Horse: ~37.2 °C to 38.3 °C
  • Cow & Buffalo: ~38.5 °C
  • Camel: Remarkably fluctuates between 34 °C in cool mornings and 41 °C in hot desert afternoons to conserve body water by avoiding sweating!
Learning Further 2 Coldest and Hottest Places in India
  • Coldest Inhabited Place in India: Dras in Ladakh (often called the "Gateway to Ladakh"). Winter temperatures plunge as low as −45 °C to −60 °C!
  • Hottest Recorded Place in India: Phalodi in Rajasthan, where summer temperatures have reached an all-time national record of 51.0 °C.
Learning Further 3 Solar System Mystery: Why is Venus hotter than Mercury?

Although Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun (58 million km), Venus (108 million km) is the hottest planet in the Solar System with a surface temperature of 465 °C!

Reason: Mercury has no atmosphere to trap heat. Venus has an ultra-thick atmosphere composed of 96% carbon dioxide (CO₂) with thick clouds of sulfuric acid, creating a runaway greenhouse effect that traps solar heat like a super-pressure cooker!

Long Answer Questions (5 Marks)

LA 1 Describe the complete step-by-step procedure to measure the boiling temperature of water using a laboratory thermometer. (5 Marks)

Aim: To accurately measure the boiling point of pure water in a laboratory setup.

Apparatus: Beaker half-filled with water, laboratory thermometer (−10 °C to 110 °C), iron stand with clamp, tripod stand, wire gauze, and Bunsen burner/spirit lamp.

Procedure:

  1. Mount the laboratory thermometer vertically on the iron stand clamp.
  2. Place the beaker containing water on the wire gauze over the tripod stand.
  3. Lower the thermometer into the beaker so that its bulb is completely immersed in water, ensuring the bulb is suspended freely without touching the bottom or sides of the beaker.
  4. Light the burner and heat the water gently.
  5. Observe the rising liquid column in the thermometer. Note the temperature every 2 minutes.
  6. Continue heating until water begins to boil vigorously with bubbling steam.
  7. Keep observing the liquid column while water boils. Notice that the reading becomes stationary.
  8. Position your eye horizontally at the level of the liquid meniscus and note down the steady reading.

Observation & Conclusion: The reading remains constant at 100.0 °C while water boils, proving that pure water boils at 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure.

LA 2 Explain the working of a Maximum-Minimum Thermometer and its importance in weather forecasting. (5 Marks)

Principle & Construction:

  • Weather air temperature fluctuates continuously between day and night. A Maximum-Minimum thermometer (Six's Thermometer) automatically registers the highest and lowest temperatures reached during a 24-hour cycle.
  • It consists of a U-shaped glass tube containing alcohol in the bulbs and a mercury thread in the bend.
  • Resting on top of the mercury in both limbs are two lightweight steel index markers (dumbbells).

Working Mechanism:

  1. During the Day (Maximum Temp): As the air heats up in the afternoon, the alcohol in the main bulb expands, pushing the mercury column and the steel marker up the right limb. When cooler air arrives in the evening, the mercury retreats, but the steel marker remains stuck at the highest peak due to a tiny friction spring.
  2. During the Night (Minimum Temp): As air chills in the early hours before sunrise, the alcohol contracts, pulling the mercury up the left limb and pushing the minimum index marker. When the morning warms up, the marker stays at the lowest point.
  3. Resetting: A meteorologist reads both scales and resets the markers against the mercury using a small external magnet.

Significance: Essential for meteorology, agriculture (frost warnings for farmers), and aviation planning.

LA 3 Why is temperature considered a more reliable measure than the human sense of touch? Explain with the help of Activity 7.1. (5 Marks)

Flaws of the Sense of Touch:

  • The human skin does not possess an internal numerical thermometer. Instead, nerve thermoreceptors only sense the rate of heat transfer relative to the skin's current temperature.
  • In Activity 7.1, when the left hand was pre-conditioned in cold water (Bowl A) and moved to lukewarm water (Bowl B), heat flowed into the cold hand, signaling "warm". Simultaneously, the right hand was pre-conditioned in warm water (Bowl C); when plunged into Bowl B, heat flowed out of the warm hand, signaling "cold".
  • Thus, the exact same system at the exact same moment gave two contradictory results to the same human brain!

Superiority of Temperature & Thermometers:

  • Thermometers utilize fixed physical properties of matter that change reproducibly with heat — such as the linear thermal expansion of mercury/alcohol or electrical resistance of semiconductors.
  • These physical changes are independent of human perception, objective, universally repeatable, and measurable in standardized units (°C, °F, K).

  • Mistake 1: Confusing 37 °C with 98.6 °C: Normal body temperature is 37 °C or 98.6 °F. Writing 98.6 °C in exam answers loses marks immediately!
  • Mistake 2: Stating that laboratory thermometers have a kink: Only clinical thermometers have a kink! Lab thermometers have a completely smooth capillary.

  • Mistake 5: Forgetting to jerk a clinical thermometer before reuse: If you don't jerk it down below 35 °C, it will merely display the previous patient's reading!
