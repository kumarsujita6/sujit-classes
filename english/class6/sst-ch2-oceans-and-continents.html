TOPIC 1: Introduction – The 'Blue Planet'
"The ocean is everything. It covers seven-tenths of the terrestrial globe. Its breath is pure and healthy. It is an immense desert, where man is never lonely, for he feels life stirring all around. ... The ocean is the vast reservoir of Nature. The globe began with the ocean, so to speak, and who knows if it will not end with it. ..."
— Jules Verne (1870)
  • Earth Seen from Space (Fig. 2.1): The photograph of the Earth in the chapter was taken by the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. The view is centred on the Indian Ocean, with Africa to the left, India and part of Asia at the top, Australia to the right, and Antarctica at the bottom.
  • Why Earth is Called the 'Blue Planet':
    • When we rotate a globe gently or look at a picture of the Earth seen from the Moon or outer space, the most widespread colour visible is blue.
    • Blue represents water. Almost three-fourths (≈ 3/4 or 75%, or seven-tenths as noted by Jules Verne) of the Earth's surface is covered with water.
    • Because it appears mostly blue when seen from outer space, early astronauts lovingly called the Earth the 'blue planet'.
  • Land on the Globe: The second colour visible in the picture of the Earth (Fig. 2.1) is brown, which is the colour of land. Land covers a little over one-fourth (≈ 1/4 or 25%) of the globe.
  • Vital Role of Oceans and Continents: Both oceans and continents play a vital role in shaping the climate of the Earth. They affect all aspects of life, including all plants and animals, and therefore, human life too. We see their impact throughout our history and culture, and in our daily lives.
📘 Key Definitions
  • Oceans: The largest water bodies we see on the globe are called 'oceans'.
  • Landmass: A large body of land is called a 'landmass'.
  • Continent: A large continuous expanse of land is called a 'continent'.
🧪 In-Box Info Decoded ("DON'T MISS OUT" - Page 28): The Emblem of the Indian Navy
  • 🧪 Concept / Setup: The emblem of the Indian Navy features the Lion Capital of Ashoka above an anchor, containing the motto शं नो वरुणः (Śaṃ no Varuṇaḥ, pronounced 'Sham no Varunah').
  • 👀 Observation / Meaning: The motto means, "Be auspicious to us, O Varuṇa."
  • 💡 Conclusion / Reason: This is an invocation to Varuṇa, a Vedic deity associated with the oceans, the sky, and water in general. It demonstrates how deeply the ocean is woven into Indian heritage and maritime defence.
TOPIC 2: The Distribution of Water and Land on the Earth
  • Unequal Distribution: Oceans and continents are not distributed equally between the Northern Hemisphere and the Southern Hemisphere.
  • Smaller Extensions of Oceans: On maps (Fig. 2.2), the blue areas consist of oceans along with their smaller extensions, which have various names — 'sea', 'bay', 'gulf', etc.
  • 🔥 Salty Seawater vs. Freshwater:
    • The oceans together hold most of the water available on the planet.
    • However, this seawater is salty and unfit for consumption by most land animals, including humans.
    • On the other hand, freshwater makes up a very small proportion of the planet's water resources.
    • Sources of Freshwater: It is found in glaciers, rivers, lakes, in the atmosphere, and also underground.
📘 Key Definition

Groundwater: Freshwater that is found underground is called 'groundwater'.

TOPIC 3: Oceans
  • The Five Oceans: On the world map (Fig. 2.3 on page 32), we can observe five oceans — the Pacific Ocean, the Atlantic Ocean, the Indian Ocean, the Arctic Ocean, and the Southern (or Antarctic) Ocean.
  • Conventional Boundaries:
    • Although we list five oceans, it is clear from the map that they are not really separate.
    • The lines that divide them on the map are no more than conventions — the natural world does not follow such boundaries.
    • Seawater constantly flows across different oceans, sustaining a rich diversity of marine life. Many plant and animal species can be found across multiple oceans.
  • Marine Flora and Fauna:
    • Marine Flora: Includes tiny plants called algae and all kinds of seaweeds.
    • Marine Fauna: Consists of thousands of species of colourful fish, dolphins, whales, and countless mysterious deep-sea creatures.
    • NCERT Visual Examples (Page 30 "Ocean Life" Collage): Coral reef, A star fish on a sea anemone, Shark, Dolphins, Sperm whale (mother and baby), Algae, Emperor penguins, Shallow coral reef with colourful tropical fish, and Bonaire sea turtle.
    • Each part of the ocean, from the sun-lit surface to the dark depths, has its own diverse life forms.
  • 🔥 Size Ranking of the Five Oceans (Largest to Smallest):
    1. Pacific Ocean (Largest of all)
    2. Atlantic Ocean (Second largest)
    3. Indian Ocean (Third largest)
    4. Southern (or Antarctic) Ocean (Fourth largest)
    5. Arctic Ocean (Smallest)
📘 Key Definitions (Page 31 Margin Box)
  • Marine: Related to or found in the oceans and seas.
  • Flora: The plant life of a particular region or period of time.
  • Fauna: The animal life of a particular region or period of time.

TOPIC 4: Oceans and Disasters
  • Clouds and Monsoon Rains:
    • In the picture of the Earth at the start of the chapter (Fig. 2.1), the white shapes across the globe are large masses of clouds.
    • Such clouds bring rain to the continents; for instance, the monsoon rains we in India expect every summer originate in the ocean — without such rains, our agriculture and all life will suffer.
  • Storms and Cyclones: Oceans often also give rise to storms — violent events with extreme rainfall or very strong winds, such as cyclones, which can cause widespread damage to coastal regions of the world.
  • 🔥 Tsunamis:
    • A tsunami is another natural disaster that originates in the ocean.
    • It is a huge and powerful wave generally caused by a strong earthquake or a volcanic eruption at the bottom of the ocean.
    • Tsunamis can travel thousands of kilometres and submerge coastal areas, causing widespread damage.
📘 Key Definitions
  • Cyclones / Storms: Violent events originating in oceans with extreme rainfall or very strong winds that cause widespread damage to coastal regions.
  • Tsunami: A huge and powerful wave generally caused by a strong earthquake or a volcanic eruption at the bottom of the ocean.
🧪 In-Box Info Decoded ("DON'T MISS OUT" - Page 34): The 26 December 2004 Tsunami & NDMA
  • 🧪 Event / Setup: On 26 December 2004, India and another 13 countries around the Indian Ocean were struck by a powerful tsunami caused by an earthquake in Indonesia.
  • 👀 Observation (Impact):
    • More than two lakh people lost their lives.
    • In India, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the coasts of Tamil Nadu and Kerala were severely affected and suffered much damage and loss of life.
  • 💡 Conclusion / Reason (Early Warning & Disaster Management):
    • Such tsunamis are rare but very destructive; luckily, they can often be detected before they hit a coast.
    • Many countries collaborate in such 'early warning systems'. In particular, there is an Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System, to which many countries, including India, contribute to protect lives and property.
    • Events that lead to loss of life and property are handled under disaster management. India has its own 'National Disaster Management Authority' (NDMA) to deal with all kinds of disasters.
TOPIC 5: Continents
  • 🔥 Why Continents Can Be Counted Between Four and Seven:
    • Depending on our choice, we may list any number of continents between four and seven:
    • North America and South America: Generally considered to be two continents; but if seen as a single landmass, they can also be considered as one (America).
    • Europe and Asia ('Eurasia'): Generally considered as two continents, although the map makes it clear that they form a single landmass. For historical and cultural reasons, Europe's evolution has been very different from Asia's, which is why they can be seen as two continents. Geologists, however, often regard them as a single continent called 'Eurasia'.
    • Africa and Eurasia: Generally regarded as two continents, but sometimes as one (Africa-Eurasia).
    • Most Widely Used List: In practice, the list of seven continents is the one most widely adopted and used.

TOPIC 6: Islands
  • What is an Island?
    • Continents do not include all landmass. Some smaller pieces of land are left out; surrounded by water on all sides, they are called islands.
    • ⚠️ Important Distinction: Continents are also surrounded by water, but because they are so large, they are not considered islands.
  • Global & Indian Islands:
    • There are lakhs of islands on the planet, of very different sizes.
    • 🔥 Greenland: The largest island in the world. You would have to add the areas of the 10 largest states of India to reach its size.
    • 🔥 Islands of India: India has more than 1,300 small islands! Those include two major groups — Andaman and Nicobar Islands in the Bay of Bengal and Lakshadweep Islands in the Arabian Sea (see Fig. 2.4).
  • 🔥 Indian Antarctica Programme & 'Dakshin Gangotri':
    • Since 1981, the Indian Antarctica Programme has been exploring Antarctica, a continent with a very cold climate and harsh environment (mostly ice, as seen in the white expanse at the bottom of Fig. 2.1).
    • In 1983, India established its first scientific base station there, called 'Dakshin Gangotri' (two more bases were established later).
    • About 40 teams of Indian scientists have conducted research in this faraway region, especially on the evolution of climate and environment.
    • The settlement where the scientists live has a library and even a post office!
📘 Key Definition

Island: A smaller piece of land (not a continent) surrounded by water on all sides is called an island.

TOPIC 7: Oceans and Life
  • Oceans and the Water Cycle:
    • Oceans and continents are vital parts of the environment and affect most aspects of our lives, even if we do not notice it.
    • Oceans send rain to the continents; this is part of the Earth's water cycle.
    • Without oceans, there would be no rainfall! The Earth would be a desert.
  • 🔥 Why Oceans are Called 'The Planet's Lungs':
    • More than half (>50%) of the world's oxygen is produced by the oceans' flora, which is why they are called 'the planet's lungs'.
    • The oceans, therefore, play a crucial role in regulating the climate and sustaining life on Earth.
  • Impact on Human History and Culture:
    • From early times, people have used oceans and seas to migrate to other regions, to trade in all kinds of goods, to conduct military campaigns, and as a source of food through fishing.
    • Oceans have also nourished the cultures of coastal people all over the world. Almost all of them have tales and legends about the sea, sea gods and goddesses, sea monsters, and treasures from the sea — the oceans' dangers but also their blessings.
  • 🔥 Human Threats to the Marine Environment:
    • Marine Pollution: Scientific studies have shown how the oceans are polluted by human activity — we throw several million tonnes of plastic waste into the oceans every year, choking marine life. There are several other forms of pollution.
    • Overfishing: Overfishing (excessive fishing) is another cause for the decline of marine life.
    • Collective Responsibility: It is our collective responsibility to protect oceans for the future of the planet and of humanity.

Part A: "The Big Questions" (Page 27)

Big Question 1: What are oceans and continents? What are their names and their distribution?
View CBSE Topper-Style Answer
  • Oceans: The largest water bodies we see on the globe are called oceans, covering almost three-fourths of the Earth's surface. The five oceans (from largest to smallest) are:
    1. Pacific Ocean
    2. Atlantic Ocean
    3. Indian Ocean
    4. Southern (or Antarctic) Ocean
    5. Arctic Ocean
  • Continents: Large continuous expanses of land are called continents, covering a little over one-fourth of the globe. In the widely used count of seven continents (from largest to smallest), they are: Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, and Australia.
  • Distribution: Oceans and continents are not distributed equally between the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. The Northern Hemisphere has more land than the Southern Hemisphere, while the Southern Hemisphere holds more water.
Big Question 2: In what ways do oceans and continents impact life on Earth, including human life?
View CBSE Topper-Style Answer
  • Water Cycle & Rainfall: Oceans send rain (such as India's summer monsoon rains) to the continents as part of the Earth's water cycle. Without oceans, there would be no rainfall and the Earth would be a desert.
  • Oxygen Production ('The Planet's Lungs'): More than half of the world's oxygen is produced by the oceans' flora (algae and seaweeds), sustaining life on Earth.
  • Biodiversity & Habitats: Oceans sustain diverse marine flora and fauna, while continents support terrestrial ecosystems and human settlements.
  • Human History, Trade & Culture: Since early times, humans have used oceans to migrate, trade, conduct military campaigns, and obtain food through fishing. They also shape coastal cultures, legends, and deities.
  • Natural Disasters: Oceans also generate extreme weather events like cyclones and destructive waves like tsunamis that impact coastal life.

Q1. Explain the following terms: (a) Continent (b) Ocean (c) Island
View CBSE Topper-Style Answer
  • (a) Continent:
    • Definition: A large continuous expanse of land on the Earth is called a continent.
    • Key Features: Continents cover a little over one-fourth of the globe. Because they are so large, they are not considered islands even though they are surrounded by water.
    • Examples: The seven continents are Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, and Australia.
  • (b) Ocean:
    • Definition: The largest water bodies we see on the globe are called oceans.

  • (c) Island:
    • Definition: A smaller piece of land (smaller than a continent) surrounded by water on all sides is called an island.
    • Key Features & Examples: There are lakhs of islands on Earth. Greenland is the largest island in the world, while India has over 1,300 small islands, including the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep Islands.

Q1. Look at the statements below regarding the distribution and nature of water on Earth and identify the INCORRECT statement:

(a) Almost three-fourths of the Earth's surface is covered with water, making it appear blue from space.
(b) The Northern Hemisphere holds a greater proportion of water than the Southern Hemisphere.
(c) The boundaries dividing the five oceans on a world map are merely human conventions.
(d) Freshwater makes up a very small proportion of the planet's water and is found in glaciers, rivers, lakes, the atmosphere, and as groundwater.

Show Answer & Explanation

✅ Answer: (b) The Northern Hemisphere holds a greater proportion of water than the Southern Hemisphere.

💡 Explanation: As seen in the polar maps (Fig. 2.2), the Southern Hemisphere holds more water, whereas the Northern Hemisphere has more land than the Southern Hemisphere.

3. Very Short Answer Questions (2 Marks Each – 5 Questions)

High Frequency PYQ Concept Q1. What is the motto of the Indian Navy? Explain its meaning and significance.
View Answer
  • Motto: The emblem of the Indian Navy contains the motto Sam noh Varunah (pronounced 'Sham no Varunah'), which means, "Be auspicious to us, O Varuṇa."
  • Significance: It is an invocation to Varuṇa, a Vedic deity associated with the oceans, the sky, and water in general.
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q2. Differentiate between 'Marine Flora' and 'Marine Fauna' with examples from the chapter.
View Answer
  • Marine Flora: Refers to the plant life found in the oceans and seas, such as tiny plants called algae and all kinds of seaweeds.
  • Marine Fauna: Refers to the animal life found in the oceans and seas, such as thousands of species of colourful fish, dolphins, whales, sharks, starfish, and sea turtles.
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q3. State the main geographical limits of the Indian Ocean and name its two parts on either side of India.
View Answer
  • Main Limits: The main limits of the Indian Ocean are Asia to the north, Africa to the west, Australia to the east, and the Southern Ocean in the south.
  • Parts on Either Side of India: The Arabian Sea to the west and the Bay of Bengal to the east.
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q4. Why are continents not considered islands despite being surrounded by water? Name the largest island in the world.
View Answer
  • Reason: Continents are also surrounded by water, but because they are so large (large continuous expanses of land), they are not considered islands.
  • Largest Island: Greenland is the largest island in the world (equal in area to the 10 largest states of India combined).
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q5. Why is June 8 celebrated as World Oceans Day, and what are the two main human activities threatening marine life?
View Answer
  • World Oceans Day (June 8): Designated by the United Nations to remind us of the major role the ocean plays in everyday life as the lungs of our planet, a major source of food and medicine, and a critical part of the biosphere.
  • Two Human Threats: (1) Dumping several million tonnes of plastic waste into the oceans every year, choking marine life, and (2) Overfishing (excessive fishing).

4. Short Answer Questions (3 Marks Each – 5 Questions)

High Frequency PYQ Concept Q1. What is a tsunami? How is it caused, and how do nations collaborate to protect lives from it?
View Answer
  1. Definition & Cause: A tsunami is a huge and powerful wave originating in the ocean, generally caused by a strong earthquake or a volcanic eruption at the bottom of the ocean.
  2. Impact: Tsunamis can travel thousands of kilometres and submerge coastal areas, causing widespread damage (such as the 26 December 2004 tsunami triggered by an earthquake in Indonesia).
  3. Early Warning & Management: Tsunamis can often be detected before hitting a coast through collaborative 'early warning systems' like the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System (to which India contributes) and managed by India's National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA).
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q2. Explain why the count of continents can vary between four and seven depending on our choice.
View Answer
  • North and South America: Generally considered two continents, but if seen as a single landmass, they can be counted as one ('America').
  • Europe and Asia ('Eurasia'): Form a single landmass and are regarded by geologists as one continent called 'Eurasia', though for historical and cultural reasons they are commonly counted as two.
  • Africa and Eurasia: Generally regarded as two continents, but sometimes combined as one ('Africa-Eurasia'). Thus, depending on how we group them, we get 4, 5, 6, or 7 continents (with 7 being the most widely adopted).
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q3. Write a short note on India's island groups and the Indian Antarctica Programme.
View Answer
  • India's Islands: India has more than 1,300 small islands, including two major groups — the Andaman and Nicobar Islands in the Bay of Bengal and the Lakshadweep Islands in the Arabian Sea.
  • Indian Antarctica Programme: Since 1981, India has been exploring Antarctica's harsh, icy environment.
  • Dakshin Gangotri: In 1983, India established its first scientific base station there called 'Dakshin Gangotri' (and two more bases later), where about 40 teams of Indian scientists have researched climate and environment evolution in a settlement that has a library and a post office.
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q4. Arrange the seven continents in ascending order (smallest to largest) and compare the size of 'Eurasia' with South America using the chapter's square-grid activity.
View Answer
  • Smallest to Largest (Ascending Order): Australia (8 squares) → Europe (10) → Antarctica (13) → South America (18) → North America (24) → Africa (30) → Asia (44, Largest).
  • Eurasia vs. South America: Combining Europe (10 squares) and Asia (44 squares) into Eurasia gives 54 squares, which is exactly three times (3×) the size of South America (18 squares).
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q5. How have oceans impacted humanity historically, economically, and culturally from early times?
View Answer
  1. Migration & Trade: People have used oceans and seas since early times to migrate to other regions and trade in all kinds of goods.
  2. Food & Military Campaigns: Oceans have been used to conduct military campaigns and as a vital source of food through fishing.
  3. Cultural Impact: Oceans have nourished coastal cultures worldwide with tales and legends about the sea, sea gods and goddesses (like the Vedic deity Varuṇa), sea monsters, and treasures.

5. Long Answer Questions (5 Marks Each – 3 Questions with Marking Scheme)

LA Q1. "Oceans play a crucial role in regulating the climate and sustaining life on Earth, yet the marine environment is under severe threat from human activity." Explain this statement in detail.
View 5-Mark Step-by-Step Answer
  • 1. Role in Water Cycle & Rainfall (1 Mark): Oceans form clouds that send rain (such as India's summer monsoon rains) to the continents as part of the Earth's water cycle. Without oceans, there would be no rainfall and the Earth would be a desert.
  • 2. 'The Planet's Lungs' (1 Mark): More than half (>50%) of the world's oxygen is produced by the oceans' flora (algae and seaweeds), earning them the title 'the planet's lungs'.
  • 3. Sustaining Biodiversity & Humanity (1 Mark): Oceans support vast marine flora and fauna and serve humanity as a major source of food (fishing), medicine, migration, and trade.
  • 4. Threats from Pollution & Overfishing (1 Mark): Humans throw several million tonnes of plastic waste into the oceans every year, choking marine life, alongside other forms of pollution and overfishing (excessive fishing).
  • 5. Collective Responsibility & World Oceans Day (1 Mark): The UN designated June 8 as World Oceans Day to remind us of the ocean's vital role; it is our collective responsibility to protect oceans for the future of the planet and humanity.
LA Q2. Describe the five oceans of the world, their conventional boundaries, their ranking by size, and the special geographical features of the Indian Ocean.
View 5-Mark Step-by-Step Answer
  • 1. The Five Oceans (1 Mark): The five oceans are the Pacific Ocean, Atlantic Ocean, Indian Ocean, Southern (or Antarctic) Ocean, and Arctic Ocean.
  • 2. Interconnectedness & Conventional Boundaries (1 Mark): The lines dividing them on maps are no more than conventions; in nature, all oceans are interconnected and seawater constantly flows across them.
  • 3. Size Ranking from Largest to Smallest (1.5 Marks): (1) Pacific Ocean (largest), (2) Atlantic Ocean, (3) Indian Ocean (3rd largest), (4) Southern Ocean (4th), and (5) Arctic Ocean (smallest).
  • 4. Limits & Extensions of the Indian Ocean (1.5 Marks): Bounded by Asia (north), Africa (west), Australia (east), and the Southern Ocean (south). On either side of India lie its two parts: the Arabian Sea to the west (with Lakshadweep Islands) and the Bay of Bengal to the east (with Andaman and Nicobar Islands).
LA Q3. Explain the oceanic disasters discussed in the chapter, focusing on the causes and impact of the 26 December 2004 Tsunami and the disaster management measures taken.
View 5-Mark Step-by-Step Answer
  • 1. Storms & Cyclones (1 Mark): Oceans give rise to violent storms with extreme rainfall or very strong winds, such as cyclones, which cause widespread damage to coastal regions.
  • 2. Tsunamis & Their Cause (1 Mark): A tsunami is a huge and powerful wave originating in the ocean, generally caused by a strong earthquake or a volcanic eruption at the bottom of the ocean.
  • 3. The 26 December 2004 Tsunami (1.5 Marks): Triggered by an earthquake in Indonesia, it struck India and 13 other countries around the Indian Ocean. More than two lakh people lost their lives, and in India, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the coasts of Tamil Nadu and Kerala suffered severe damage.
  • 4. Early Warning Systems (1 Mark): Countries collaborate in early detection through the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System, to which India contributes, to protect lives and property before a tsunami hits a coast.
  • 5. Disaster Management Authority (0.5 Marks): India has its own National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) to deal with all kinds of disasters.

6. Case-Study / Source-Based Questions (CBE Pattern – 2 Sets)

Case Study 1: Read the source passage and answer the questions below:
"The oceans together hold most of the water available on the planet. But this seawater is salty and unfit for consumption by most land animals, including humans. On the other hand, freshwater makes up a very small proportion of the planet's water resources; it is found in glaciers, rivers, lakes, in the atmosphere and also underground (the last is called 'groundwater')."

(i) Why is the abundant water held in the oceans unfit for human consumption? (1M)
(ii) Where is freshwater found on Earth, and what is underground freshwater called? (1M)
(iii) How do oceans still provide fresh water to continents for agriculture and life? (2M)

View Case Study 1 Answers
  • (i) Because ocean water (seawater) is salty.
  • (ii) Freshwater is found in glaciers, rivers, lakes, in the atmosphere, and underground. Underground freshwater is called 'groundwater'.
  • (iii) Through the Earth's water cycle, large masses of clouds originate over the oceans and bring rain (such as India's summer monsoon rains) to the continents; without oceans, there would be no rainfall and agriculture would suffer.
Case Study 2: Read the source passage and answer the questions below:
"Since 1981, the Indian Antarctica Programme has been exploring Antarctica, a continent with a very cold climate and harsh environment... In 1983, India established its first scientific base station there, called 'Dakshin Gangotri' (two more bases were established later). About 40 teams of Indian scientists have conducted research in this faraway region, especially on the evolution of climate and environment."

(i) In which year did the Indian Antarctica Programme start, and what was India's first base station called? (1M)
(ii) What is the primary subject of research for the 40 teams of Indian scientists in Antarctica? (1M)
(iii) Describe the climate of Antarctica, which ocean surrounds it, and how its size compares to Europe and Australia. (2M)

View Case Study 2 Answers
  • (i) It started in 1981, and India's first scientific base station established in 1983 was called 'Dakshin Gangotri'.
  • (ii) They conduct research especially on the evolution of climate and environment.
  • (iii) Antarctica has a very cold climate and harsh environment (mostly covered in ice) and is surrounded by the Southern (or Antarctic) Ocean. By size, Antarctica (5th largest, 13 squares) is larger than both Europe (10 squares) and Australia (8 squares).

⚡ "Exam Hall Mistakes" & 5-Minute Rapid Revision Cheat Sheet

⚠️ 5 Common "Exam Hall Mistakes" & How to Avoid Them
  1. Mistake 1: Swapping the seas of Andaman & Nicobar and Lakshadweep Islands.
    Fix: Remember Lakshadweep = Arabian Sea (West) and Andaman & Nicobar = Bay of Bengal (East).
  2. Mistake 2: Writing that the Southern Ocean is the smallest ocean.
    Fix: The Southern Ocean is 4th largest; the Arctic Ocean is the 5th and smallest ocean.
  3. Mistake 3: Naming Australia as the largest island in the world.
    Fix: Australia is the smallest continent. Greenland is the largest island in the world.
  4. Mistake 4: Confusing the cause of a Tsunami with strong winds/cyclones.
    Fix: Cyclones involve strong winds and extreme rainfall, whereas a Tsunami is caused by a strong earthquake or volcanic eruption at the bottom of the ocean.
  5. Mistake 5: Assuming the Northern Hemisphere has more water than the Southern Hemisphere.
    Fix: The Northern Hemisphere has more land than the Southern Hemisphere, while the Southern Hemisphere holds more water.
