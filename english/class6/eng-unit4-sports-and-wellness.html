Part A: "Change of Heart" (Short Story)

1. Pre-Reading Prompts Decoded

  • Playing for Fun vs. Playing for Competition: When playing for fun (like marbles with friends), the goal is laughter, relaxation, and bonding. In competitive sports, rules are formalized, but fair play and sporting spirit must always remain paramount.
  • Key Terminology:
    • Friendly Matches: Practice games played between teams or players without the pressure of an official championship trophy.
    • Opponent: A respected fellow competitor with whom one plays a match, not an enemy.
    • Sporting Spirit: Playing strictly by rules, respecting the umpire, showing fairness to opponents, winning with humility, and accepting defeat with graceful dignity.

2. Detailed Narrative Breakdown

1. Prabhat's Obsessive Fear of Losing: Prabhat was an athletic schoolboy who possessed a crippling character flaw: he could not tolerate defeat. Winning made him feel euphoric; losing plunged him into misery. Consequently, if Prabhat suspected he might lose a game of marbles or cricket, he refused to participate. The only sport he played enthusiastically was badminton, where his skill almost always guaranteed victory.

2. Surya's Arrival: A talented new student named Surya joined the school. Surya was a phenomenal badminton player. When the physical education coach scheduled the monthly Friday friendly matches, he paired Prabhat against Surya. Crucially, the coach appointed Prabhat as the official scorer for the court.

3. Contrasting Personalities: Leading up to Friday, Prabhat practiced with grim, anxious intensity. In contrast, Surya walked around cheerful, relaxed, and cracking jokes, playing purely for the love of the game.

4. The Fateful Match & Cheating: On Friday, Surya displayed dazzling court mobility, powerful smashes, and delicate drop shots. Sensing that Surya was outplaying him, panic seized Prabhat. During scoring transitions, Prabhat discreetly altered the tally, marking points for himself that Surya had won. Surya clearly noticed Prabhat's fraudulent scoring. Yet, instead of stopping play, shouting, or summoning the coach, Surya smiled calmly, continued to play with boundless joy, and allowed Prabhat to win the match on paper.

5. The Bitter "Victory": As the final point ended, classmates cheered for Prabhat. Surya walked up to the net with an open smile, shook Prabhat's trembling hand warmly, and congratulated him sincerely on playing a great match. But instead of joy, an unbearable suffocating wave of guilt and self-loathing crashed over Prabhat. He had the "win", but his conscience was shattered. Surya's graceful magnanimity had exposed Prabhat's petty dishonesty.

6. The Change of Heart: Unable to sleep or swallow food, Prabhat realized that winning by cheating was far worse than losing honorably. The next morning, Prabhat went directly to Surya and confessed his shame, admitting that Surya was the true champion. Surya embraced him warmly, saying: "The score doesn't matter, my friend; what matters is that we had fun playing!" Prabhat's obsession with winning melted away, replaced by genuine sporting spirit and a lifelong friendship.

Part B: "The Winner" by Georgia Heard (Poem)

1. Full Poem Text & Stanza Breakdown

"Evenings,
we play ball
next to the creek
in our neighbour’s field.
We run so fast
I
can’t
even
catch
my
breath.
When blue dark turns to black,
cold grass aches our feet,
trees creep close—
game’s over.
Night wins!"

2. In-Depth Literary Analysis & Sensory Imagery

  • Vivid Sensory Imagery:
    • Visual: "blue dark turns to black" — Capturing the exact magical transition of twilight into nocturnal darkness; "trees creep close" — As darkness envelops the field, distant tree silhouettes appear to loom nearer like tall shadows.
    • Tactile: "cold grass aches our feet" — Evening dew chilling the bare, tired feet of running children.
    • Kinesthetic / Physical: "I can't even catch my breath" — Displayed vertically word-by-word to visually mimic the panting rhythm of an exhausted child gasping for air.
  • Personification & Paradox in the Title: The children are deeply invested in their ball game, wondering who will win. But in the final twist, human competition is rendered completely irrelevant by nature: "Night wins!" Nightfall is personified as the supreme victor that gently commands all games to end and calls children home to rest.

Part C: "Yoga—A Way of Life" (Informative Article)

1. Detailed Text Breakdown

1. Meaning & Origins: 'Yoga' is derived from the ancient Sanskrit root yuj, which signifies "to join, yoke, or unite"—the harmonious integration of physical body, vital breath, and mental consciousness. Systematized thousands of years ago by sage Maharishi Patanjali in the seminal text Yoga Sutras, yoga is not a mere religion, but an empirical, universal science of healthy living.

2. Core Pillars of Practice:

  • Āsanas (Physical Postures): Rhythmic body stretches and postures (e.g., Tadasana, Vrikshasana, Bhujangasana) that systematically tone muscles, improve joint lubrication, align the skeletal frame, and strengthen internal organs.
  • Prāṇāyāma (Breath Control): Mindful breathing exercises (e.g., Anulom Vilom, Kapalbhati, Bhramari) that cleanse lung passages, regulate oxygenation, and soothe the autonomic nervous system.
  • Dhyāna (Meditation): Cultivating single-pointed inward focus, quietening psychological chatter, and achieving deep inner calm.

3. Proven Scientific Health Benefits:

  • Immunity & Cellular Vitality: Stimulates lymphatic drainage, eliminating cellular toxins.
  • Cardiovascular & Respiratory Strength: Lowers resting heart rate and reduces stress hormones (cortisol).
  • Weight Management & Posture: Burns excess calories and corrects postural defects caused by sedentary smartphone/desk habits.

4. Global Recognition & International Yoga Day: Recognizing yoga's universal gift to human health, the United Nations General Assembly declared June 21 as International Day of Yoga following a visionary resolution championed by India. To make yoga universally accessible, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the WHO mYoga app, providing free multilingual instructional audio and video guides across the globe.

1. Past Perfect Tense: Distinguishing Old Action from New Action

When two actions occurred in the past, English grammar uses the Past Perfect (had + V3) for the earlier (first/older) action, and the Simple Past (V2) for the later (second/newer) action.

Sentence First Action (Old Action • Past Perfect) Second Action (New Action • Simple Past)
Everyone was discussing how Prabhat had played so well. had played was discussing
He had won the game but he did not feel happy. had won did not feel
Surya had not felt bad about losing the game. had not felt was surprising
Prabhat had finished his homework before going to the match. had finished went / going

2. Homophone Distinction: Practice vs. Practise • Advice vs. Advise

Word with 'C' (Noun) Definition & Example Word with 'S' (Verb) Definition & Example
Practice (noun) The actual application, routine, or exercise of a skill.
Example: "Developing good health is a daily practice."		 Practise (verb) The repeated action of performing an activity to acquire skill.
Example: "I practise yoga every morning."
Advice (noun) Guidance or recommendations offered to someone.
Example: "Grandpa gave me valuable advice."		 Advise (verb) To recommend, counsel, or suggest an action.

Part 1: Solutions for "Change of Heart"

Let us think and reflect (Pages 108–109): Comprehension Q&A
  1. Why did Prabhat only participate in games he was sure of winning?
    Prabhat had an extreme fear of defeat. Losing made him feel terrible and humiliated. He derived his entire self-esteem from winning, so he avoided games where defeat was possible.
  2. How did Surya react when he noticed that Prabhat was altering the score?
    Surya did not become angry, accuse Prabhat, or stop the match. He smiled gently, continued playing with boundless enthusiasm, and generously allowed Prabhat to win the match on paper.
  3. Why was Prabhat unable to celebrate his victory?
    Prabhat felt suffocated by guilt and shame. Seeing Surya congratulate him with a sincere smile made Prabhat realize how dishonorable and hollow his victory was. His conscience condemned his cheating.
  4. Explain how the title of the story, 'Change of Heart', is suitable.
    The title is perfectly apt because Prabhat undergoes a profound moral and emotional transformation. He moves from being an egoistic, insecure boy who would cheat to win into an honest athlete who embraces sporting spirit, integrity, and true friendship.

Section 6: Common Exam Mistakes & 15 Rapid Revision Points

5 Common Student Mistakes in Exams

❌ Mistake 1: Confusing 'Practice' with 'Practise'

Incorrect: Writing "I practice badminton every day."
Correct: When used as a verb (an action), use practise with an 's'! Use practice with a 'c' when it is a noun (e.g., "His daily practice is commendable.").

❌ Mistake 2: Reversing Order in Past Perfect Sentences

Incorrect: "The train arrived before we had bought tickets."
Correct: The older action gets 'had + V3'! "We had bought tickets before the train arrived."

❌ Mistake 3: Misinterpreting "Night Wins!"

Incorrect: Stating that a boy named "Night" scored the winning goal in the ball game.
Correct: "Night wins!" is a personification of evening darkness arriving and ending the children's match.

❌ Mistake 4: Believing Yoga is Just Physical Gymnastics

Incorrect: Describing yoga as merely flexible body contortions.
Correct: Yoga is a holistic physical, mental, and spiritual science integrating Asanas, Pranayama, and Dhyana.

❌ Mistake 5: Assuming Surya Did Not Notice the Cheating

Incorrect: Writing that Surya was oblivious to Prabhat's altered scoresheet.
Correct: Surya clearly noticed the cheating, but chose to smile and let Prabhat win because Surya cared about friendship and fun, not ego.
