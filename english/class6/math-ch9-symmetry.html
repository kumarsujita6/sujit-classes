9.1 Line of Symmetry (Reflection Symmetry)

Definition: Line of Symmetry (Axis of Symmetry)

A figure is said to have line symmetry (or reflection symmetry) if there exists a straight line dividing the figure into two halves such that, when the figure is folded along that line, the two halves coincide with each other point-for-point. The folding line is called the axis of symmetry or mirror line.

The Geometric Mechanics of Reflection

In reflection across a mirror line m:

  • For every point P on one side of line m, its reflected image P′ lies on the opposite side.
  • The segment PP′ is perpendicular to line m.
  • Line m bisects PP′ at 90° (it is the perpendicular bisector of the segment connecting each point to its image).
  • Points situated directly on the mirror line do not move; they remain invariant.

Paper Folding & Punch-Hole Geometry

Punching holes in folded sheets reveals symmetry through physical action:

  • Single Fold: If a square sheet is folded once along its vertical centerline and a hole is punched through both layers, unfolding reveals 2 holes located symmetrically on opposite sides of the fold line.
  • Double Fold (Vertical + Horizontal): If folded vertically and then horizontally, punching a single hole creates 4 holes situated symmetrically in the four quadrants.
  • Diagonal Fold: Folding a square along its diagonal and punching a hole creates pairs reflected across the diagonal axis.
Crucial Conceptual Paradox: The Diagonal of a Rectangle

Question: Is the diagonal of a non-square rectangle a line of symmetry?
Answer: Absolutely NOT! While a diagonal divides a rectangle into two congruent triangles of identical area, when you physically fold the rectangle along the diagonal, the two halves do NOT overlap. The opposite corners protrude outward like a chevron kite! Therefore, a general rectangle has only 2 lines of symmetry (the horizontal and vertical lines joining midpoints of opposite sides), NOT 4!

The Regular Polygon Symmetry Theorem

A regular polygon is a polygon with all sides equal in length and all interior angles equal in measure. A profound mathematical law connects sides to lines of symmetry:

The n-Line Regular Polygon Theorem

Every regular polygon with n sides has exactly n lines of symmetry:
• Equilateral Triangle (n = 3): 3 lines of symmetry (the 3 angle bisectors / medians).
• Square (n = 4): 4 lines of symmetry (2 side medians + 2 diagonals).
• Regular Pentagon (n = 5): 5 lines of symmetry (connecting each vertex to the midpoint of opposite side).
• Regular Hexagon (n = 6): 6 lines of symmetry (3 connecting opposite vertices + 3 connecting opposite side midpoints).
• Regular Octagon (n = 8): 8 lines of symmetry.
• Circle (n → ∞): Infinitely many lines of symmetry (any straight line passing through its center is a diameter and a line of symmetry).

9.2 Rotational Symmetry

Definition: Rotational Symmetry

A plane figure has rotational symmetry if, when rotated around a fixed point through an angle strictly less than 360°, it fits onto its original outline and looks completely unchanged.

Core Components of Rotational Symmetry

  1. Center of Rotation: The fixed pivot point about which the shape rotates. For polygons, it is the intersection of diagonals or circumcenter; for a circle, it is the center.
  2. Smallest Angle of Symmetry (θ): The minimal positive angle of rotation required to make the figure match itself.
    Formula: θ = 360° / n, where n is the order.
  3. Order of Rotational Symmetry (n): The total number of distinct times the figure matches its original appearance during one complete 360° revolution.
    Crucial Rule: Since every shape trivially matches itself after a full 360° turn, a figure has rotational symmetry only if its Order ≥ 2 (Order 1 means NO rotational symmetry).
  4. Sequence of Symmetry Angles: All valid rotation angles are exact integer multiples of the smallest angle:
    Angles = θ, 2θ, 3θ, ..., nθ (= 360°).

Section 9.1 — In-Text Foldings & Diagonal Paradox (Pages 221–222)

Question:
(a) How many lines of symmetry does a square shape have?
(b) Is the diagonal of a non-square rectangle a line of symmetry? Explain what happens on folding.
(c) In square ABCD, what happens when reflected along diagonal AC? Where do B and D go?

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a) — Square Line Count:
Step 1: Square ABCD has 4 equal sides and four 90° angles.
Step 2: Lines of symmetry: 1 vertical median, 1 horizontal median, 2 diagonal axes.
Step 3: Total count = 2 + 2 = 4 lines of symmetry.

Part (b) — Rectangle Diagonal Paradox:
Step 1: Take a rectangle of length 6 cm and breadth 4 cm. Fold along diagonal AC.
Step 2: In a non-square rectangle, adjacent sides are unequal (AB ≠ BC). When folded along AC, vertex B does NOT land on vertex D; the edges stick out at an angle.
Step 3: Therefore, No, a rectangle's diagonal is NOT a line of symmetry.

Part (c) — Diagonal Reflection in Square ABCD:
Step 1: Diagonal AC acts as the mirror line.
Step 2: Vertices A and C lie on the mirror line, so their positions remain unchanged.
Step 3: Vertex D reflects across AC and lands exactly at the position of vertex B, while B lands at D.

Section 9.2 — In-Text Radial Arms & Angles (Pages 235–236)

Question:
(a) Find the smallest angle and all angles of symmetry for a figure with 5 radial arms.
(b) Find the smallest angle and all angles of symmetry for a figure with 6 radial arms.
(c) For a figure with 7 radial arms, calculate the smallest angle. Is it a whole number? Express as a mixed fraction.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Formula:
Smallest angle of symmetry = 360° ÷ (Number of radial arms n). All angles are multiples of this angle up to 360°.

Step 2: Step-by-step arithmetic working:
Part (a) — 5 Radial Arms:
Smallest angle = 360° ÷ 5 = 72°.
All angles: 72°, 144°, 216°, 288°, 360° (5 angles of symmetry).

Part (b) — 6 Radial Arms:
Smallest angle = 360° ÷ 6 = 60°.
All angles: 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°, 360° (6 angles of symmetry).

Part (c) — 7 Radial Arms:
Smallest angle = 360° ÷ 7.
360 ÷ 7 = 51 with remainder 3 ⇒ 51 3/7°.
This is NOT a whole number.

Step 3: Final values:
(a) 72° (order 5); (b) 60° (order 6); (c) 51 3/7° (order 7).

Step 4: Concluding statement:
The smallest angles are 72°, 60°, and 51 3/7° respectively.

Section 9.2 — Figure it Out Q3: Dual Symmetry Sketches (Page 238)

Question: Draw, wherever possible, a rough sketch of:
(a) A triangle with at least two lines of symmetry and at least two angles of symmetry.
(b) A triangle with only one line of symmetry but no rotational symmetry.
(c) A quadrilateral with rotational symmetry but NO reflection symmetry.
(d) A quadrilateral with reflection symmetry but NO rotational symmetry.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a): An Equilateral Triangle. It has 3 lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order 3 (angles: 120°, 240°, 360°). Note: A triangle cannot have exactly two lines of symmetry; having two forces it to have three!

Part (b): An Isosceles Triangle (with two equal sides and one unequal side). It has exactly 1 line of symmetry (vertical altitude) and only matches after a full 360° turn (order 1, hence no rotational symmetry).

Part (c): A Parallelogram (non-rectangular, non-rhombus). It has 0 lines of reflection symmetry, but when rotated 180° around its diagonal intersection, it matches itself perfectly (rotational symmetry of order 2).

Part (d): A Kite (or an Isosceles Trapezium). A kite has exactly 1 line of reflection symmetry (its main diagonal), but has no rotational symmetry (order 1).

5.1 Multiple Choice Questions (5 MCQs with Trap Analysis)

MCQ 1: Lines of Symmetry in a Non-Square Rectangle

Question: How many lines of reflection symmetry does a standard non-square rectangle possess?

A) 4     B) 2     C) 1     D) 0

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: B) 2

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Option A (4) is selected by students who falsely assume that diagonals are lines of symmetry in a rectangle. While diagonals divide the rectangle into two congruent triangles, folding along a diagonal does NOT make the halves overlap. Only the two lines joining midpoints of opposite sides are lines of symmetry.

MCQ 2: Smallest Angle of Rotational Symmetry

Question: A figure has rotational symmetry of order 8. What is its smallest angle of rotation?

A) 90°     B) 60°     C) 45°     D) 30°

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: C) 45°

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Formula for smallest angle is θ = 360° ÷ n. For n = 8: 360° ÷ 8 = 45°. Dividing 360 by 6 gives 60° (Option B) and by 4 gives 90° (Option A).

MCQ 3: Rotational Symmetry Without Reflection Symmetry

Question: Which of the following quadrilaterals has rotational symmetry of order 2, but ZERO lines of reflection symmetry?

A) Rhombus     B) Kite     C) Parallelogram     D) Isosceles Trapezium

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: C) Parallelogram

Conceptual Trap Decoded: A rhombus (Option A) has 2 lines of reflection symmetry. A kite (Option B) has 1 line of symmetry but no rotational symmetry. A parallelogram has point/rotational symmetry of 180° (order 2), but folding along any line fails to overlap the opposite halves.

MCQ 4: Alphabet Letter with Both Symmetries

Question: Which capital letter of the English alphabet has BOTH vertical and horizontal lines of symmetry?

A) M     B) E     C) H     D) N

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: C) H

Conceptual Trap Decoded: 'M' has only vertical line symmetry; 'E' has only horizontal line symmetry; 'N' has rotational symmetry of order 2 but ZERO lines of symmetry. 'H' has both a vertical axis and a horizontal axis of symmetry.

MCQ 5: Regular Polygon Symmetry Law

Question: If a regular polygon has 12 lines of symmetry, what is the measure of the angle between two adjacent symmetry axes?

A) 30°     B) 15°     C) 45°     D) 60°

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: B) 15°

Conceptual Trap Decoded: 12 lines of symmetry pass through the center, dividing the 360° full circle into 2 × 12 = 24 equal sectors! Hence, the angle between adjacent lines of symmetry is 360° ÷ 24 = 15° (NOT 360° ÷ 12 = 30°, which is the rotational step).

5.2 Assertion & Reasoning Questions (4 Questions)

Directions: Choose (A) if both A and R are true and R is correct explanation of A; (B) if both are true but R is NOT correct explanation; (C) if A is true but R is false; (D) if A is false but R is true.

A&R 1: Infinite Symmetry in a Circle

Assertion (A): A circle has an infinite number of lines of symmetry.
Reason (R): Any straight line passing through the center of a circle is a diameter, and folding along any diameter maps the circle onto itself.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: Because a circle is defined as all points equidistant from the center, reflection across any line through the center leaves all radial distances invariant. Since there are infinitely many diameters, there are infinitely many lines of symmetry.

A&R 2: Order of Rotational Symmetry in Regular Polygons

Assertion (A): A regular pentagon has rotational symmetry of order 5 with smallest angle 72°.
Reason (R): A regular polygon with n sides has rotational symmetry of order n, and its smallest angle is 360° ÷ n.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: For n = 5: Smallest angle = 360° ÷ 5 = 72°, and order = 5. Reason directly provides the universal mathematical law.

A&R 3: Two Lines of Symmetry in a Triangle Fallacy

Assertion (A): A triangle can have exactly two lines of symmetry.
Reason (R): If two sides of a triangle are equal, the triangle has at least one line of symmetry.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (D) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) is true.

Explanation: A triangle can have either 0 lines of symmetry (scalene), 1 line of symmetry (isosceles), or 3 lines of symmetry (equilateral). It is geometrically impossible for a triangle to have exactly 2 lines of symmetry, because if two altitudes are symmetry axes, all three sides are forced to be equal, automatically creating a 3rd axis!

A&R 4: The Order 1 Triviality

Assertion (A): A scalene triangle is said to have rotational symmetry of order 1.
Reason (R): Any plane shape will look identical to itself after a complete rotation of 360°.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (B) Both A and R are true, but R does not mean the shape possesses rotational symmetry in mathematical parlance (Order 1 is considered NO rotational symmetry).

Explanation: While mathematically every figure trivially coincides at 360° (order 1), we state that a figure possesses rotational symmetry only if its order is 2 or greater.

Question: A decorative star has a rotational symmetry of order 10.

SA 4: Rhombus Lines of Symmetry Proof (3 Marks)

Question: State how many lines of symmetry a rhombus has, and explain why the lines joining the midpoints of opposite sides are NOT lines of symmetry in a non-square rhombus.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: A rhombus has exactly 2 lines of symmetry, which are its two diagonals. [1 Mark]

Step 2: In a rhombus, opposite angles are not 90° (one pair is acute, one pair is obtuse). If you fold along a line connecting side midpoints, an acute angle would have to overlap an obtuse angle, which is impossible. Hence midpoints do not form symmetry lines. [2 Marks]

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Formula: Number of sectors = 360° ÷ 60° = 6. [1 Mark]

Step 2: The kaleidoscope generates a pattern with order 6 rotational symmetry, producing 5 reflected images plus the original object (6 identical sectors). [1 Mark]

Question: On a Cartesian coordinate dot grid, a triangle has vertices at P(2, 3), Q(5, 3), and R(4, 7).
(a) Find the coordinates of the reflected image ΔP′Q′R′ across the Y-axis.
(b) Find the coordinates of the reflected image ΔP″Q″R″ across the X-axis.
(c) Find the coordinates of the image after a 180° rotation about the origin (0, 0).
(d) Compare the image from part (c) with successive reflections across both axes.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Rubric

Marking Breakdown:
[Part (a): Y-axis reflection — 1.5 Marks]
[Part (b): X-axis reflection — 1.5 Marks]
[Part (c): 180° rotation — 1 Mark]
[Part (d): Mathematical comparison — 1 Mark]

Full Solution:
Part (a) Reflection across Y-axis: Transformation rule: (x, y) → (−x, y).
- P(2, 3) → P′(−2, 3)
- Q(5, 3) → Q′(−5, 3)

- Q(5, 3) → Q″(5, −3)
- R(4, 7) → R″(4, −7)

Part (c) 180° Rotation about Origin: Transformation rule: (x, y) → (−x, −y).
- P(2, 3) → (−2, −3)
- Q(5, 3) → (−5, −3)
- R(4, 7) → (−4, −7)

Part (d) Mathematical Theorem:
Reflecting across the Y-axis and then across the X-axis yields: (x, y) → (−x, y) → (−x, −y). This proves that two successive perpendicular reflections are mathematically identical to a 180° rotation (half turn) around the intersection point!

LA 3: The Regular Octagon Comprehensive Proof (5 Marks)

Question: For a regular octagon (stop sign shape):
(a) State its number of lines of symmetry and describe their locations.
(b) Find its order of rotational symmetry and all angles of symmetry.
(c) If a straight line cuts the regular octagon into two equal halves, must that line always be a line of symmetry? Justify with a counterexample.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Rubric

Marking Breakdown:
[Part (a): Lines and locations — 2 Marks]
[Part (b): Rotational order and angles — 1.5 Marks]
[Part (c): Area bisection vs symmetry line proof — 1.5 Marks]

Full Solution:
Part (a): By the Regular Polygon Law, a regular octagon has 8 lines of symmetry:
- 4 lines connecting opposite pairs of vertices.
- 4 lines connecting midpoints of opposite parallel edges.

Part (b): Order of rotational symmetry = 8.
Smallest angle = 360° ÷ 8 = 45°.
All angles: 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°, 225°, 270°, 315°, 360°.

Part (c) Critical Proof:
No! Any straight line passing through the center of an octagon divides its area into two equal halves (by 180° rotational symmetry). However, if you choose a line passing through the center at an angle of 10° to an edge, folding along this line will NOT make the vertices overlap. Therefore, a line dividing a shape into two equal areas is NOT necessarily an axis of symmetry.

HOTS Problem 6: Asymmetric Polygon with Area Bisector

Question: Draw an example of a quadrilateral where a line divides it into two halves of equal area, but the line is NOT a line of symmetry.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Take a non-square rectangle ABCD (e.g. 6 cm × 4 cm).
Step 2: Draw diagonal AC.
Step 3: Area of ΔABC = ½ × 6 × 4 = 12 cm². Area of ΔADC = ½ × 6 × 4 = 12 cm². The areas are strictly equal!
Step 4: However, folding along diagonal AC does not overlap the halves.
Step 5: Therefore, the diagonal of a rectangle divides it into two equal areas but is NOT a line of symmetry.

HOTS Problem 7: Rotational Angles Non-Multiples Check

Question: Can a figure have rotational symmetry angles of 70° and 120°? Justify mathematically.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: If a figure has rotational symmetry angles α and β, their difference β − α must also be a symmetry angle.
Step 2: 120° − 70° = 50°. Then 70° − 50° = 20°. Then 50° − 2×20° = 10°.
Step 3: If 10° is a symmetry angle, the order would be 360° ÷ 10° = 36.
Step 4: For 70° to be an angle of symmetry, 360° must be an exact multiple of the smallest angle. Since 360 is not divisible by 70 (360 ÷ 70 = 5.14), 70° CANNOT be an angle of symmetry for any closed shape!

HOTS Problem 8: Semicircle vs Quarter-Circle Symmetry

Question: Compare the reflection and rotational symmetries of: (a) A semicircle, (b) A quarter-circle sector.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a) Semicircle: Has 1 line of symmetry (the perpendicular bisector of the diameter baseline). Has no rotational symmetry (order 1).

Part (b) Quarter-Circle: Has 1 line of symmetry (the 45° angle bisector of the right-angled corner). Has no rotational symmetry (order 1).

HOTS Problem 9: Shading Sectors for Specific Rotational Order

Question: A circle is divided into 12 identical sectors. What is the minimum number of sectors that must be shaded so that the resulting pattern has rotational symmetry of order 3, but NO reflection symmetry?

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: For order 3, the circle must match itself after 360° ÷ 3 = 120° rotations.
Step 2: 120° spans 12 ÷ 3 = 4 sectors.
Step 3: To achieve order 3, whatever is shaded in the first 4 sectors must repeat identically in sectors 5–8 and sectors 9–12.
Step 4: To avoid reflection symmetry, shade an asymmetric pattern in each group (e.g. shade sector 1 and sector 2, but not sector 3 or 4).
Step 5: Minimum non-trivial shading = 1 sector per group × 3 groups = 3 sectors (e.g., sectors 1, 5, 9). Note: Shading sectors 1, 5, 9 creates 3 lines of symmetry! To have NO reflection symmetry, shade an asymmetric pair in each block: sectors (1, 2), (5, 6), (9, 10) ⇒ 6 sectors.

HOTS Problem 10: Swastika / Pinwheel Mathematical Symmetry

Question: Explain mathematically why a four-armed pinwheel or swastika symbol has rotational symmetry of order 4, but exactly 0 lines of reflection symmetry.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: The 4 arms are identical and rotated by 90° relative to each other. Turning through 90°, 180°, 270°, and 360° brings each arm onto the next ⇒ Order 4 rotational symmetry.
Step 2: However, each arm bends in a specific direction (clockwise or counter-clockwise).
Step 3: When reflected across any mirror line, a clockwise bend reflects into a counter-clockwise bend (chirality flips).
Step 4: Because the bend direction inverts, the reflected image never matches the original.
Step 5: Hence, it has 0 lines of reflection symmetry.

Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 Rapid Revision One-Liners

Top 5 Exam Hall Traps in Symmetry

⚠️ Trap 1: The Rectangle Diagonal Fallacy (Writing 4 Lines of Symmetry)

A perennial mistake on board exams is asserting that a rectangle has 4 lines of symmetry. A non-square rectangle has only 2 lines of symmetry (the horizontal and vertical medians). While a diagonal splits a rectangle into two congruent triangles, folding along the diagonal results in edges jutting outward at an angle; the halves do NOT coincide!

⚠️ Trap 2: Believing "Order 1" Means Possessing Rotational Symmetry

Because every single physical object coincides with itself after a 360° complete turn, students often write that an asymmetric scalene triangle "has rotational symmetry of order 1". In mathematics, a figure is defined to possess rotational symmetry only if its Order ≥ 2. Order 1 signifies NO rotational symmetry.

⚠️ Trap 3: Crediting Parallelograms with 2 Lines of Symmetry

A general parallelogram looks visually balanced, tempting students to assume its diagonals or medians are lines of symmetry. In reality, a general parallelogram has ZERO lines of reflection symmetry! It only possesses 180° rotational symmetry (Order 2).

⚠️ Trap 4: Confusing Area Bisection with Reflection Symmetry

Just because a straight line bisects the area of a shape into two equal pieces does NOT make it an axis of symmetry. For instance, any line passing through the center of a parallelogram cuts its area in half, but none of these lines are reflection axes.

⚠️ Trap 5: Forgetting to List Multiples for Angles of Symmetry

When asked for the "angles of symmetry", students frequently write only the smallest angle θ (e.g., 60°) and stop. Remember: all positive multiples up to 360° must be listed (e.g., 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°, 360°).

15 Rapid Revision One-Liners (Pre-Exam Checklist)

  1. A line of symmetry divides a figure into two identical halves that coincide point-for-point upon folding.
  2. Every regular polygon with n sides has exactly n lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order n.
  3. An equilateral triangle has 3 lines of symmetry; an isosceles triangle has 1; a scalene triangle has 0.
  4. A square has 4 lines of symmetry (2 medians + 2 diagonals).
  5. A rectangle has only 2 lines of symmetry (medians); its diagonals are NOT reflection lines.
  6. A rhombus has 2 lines of symmetry, which are its two diagonals.
  7. A circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry passing through its center.
  8. Rotational symmetry means a figure matches its original appearance under a rotation strictly less than 360°.
  9. The center of rotation is the fixed pivot point around which rotation occurs.
  10. The order of rotational symmetry is the number of matching orientations within one complete 360° turn.
  11. A figure possesses rotational symmetry only if its Order ≥ 2.
  12. The smallest angle of symmetry is calculated as θ = 360° / n.
  13. All angles of symmetry are integer multiples of the smallest angle: θ, 2θ, ..., nθ = 360°.
  14. A parallelogram has rotational symmetry of order 2, but ZERO lines of reflection symmetry.
  15. A line dividing a figure into two equal surface areas is NOT necessarily a line of symmetry.
