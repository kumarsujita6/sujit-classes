Line-by-Line Conceptual Notes

7.1 Fractional Units and Equal Shares

The concept of a fraction arises naturally whenever whole quantities are partitioned equally:

The Fundamental Sharing Principle: When m identical whole items are distributed equally among n recipients, the exact share received by each individual is represented by the fraction:
Share per person = m / n
• Numerator (m): The number of equal parts being considered or shared.
• Denominator (n): The total number of equal parts into which the whole is divided.

Unit Fractions & The Inverse Size Law:

A unit fraction is any fraction having 1 as its numerator (e.g., 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5... 1/n).

The Inverse Size Law: As the denominator increases, each individual slice becomes strictly smaller!
1/2 > 1/3 > 1/4 > 1/5 > 1/6 > ... > 1/100
Sharing 1 roti among 2 children gives half a roti each. Sharing that same roti among 10 children gives a tiny sliver (1/10th) each.

7.2 Fractional Units as Parts of a Whole

A whole can be a single geometric object (a circle, chikki, rectangle) or a discrete collection (a basket of 20 apples).

  • Equal Area vs. Equal Shape: The parts do NOT need to look identical in shape to represent the same fraction! As long as their enclosed surface area is equal, each part represents 1/n of the whole.
  • Fraction of a Collection: To find a fraction of a number (e.g. 3/4 of 24), divide the total by the denominator, then multiply by the numerator: (24 ÷ 4) × 3 = 6 × 3 = 18.

7.4 Marking Fraction Lengths on the Number Line

Fractions are numbers with precise, definite spatial coordinates on the real number line:

  • Partitioning the Unit Interval [0, 1]: To plot fractions with denominator 5, divide the segment between 0 and 1 into 5 equal sub-intervals. The tick marks represent 1/5, 2/5, 3/5, 4/5, and 5/5 = 1.
  • Infinite Density of Fractions: Between ANY two fractions on the number line, no matter how close, there exist infinitely many other fractions! For example, between 0 and 1/2 lies 1/4; between 0 and 1/4 lies 1/8, and so on without end.

7.5 Proper, Improper, and Mixed Fractions

Fractions are classified based on the relative size of their numerator and denominator:

Fraction Type Defining Condition Value Relative to 1 Examples
Proper Fraction Numerator < Denominator Strictly < 1 1/2, 3/4, 5/8, 9/10
Improper Fraction Numerator ≥ Denominator Greater than or equal to 1 5/4, 7/3, 11/5, 4/4 = 1
Mixed Fraction Whole Number + Proper Fraction Strictly > 1 1 ¾, 2 ⅓, 5 ½

Conversion Algorithms:

  • Improper → Mixed: Divide numerator by denominator.
    Improper = Quotient + (Remainder / Denominator).
    Example: 17 ÷ 5 = 3 with remainder 2 ⇒ 3 ⅖.
  • Mixed → Improper:
    Improper Numerator = (Whole × Denominator) + Numerator.
    Example: 4 ⅔ = (4 × 3 + 2) / 3 = (12 + 2) / 3 = 14/3.

7.6 Equivalent Fractions & Simplest Form

Fractions that represent the exact same proportion of a whole or point on the number line are called Equivalent Fractions.

The Golden Scaling Rule: Multiplying or dividing both numerator and denominator by the same non-zero integer k preserves the value:
a / b = (a × k) / (b × k) = (a ÷ k) / (b ÷ k)

Cross-Multiplication Test for Equivalence:

Two fractions a/b and c/d are equivalent if and only if their cross-products are equal:
a / b = c / d  ⇔  a × d = b × c

Simplest Form (Lowest Terms):

A fraction a/b is in its simplest form (or irreducible form) if its numerator and denominator are co-prime (their HCF is 1).
To reduce any fraction to simplest form, divide both numerator and denominator by their HCF:
Example: 36/48 → HCF(36, 48) = 12 → (36 ÷ 12) / (48 ÷ 12) = 3/4.

7.7 Comparing Fractions

Three standardized methods exist to determine which of two fractions is larger:

  1. Like Denominators: The fraction with the greater numerator is larger (e.g., 5/7 > 3/7).
  2. Like Numerators: The fraction with the smaller denominator is larger (e.g., 3/5 > 3/8).
  3. Unlike Fractions (Cross-Multiplication): To compare a/b and c/d, compare cross-products (a × d) and (b × c):
    • If a × d > b × c, then a/b > c/d.
    • If a × d < b × c, then a/b < c/d.
  4. Unlike Fractions (Common Denominator / LCM): Convert both fractions to equivalent fractions having a common denominator equal to LCM(b, d), then compare numerators.

7.8 Addition and Subtraction of Fractions

1. Like Denominators:

Add or subtract the numerators while retaining the common denominator unchanged:
a / c + b / c = (a + b) / c  |  a / c − b / c = (a − b) / c

2. Unlike Denominators (LCM Method):

  1. Find the LCM of the denominators.
  2. Convert each fraction to an equivalent fraction with denominator = LCM.
  3. Add or subtract the converted numerators.
  4. Simplify the resulting fraction to lowest terms.

7.9 Historical Pedagogy: Brahmagupta's Universal Formulation

In 628 CE, in his masterwork Brahmasphutasiddhanta, the great Indian mathematician Brahmagupta wrote the algebraic rule for adding and subtracting any two fractions:

Brahmagupta's Universal Cross-Addition Rule:
a / b + c / d = (a × d + b × c) / (b × d)

Brahmagupta's Universal Cross-Subtraction Rule:
a / b − c / d = (a × d − b × c) / (b × d)

This universal method automatically yields a common denominator (b × d) without first needing to find the LCM, followed by reducing to lowest terms!

MCQ 1: Fractional Unit of a Segment

Question: A length of 1 unit is divided into 7 equal parts. What fraction represents the total length of 5 such parts taken together?

A) 7/5     B) 5/7     C) 1/7     D) 5/35

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: B) 5/7

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Students frequently confuse the denominator (total parts into which the whole is divided) with the numerator (parts selected), picking Option A (7/5), which is an improper fraction greater than 1.

Step-by-step Justification: Each equal part has length 1/7. Taking 5 such parts yields 5 × (1/7) = 5/7.

MCQ 2: Equivalent Fractions Property

Question: Which of the following fractions is NOT equivalent to 3/4?

A) 6/8     B) 15/20     C) 9/16     D) 27/36

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: C) 9/16

Conceptual Trap Decoded: In Option C, the numerator 3 is squared to 9, and the denominator 4 is squared to 16. Squaring does NOT preserve fraction equivalence! Equivalence requires multiplying both terms by the same linear factor: 3×3 = 9, but 4×3 = 12 (not 16).

Verification: 6/8 = 3/4 (factor 2); 15/20 = 3/4 (factor 5); 27/36 = 3/4 (factor 9). But 9/16 ≠ 3/4.

MCQ 3: Fraction Comparison with Identical Numerators

Question: Which of the following comparison statements is TRUE?

A) 4/7 < 4/9     B) 4/7 > 4/9     C) 4/7 = 4/9     D) 4/7 ≤ 4/11

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: B) 4/7 > 4/9

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Students intuitively see that 9 > 7 and erroneously assume that 4/9 must be greater than 4/7. However, dividing a whole into 7 parts produces larger portions than dividing the same whole into 9 parts. Hence 4 parts of size 1/7 are greater than 4 parts of size 1/9.

MCQ 4: Addition of Unlike Fractions Trap

Question: The value of 1/2 + 1/3 is:

A) 2/5     B) 1/5     C) 5/6     D) 2/6

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

Correct Answer: C) 5/6

Conceptual Trap Decoded: Option A (2/5) is the classic "universal error" where students add numerators (1+1=2) and add denominators (2+3=5). Denominators name the size of parts and cannot be added directly!

Working: 1/2 + 1/3 = 3/6 + 2/6 = (3+2)/6 = 5/6.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution & Conceptual Trap

5.2 Assertion & Reasoning Questions (4 Questions)

Directions: Choose (A) if both A and R are true and R is correct explanation of A; (B) if both are true but R is NOT correct explanation; (C) if A is true but R is false; (D) if A is false but R is true.

A&R 1: Lowest Terms and Coprime Numbers

Assertion (A): The fraction 15/28 is in its lowest terms (simplest form).
Reason (R): A fraction a/b is in lowest terms if and only if the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of a and b is 1.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: Factors of 15 are 1, 3, 5, 15. Factors of 28 are 1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28. HCF(15, 28) = 1. Since their HCF is 1, 15 and 28 are coprime, meaning 15/28 cannot be simplified further. Reason correctly defines the criterion.

A&R 2: Proper Fraction Values

Assertion (A): Every proper fraction is strictly less than 1.
Reason (R): In a proper fraction, the numerator is strictly smaller than the denominator.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: By definition, a proper fraction represents taking fewer parts than the total number of parts that make up a whole unit. Hence p < q implies p/q < 1.

A&R 3: Density of Fractions

Assertion (A): There are exactly 3 fractions between 1/5 and 4/5.
Reason (R): The fractions 2/5, 3/5, and 3.5/5 lie between 1/5 and 4/5.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (D) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) contains true observations. (Or C/D: A is unequivocally FALSE).

Explanation: Assertion is completely FALSE. Between any two fractions, there exist an infinite number of fractions (e.g., 21/100, 22/100, 301/1000, etc.). Thinking there are only two or three fractions arises from looking only at the like denominator 5.

A&R 4: Adding Denominators Fallacy

Assertion (A): 3/7 + 2/7 = 5/7.
Reason (R): When adding like fractions, we add the numerators and keep the common denominator unchanged.

💡 Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Explanation: Since each part has identical unit size 1/7, combining 3 parts and 2 parts gives (3+2) parts of size 1/7 = 5/7. Denominators are not added.

Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 Rapid Revision One-Liners

Top 5 Exam Hall Traps in Fractions

⚠️ Trap 1: Adding Denominators Directly (The Universal Fraction Fallacy)

A fatal student mistake is computing 1/2 + 1/3 = (1+1)/(2+3) = 2/5. Denominators name the size of the partition units, not items to be counted! You can only add numerators once denominators are identical: 1/2 + 1/3 = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.

⚠️ Trap 2: Intuitive "Bigger Denominator = Bigger Fraction" Illusion

When comparing 3/5 and 3/8, students often mistakenly write 3/8 > 3/5 because 8 > 5. In reality, dividing a cake into 8 slices yields much smaller pieces than dividing it into 5 slices. Therefore, for equal numerators, smaller denominator means larger fraction: 3/5 > 3/8.

⚠️ Trap 3: Generating "Equivalent" Fractions by Addition Instead of Multiplication

Adding the same constant to the top and bottom does NOT produce an equivalent fraction! For example, (2 + 3) / (5 + 3) = 5/8 ≠ 2/5 (2/5 = 0.40, while 5/8 = 0.625). Equivalent fractions are formed only by multiplying or dividing both terms by the same non-zero factor k.

⚠️ Trap 4: Mixed Fraction Reversal Error

When converting 23/5 to a mixed fraction, 23 ÷ 5 = 4 with remainder 3. Students frequently write 3 4/5 instead of 4 3/5, reversing the quotient and remainder. Remember: Quotient is the Whole Number, Remainder is the Numerator.

⚠️ Trap 5: Forgetting to Reduce to Lowest Terms

In CBSE board examinations, leaving a final answer as 18/24 instead of simplifying to 3/4 results in a 1/2 mark deduction. Always check if the numerator and denominator share any common factor greater than 1, and divide by their HCF.

15 Rapid Revision One-Liners (Pre-Exam Checklist)

  1. A fraction represents one or more equal parts of a whole unit or collection.
  2. In a/b, the denominator 'b' indicates the total number of equal parts, and the numerator 'a' indicates how many parts are considered.
  3. A unit fraction always has 1 as its numerator (e.g., 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/8).
  4. A proper fraction has its numerator smaller than its denominator (p < q) and is strictly less than 1.
  5. An improper fraction has its numerator greater than or equal to its denominator (p ≥ q) and is ≥ 1.
  6. A mixed fraction consists of a non-zero whole number and a proper fraction (e.g., 2 3/4).
  7. To convert improper fraction p/q to mixed: Quotient + (Remainder / q).
  8. To convert mixed fraction W R/q to improper: (W × q + R) / q.
  9. Equivalent fractions represent the exact same amount; formed by multiplying or dividing numerator and denominator by the same number.
  10. A fraction is in lowest terms (simplest form) if the HCF of numerator and denominator is 1.
  11. Between any two numbers or fractions on the number line, there exist infinitely many fractions.
  12. When comparing fractions with the same denominator, the fraction with the larger numerator is greater.
  13. When comparing fractions with the same numerator, the fraction with the smaller denominator is greater.
  14. Brahmagupta's rule adds unlike fractions: (a/b) + (c/d) = (a×d + c×b) / (b×d).
  15. Never add denominators: Like fractions are added by summing numerators over the common denominator: a/c + b/c = (a + b)/c.
