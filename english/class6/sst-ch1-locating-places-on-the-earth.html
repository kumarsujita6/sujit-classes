TOPIC 1: Introduction & A Map and Its Components
"The globe of the Earth stands in space, made up of water, earth, fire and air and is spherical.... It is surrounded by all creatures, terrestrial as well as aquatic."
— Āryabhaṭa (about 500 CE)
  • Why Do We Need Maps?
    • When visiting a city for the first time, we find places either by asking a local person or looking at a map of the city.
    • A map is like a treasure guide; it shows you where things are and how to get to them.
    • In a map, you look at the surface as if you are viewing it from the top.
  • 🔥 Three Main Types of Maps:
    1. Physical Maps: Mainly show natural features such as mountains, oceans, and rivers (e.g., Fig. 5.2 in the textbook).
    2. Political Maps: Show details of countries or states, boundaries, cities, etc. (for instance, a map of India with all its States, Union Territories, and their capitals).
    3. Thematic Maps: Show a specific kind of information (e.g., rainfall, vegetation, industries, or historical empires as in Fig. 6.3 and Fig. 8.1).
  • 🔥 The Three Important Components of Maps:

    Every map relies on three core components: (1) Distance, (2) Direction, and (3) Symbols.

📘 Key Definitions (Page 9)
  • Map: A representation, or a drawing, of some area — whether a small area (a village, a town), a bigger area (a district or state), or a very large area like India or the whole world — viewed as if looking from the top.
  • Atlas: A book or collection of maps is called an atlas.

Detailed Breakdown of the 3 Components of a Map:

  1. Component 1: Distance (and Scale):
    • How does a huge place fit on a small piece of paper? It is all thanks to the map's scale.
    • Scale Definition: Each centimetre on the map represents a certain distance on the ground.
    • Example 1 (Small City Map, Fig. 1.1): If 1 cm on the map represents 500 metres on the ground, we say the scale is 1 cm = 500 m.
    • Example 2 (Map of India, Fig. 5.2): The scale in the bottom-left corner is shown by a printed ruler measuring 2.5 cm with '500' written above it and 'km' on the side. This means 2.5 cm on the printed map corresponds to 500 kilometres on the ground (i.e., 1 cm = 200 km).
    • Thus, the actual distance between two points represented on a map depends on the scale that the map is using.
  2. Component 2: Direction (Cardinal & Intermediate Points):
    • Cardinal Directions (Cardinal Points): The four main directions — North (N) at the top, and moving clockwise: East (E), South (S), and West (W).
    • Intermediate Directions: The four directions halfway between the cardinal points — Northeast (NE), Southeast (SE), Southwest (SW), and Northwest (NW).
    • North Line ('N' Arrow): Most maps simply have an arrow marked with the letter 'N', which points to the north direction. Once you know North, you can easily find all other directions.
  3. Component 3: Symbols (Survey of India Standards):
    • On a map of a large city or country, there is not enough space to draw actual buildings or trees. Instead, a symbol is used to represent features like buildings (railway station, school, post office), roads, railway lines, and natural elements (river, pond, forest).
    • Symbols allow numerous details to be shown in the limited space available on a map and make maps easily understood by a variety of users.
    • Different countries use different sets of symbols. In India, the Survey of India (a government body) has fixed a standard set of symbols for maps of India (shown in Fig. 1.2 on page 12).

TOPIC 2: Mapping the Earth – The Globe & System of Coordinates
  • Why a Flat Map Cannot Represent the Earth Accurately:
    • Our planet is not a flat surface; it nearly has the shape of a sphere.
    • ⚠️ Why 'nearly' a sphere? Because it is not a perfect sphere, but is slightly flattened at the poles. However, in practice, we consider it to be spherical.
    • 🧪 The Orange Peel Analogy: Representing a sphere accurately on a flat sheet of paper is impossible. If you peel an orange into 3 or 4 large pieces and try to flatten them on a table, you cannot flatten them without tearing them at the edges!
  • The Globe:
    • A globe is a sphere (generally made of metal, plastic, or cardboard) on which a map is drawn — whether of the Earth, the Moon, Mars, or the stars and constellations in the sky.
    • Because the globe and the Earth have the same spherical shape, a globe better represents the geography of the Earth than a flat map.
  • a) Understanding Coordinates:
    • Example 1 (Market Shop): Saying "Meet me at 6 pm at the 7th shop in the 5th row from the entrance" uses two numbers (7th shop, 5th row) to pinpoint an exact location.
    • Example 2 (Chessboard): Advanced chess players record moves using letters ('a' to 'h' along the main pieces) and numbers (1 to 8 in between the two sides). When White opens by moving the queen's pawn two squares forward, it moves from d2 to d4.
    • Both examples use a system of coordinates — two values that precisely determine any location on a grid.
🧪 Activity Decoded ("LET'S EXPLORE" - Page 13): Chessboard Coordinate Move
  • 🧪 Question: White opened the game by moving the queen's pawn two squares forward from d2 to d4. Using the same terms, write down your move if you play Black and respond with the same move.
  • 💡 Answer: Black's queen sits on file d (square d8), and Black's queen's pawn starts on square d7. Moving it two squares forward towards the centre means the pawn moves from d7 to d5!
TOPIC 3: Latitudes (Parallels) & Climate Zones
  • Poles and the Equator:
    • When you rotate a globe, the two fixed points at the top and bottom are the North Pole and the South Pole.
    • Halfway between the two poles is the Equator, a circle marking the middle of the globe (Fig. 1.3).
  • What is Latitude and a Parallel of Latitude?
    • Latitude measures the distance of a point from the Equator as you travel towards one of the poles.
    • At any point of this travel, an imaginary line running east and west, parallel to the Equator, is called a parallel of latitude.
    • Each parallel of latitude draws a full circle around the Earth.
    • Length of Parallels: The largest circle is the Equator, while the circles marked by parallels of latitude grow smaller as we move northward or southward, becoming mere points at the two poles.
    • Measurement in Degrees: By convention, the Equator is latitude 0° (zero degree), while the latitudes of the two poles are 90° North (90°N) and 90° South (90°S) respectively.
  • 🔥 Connection Between Latitude and Climate:
    1. Torrid (Hot) Zone: Around the Equator, the climate is generally hot (also called 'torrid').
    2. Temperate (Moderate) Zone: As you travel away from the Equator towards either pole (as latitude increases), the climate becomes more moderate (or 'temperate').
    3. Frigid (Cold) Zone: Closer to the North or South Pole, the climate grows colder (or 'frigid').

Comparison Table: The Three Climate Zones by Latitude

Climate Zone Name Location on the Globe Nature of Climate
1. Torrid Zone Around the Equator (0°) (low latitudes). Generally hot throughout the year.
2. Temperate Zone Middle latitudes between the Equator and the Poles. More moderate (neither extremely hot nor extremely cold).
3. Frigid Zone Closer to the North Pole (90°N) and South Pole (90°S) (high latitudes). Extremely cold and icy.
TOPIC 4: Longitudes (Meridians), Grid Lines & Ujjayinī Prime Meridian
  • What are Meridians of Longitude?
    • If you travel from the North Pole to the South Pole by the shortest possible line on the globe, these north-to-south lines are called meridians of longitude (Fig. 1.3).
    • Unlike parallels of latitude (which are full circles of different sizes), meridians of longitude are all half-circles of equal length running from one pole to the other (each meridian's length is exactly half of the Equator's length).
  • The Prime Meridian (0° Longitude):
    • To measure longitudes, we need a reference point called the Prime Meridian.
    • It is also called the Greenwich Meridian because, in the year 1884, some nations decided that the meridian passing through Greenwich (an area of London in England) would become the international standard for the Prime Meridian.
    • It is marked as 0° longitude.
  • How Longitude is Measured:
    • Longitude measures the distance from the Prime Meridian as you travel east or west along the Equator, expressed in degrees.
    • Moving westward or eastward from 0°, it increases in value from 0° to 180° with the letter 'W' (West) or 'E' (East) added.
    • NCERT Examples (Round Figures): New York's longitude is 74°W, Delhi's is 77°E, and Tokyo's is 140°E.
    • ⚠️ The 180° Line Rule ("DON'T MISS OUT" - Page 16): On the globe, 180°W and 180°E are the exact same longitude line (opposite the Prime Meridian); therefore, this longitude is noted simply as 180°, omitting the letter 'W' or 'E'.
  • Coordinates, Grid Lines & Hemispheres (Fig. 1.4):
    • Latitude and longitude together are the two coordinates of a place (e.g., "Delhi lies at 29°N latitude and 77°E longitude").
    • All parallels of latitude and meridians of longitude together constitute a grid for the globe and are called grid lines.
    • Dividing the Earth into Hemispheres (Fig. 1.4):
      • The Equator (0° latitude) divides the Earth into the Northern Hemisphere and the Southern Hemisphere.
      • The Prime Meridian (0° longitude) (along with the 180° meridian) divides the Earth into the Western Hemisphere and the Eastern Hemisphere.
  • 🔥 India's Latitudinal and Longitudinal Extent (Fig. 1.6 - Page 19):
    • According to the Survey of India map (Fig. 1.6), India's mainland latitudes extend approximately from 8°N to 37°N, and longitudes extend approximately from 68°E to 97°E.

🧪 In-Box Info Decoded ("DON'T MISS OUT" - Pages 17–18): India's Ancient Prime Meridian – Madhya Rekha (Ujjayinī)
  • 🧪 Historical Fact: The Greenwich Meridian (1884) is not the first prime meridian! Many centuries before Europe, India had a prime meridian of its own called madhya rekha (or 'middle line').
  • 👀 Key Details (Fig. 1.5 - Page 18):
    • It passed through the city of Ujjayinī (today Ujjain) at 75.8°E longitude, which was a reputed centre for astronomy over many centuries.
    • Varāhamihira, a famous Indian astronomer, lived and worked there some 1,500 years ago.
    • Indian astronomers were well aware of latitude, longitude, and the need for a zero/prime meridian; the Ujjayinī meridian became the reference for calculations in all Indian astronomical texts.
    • Ancient Cities Marked on/near the Ujjayinī Meridian (Fig. 1.5): From north to south: Kurukṣhetra, Rohitaka / Rohtak (near Delhi), Mālavangara / Nagar, Ujjayinī / Ujjain, Māhiṣhmatī / Maheshwar, and Kumārī / Kanyakumari.
  • 💡 Why are some cities slightly away from the line on the map? Because measuring longitude required accurate timekeeping, which was not as precise in ancient times as it is today.

TOPIC 5: Understanding Time Zones, IST & the International Date Line
  • 🔥 Earth's Rotation & the 15° = 1 Hour Rule:
    • The Earth spins around its axis from west to east, making a full turn of 360° every 24 hours.
    • Dividing 360° by 24 hours gives 15° per hour (15 × 24 = 360), or 1° every 4 minutes.
    • Moving Eastward from the Prime Meridian (0°): We add 1 hour of local time for every 15° meridian. If it is 12 pm (noon) at Greenwich (0°), it is 1 pm at 15°E, 2 pm at 30°E, 3 pm at 45°E, and so on (Fig. 1.7).
    • Moving Westward from the Prime Meridian (0°): We subtract 1 hour for every 15° meridian. When it is 12 pm at Greenwich (0°), it is 11 am at 15°W, 10 am at 30°W, and 9 am at 45°W (Fig. 1.7).
  • Local Time vs. Standard Time & Indian Standard Time (IST):
    • Local Time: The time of a place calculated directly from its own meridian of longitude based on the position of the Sun.
    • Why Standard Time is Needed: It would be extremely inconvenient for a country to use many different local times across its cities! Therefore, most countries adopt a single standard time based on a central meridian passing through them (in India, 82.5°E).
    • 🔥 Indian Standard Time (IST): IST is 5 hours 30 minutes (or 5.5 hours) ahead of the local time at Greenwich, called Greenwich Mean Time (GMT).
  • Time Zones on the World Map (Fig. 1.8):
    • Standard times globally are organised into time zones that broadly follow 15° longitudinal bands.
    • Why are time zone lines on the world map crooked/not fully straight? Because they have to respect each country's standard time and therefore tend to follow international borders (and domestic administrative boundaries).
    • Numbers inside countries on the time zone map show hours to be added to GMT (+ sign) or subtracted from GMT (− sign).
  • 🔥 Countries with Multiple Time Zones ("DON'T MISS OUT" - Page 22):
    • Some countries like Russia, Canada, or the USA are too large from east to west to have a single time zone!
    • The USA has 6 time zones, and Russia has 11 time zones — meaning if you travel across Russia from east to west, you need to readjust your watch 10 times!
  • 🔥 The International Date Line (IDL - Pages 22–23):
    • Opposite the Prime Meridian (0°), at approximately 180° longitude, lies the International Date Line (IDL).
    • The +12 and −12 time zones touch each other at this line.
    • Date Change Rule When Crossing the IDL:
      • If you cross the IDL travelling eastward → you subtract a day (e.g., from Monday back to Sunday).
      • If you cross the IDL travelling westward → you add a day (e.g., from Sunday forward to Monday).
    • Why is the IDL 'approximately' at 180° (zig-zag/deviated)? It deviates in places to avoid dividing some countries or island groups into two different calendar days!
🧪 Activity Decoded ("LET'S EXPLORE" - Page 21): Porbandar (Gujarat) vs. Tinsukia (Assam)
  • 🧪 Scenario: Two friends — one in Porbandar (Gujarat) in the west and one in Tinsukia (Assam) in the east — speak on the phone in the late afternoon. In Assam, the sun has already set and it is dark, while in Gujarat it is still full daylight! (Longitude difference ≈ 30°).
  • 👀 Part 1: Why is it dark in Assam while still daylight in Gujarat?
    Because the Earth rotates from west to east, eastern places like Assam (Tinsukia) face the Sun first, experiencing sunrise, noon, and sunset much earlier than western places like Gujarat (Porbandar).
  • 🔢 Part 2: Calculate the Difference in Local Time:
    Given longitudinal difference = 30°. Since 15° of longitude = 1 hour of time difference:
    Local Time Difference = 30° ÷ 15° per hour = 2 hours. (Tinsukia's local time is 2 hours ahead of Porbandar's local time).
  • 💡 Part 3: Explain the Difference Between Local Time and Standard Time Using This Example:
    Even though Tinsukia's local time (based on the Sun's position in the sky) is 2 hours ahead of Porbandar's local time, both friends' watches show the exact same clock time (say, 5:30 PM) because the entire country of India follows one common Standard Time (IST)!
💡 Memory Hacks for Time & Date Calculations
  • Rule 1 (Longitude to Time): EGA - WLS
    East Gains Add (Going East → Add time) | West Loses Subtract (Going West → Subtract time).
  • Rule 2 (Crossing the International Date Line):
    Travelling Westward across the IDL (towards Asia/India) → Add 1 Day (+1 day).
    Travelling Eastward across the IDL (towards Americas) → Subtract 1 Day (−1 day).

✍️ Complete NCERT Solutions (In-Text + End-of-Chapter Exercises)

Part A: "The Big Questions" (Page 7)

Big Question 1: What is a map and how do we use it? What are its main components?
View CBSE Topper-Style Answer
  • What is a Map: A map is a representation, or a drawing, of some area (small like a village/town or large like a country/world) viewed as if looking at the surface from the top.
  • How We Use It: We use maps like a treasure guide to locate places, find routes between locations, and study physical features, political boundaries, or specific themes.
  • Three Main Components of a Map:
    1. Distance (Scale): The ratio between map distance and actual ground distance (e.g., 1 cm = 500 m), allowing large areas to fit on paper.
    2. Direction: Indicated using the four cardinal directions (North, East, South, West) and four intermediate directions (NE, SE, SW, NW).
    3. Symbols: Standardised marks, letters, and colours (fixed in India by the Survey of India) used to represent buildings, roads, railways, and water bodies in limited space.
Big Question 2: What are coordinates? How can latitude and longitude be used to mark any location on the Earth?
View CBSE Topper-Style Answer
  • Coordinates: Coordinates are a pair of values (like row and shop number in a market, or file letter and rank number on a chessboard) that pinpoint an exact position on a grid.
  • Marking Any Location Using Latitude and Longitude:
    • Latitude gives the angular distance in degrees north or south of the Equator (0°), up to 90°N or 90°S.
    • Longitude gives the angular distance in degrees east or west of the Prime Meridian (0°), up to 180°E or 180°W.
    • The intersection of a place's parallel of latitude and meridian of longitude on the globe's grid uniquely locates that place on Earth (for example, Delhi is located at approximately 29°N latitude and 77°E longitude).
Big Question 3: How are local time and standard time related to longitude?
View CBSE Topper-Style Answer
  • Earth's Rotation & Longitude: The Earth spins 360° from west to east in 24 hours, which means it covers 15° of longitude every hour (or 1° in 4 minutes).
  • Relation to Local Time: Every meridian of longitude has its own local time based on the Sun's position. Moving eastward from the Prime Meridian (0°), local time increases by 1 hour for every 15°, while moving westward, it decreases by 1 hour for every 15°.
  • Relation to Standard Time: To avoid confusion from multiple local times within the same country, nations adopt a single standard time based on a central meridian of longitude passing through them (e.g., Indian Standard Time or IST is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of GMT).

Q2. Why is it 5:30 pm in India when it is 12 pm or noon in London?
View CBSE Topper-Style Answer

  • 3. Time Difference (+5 Hours 30 Minutes): Because Indian Standard Time (IST) is fixed at 5 hours 30 minutes (5.5 hours) ahead of Greenwich Mean Time (GMT) in London, when it is 12:00 pm (noon) in London, we add 5 hours 30 minutes to get 5:30 pm in India.

Q5. What is the difference between local time and standard time? Discuss it in groups, with each group writing an answer in 100 to 150 words. Compare the answers.
View CBSE Topper-Style Answer (130 Words)

Local time is the time of a specific place determined by the apparent position of the Sun in the sky and its exact meridian of longitude. Since the Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours (15° every hour), places on different longitudes have different local times. For example, Tinsukia in Assam (30° east of Porbandar in Gujarat) has a local time that is two hours ahead of Porbandar.

However, if every town or state used its own local time, railway timetables, flights, offices, and communication across a country would become chaotic. To solve this problem, a country adopts a uniform standard time based on a central meridian passing through it. In India, we follow Indian Standard Time (IST), which is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of Greenwich Mean Time (GMT), ensuring the whole nation uses one common clock time.

  • Reason: Local time depends only on longitude (not on latitude), because a meridian of longitude runs from North Pole to South Pole and all places on the same meridian face the Sun at the exact same moment. Since both Delhi and Bengaluru lie on almost the same meridian of longitude (77°E), their local time is identical.
Q7. Mark the following statements as true or false; explain your answers with a sentence or two.
View All 6 True/False Solutions with Explanations
  1. "All parallels of latitude have the same length."
    → ❌ False. The Equator (0°) is the longest circle of latitude, and the circles marked by parallels of latitude grow smaller as we move northward or southward towards the poles.
  2. "The length of a meridian of longitude is half of that of the Equator."
    → ✅ True. While the Equator is a full circle around the spherical Earth, every meridian of longitude is a half-circle running from the North Pole to the South Pole, making its length half that of the Equator.
  3. "The South Pole has a latitude of 90°S."
    → ✅ True. By convention, the Equator is 0° latitude and the South Pole is at the southernmost point at a latitude of 90° South (90°S).
  4. "In Assam, the local time and the IST are identical."
    → ❌ False. Assam lies further east (around 92°E–95°E) of India's standard meridian, so the Sun rises and sets earlier there; hence Assam's local time is ahead of Indian Standard Time (IST).
  5. "Lines separating the time zones are identical with meridians of longitude."
    → ❌ False. Although time zones broadly follow 15° meridians of longitude, the actual lines separating time zones are not straight because they bend to follow international and national borders so countries aren't split inconveniently.
  6. "The Equator is also a parallel of latitude."
    → ✅ True. The Equator is the reference (0°) parallel of latitude and is the largest circle of latitude on the globe.

4. Short Answer Questions (3 Marks Each – 5 Questions)

High Frequency PYQ Concept Q1. Explain the connection between latitude and climate zones on the Earth.
View Answer
  1. Torrid (Hot) Zone: Around the Equator (0°), the climate is generally very hot and is called 'torrid'.
  2. Temperate (Moderate) Zone: As we travel away from the Equator towards either pole (as latitude increases), the climate becomes more moderate and is called 'temperate'.
  3. Frigid (Cold) Zone: Closer to the North Pole (90°N) or South Pole (90°S), the climate grows extremely cold and is called 'frigid'.
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q2. Differentiate between Parallels of Latitude and Meridians of Longitude (any three points).
View Answer
  1. Direction: Parallels of latitude run east to west parallel to the Equator, whereas meridians of longitude run north to south from pole to pole.
  2. Shape & Length: Parallels of latitude are full circles that shrink in size from the Equator to the poles, whereas meridians of longitude are all half-circles of equal length (half the Equator).
  3. Reference Line & Range: Latitudes are measured from the Equator (0°) up to 90°N and 90°S, whereas longitudes are measured from the Prime Meridian (0°, Greenwich) up to 180°E and 180°W.
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q3. How does the Earth's rotation help us calculate local time using longitudes? Illustrate with an example.
View Answer
  • Rotation Rate: The Earth rotates on its axis from west to east, completing a full circle of 360° in 24 hours. This equals 15° of longitude per hour (15 × 24 = 360).
  • Eastward Addition: For every 15° we move eastward from the Prime Meridian (0°), we add 1 hour to Greenwich time (e.g., if it is 12 pm at 0°, it is 1 pm at 15°E and 2 pm at 30°E).
  • Westward Subtraction: For every 15° we move westward from 0°, we subtract 1 hour (e.g., 11 am at 15°W and 10 am at 30°W).
High Frequency PYQ Concept Q4. What is the International Date Line (IDL)? Explain what happens when a traveller crosses it eastward vs. westward, and why the line is not straight.
View Answer
  • Definition: The International Date Line (IDL) is an imaginary line located at approximately 180° longitude, directly opposite the Prime Meridian (0°), where the +12 and −12 time zones meet.
  • Rule for Crossing: Crossing the IDL travelling eastward requires subtracting a day (e.g., Monday to Sunday), while crossing it travelling westward requires adding a day (e.g., Sunday to Monday).
  • Why It Deviates: The IDL is not completely straight along 180° because it deviates in places to avoid dividing any country or island group into two different calendar days.

View 5-Mark Step-by-Step Answer
  • 1. System of Coordinates (1 Mark): Just like locating a shop by row and shop number or a square on a chessboard (e.g., d4), any place on Earth can be pinpointed using two spherical coordinates: latitude and longitude.
  • 2. Parallels of Latitude (1 Mark): Imaginary east-west circles parallel to the Equator (0°) that measure angular distance north or south up to the poles (90°N and 90°S).
  • 3. Meridians of Longitude (1 Mark): Imaginary north-south half-circles running from pole to pole that measure angular distance east or west (0° to 180°) from the Prime Meridian (0° at Greenwich, 1884).

View 5-Mark Step-by-Step Answer
  • 1. Earth's Rotation (15° = 1 Hour) (1 Mark): Rotating from west to east, the Earth completes 360° in 24 hours, covering 15° of longitude every hour. Moving east adds 1 hour per 15°; moving west subtracts 1 hour per 15°.
  • 2. Porbandar vs. Tinsukia Local Time Difference (1.5 Marks): Tinsukia (Assam) lies about 30° east of Porbandar (Gujarat). Since 30° ÷ 15° = 2 hours, Tinsukia's local time is 2 hours ahead, so the sun sets in Assam while it is still daylight in Gujarat.

⚠️ 5 Common "Exam Hall Mistakes" & How to Avoid Them
  1. Mistake 1: Thinking latitudes run North-South because they are marked 'N' and 'S'.
    Fix: Parallels of latitude run East-to-West (like horizontal rungs of a ladder), even though they measure distance North or South of the Equator (0°). Meridians of longitude run North-to-South (Pole to Pole), even though they measure distance East or West of the Prime Meridian!
  2. Mistake 2: Writing 180°E or 180°W for the anti-meridian.
    Fix: Both meet at the exact same line opposite 0°, so NCERT explicitly states it must be written simply as 180° (omitting W or E).
  3. Mistake 3: Calculating a time difference when two cities have different latitudes but the same longitude (like Delhi 29°N, 77°E and Bengaluru 13°N, 77°E).
    Fix: Local time depends only on longitude! Since both cities share 77°E longitude, their local time difference is 0 minutes.
  4. Mistake 4: Reversing the International Date Line (IDL) rule when travelling East vs. West.
    Fix: Crossing the IDL Eastward = Subtract 1 Day (Monday → Sunday); Crossing the IDL Westward = Add 1 Day (Sunday → Monday).
  5. Mistake 5: Confusing PO, PTO, and PS in Survey of India map symbols.
    Fix: PO = Post Office | PTO = Post & Telegraph Office | PS = Police Station | RS = Railway Station.
