2.1 Earth's Diverse Surface & The Three Landforms Journey

Humans, like most mammals, spend their lives on land. But land does not look the same everywhere. In some places, it soars high into the clouds; elsewhere, it spreads out as flat green fields; and in other regions, it rises like a high rocky table.

📖 What is a Landform?

A landform is a natural physical feature or shape on the surface of planet Earth. Major examples include mountains, plateaus, plains, deserts, and valleys.

How do landforms take shape? They form over millions of years through two natural forces:

  • Internal Forces: Heat deep inside the Earth moves tectonic plates, causing the crust to buckle, fracture, or erupt as volcanoes, pushing land upwards into mountains and plateaus.
  • External Forces: Wind, rain, flowing rivers, and glaciers wear down rocks (erosion) and carry sediments to lower areas (deposition), creating flat plains.

🚗 An Imaginary Journey Across Three Landforms

The NCERT textbook takes us on a road trip across northern India illustrating the three major landforms:

  1. Starting Point — Chhota Nagpur (Jharkhand): An elevated, rocky upland with rolling hills, deep Sal forests, scenic waterfalls (like Hundru Falls), and rich iron and coal mines. This is a plateau.
  2. Middle Leg — Prayagraj (Uttar Pradesh): The rocky hills vanish into an endless, flat expanse of fertile green farming fields and wide rivers, where the Ganga and Yamuna meet at Triveni Sangam. This is a plain.
  3. Final Destination — Almora (Uttarakhand): The flat plain ends abruptly as towering Himalayan slopes rise into the sky. Roads wind along steep cliffs, the air turns cool, pine and deodar forests sway in the breeze, and snow-capped peaks shine in the distance. This is a mountain.
🧭 The Two Big Questions of Chapter 3 Decoded
  1. What are the major types of landforms, and what is their significance to life and culture?
    Earth's primary landforms are mountains, plateaus, and plains (plus deserts and coasts). They shape climate, soils, crops, and occupations. Culturally, people revere mountain peaks as abodes of gods, celebrate river confluences, and adapt their folk traditions to each landform.
  2. What are the challenges and opportunities of life in each landform?
    Mountains offer fresh air, mineral-rich rivers, and tourism, but pose steep slopes, landslides, and cold. Plains offer flat land and fertile soil for farming, but face floods and groundwater depletion. Plateaus hold vast mineral wealth, but often have rocky soil.

📏 Altitude, Sea Level, and Snow

  • Altitude: The vertical height of an object or landform measured above sea level. For example, Mount Everest stands at an altitude of 8,848 m.
  • Mean Sea Level: The average surface level of the world's oceans. Because all oceans are connected, sea level provides a uniform global benchmark of 0 metres.
  • Precipitation: Water reaching the ground from the atmosphere in any form — rain, snow, or hail.
  • What is Snow? Snow is water precipitation in a solid crystalline state. At high altitudes where temperatures drop below 0°C, water vapour freezes directly into delicate ice crystals that fall as soft snowflakes.
  • Why Melting Snow Feeds Rivers: Permanent snow and ice (glaciers) on high peaks melt slowly during warm summers, feeding perennial rivers like the Ganga, Yamuna, Indus, and Brahmaputra all year round!

2.2 Mountains — The Giant Towers of the Earth

Mountains are highlands rising significantly above surrounding land, recognized by a broad base, steep slopes, and a narrow summit (peak).

⛰️ Mountain vs Hill
  • Hills: Highlands of lower elevation, gentler slopes, and rounded tops.
  • Mountains: Highlands of much greater altitude, steep slopes, and sharp pointed peaks.

🗺️ Mountain Ranges of the World

Mountains usually form long continuous chains called mountain ranges:

  • Himalayas (Asia): Stretches 2,400 km across India, Nepal, Bhutan, and China; home to the highest peaks.
  • Andes (South America): The world's longest continental range (~7,000 km).
  • Alps (Europe): Extends ~1,200 km across central Europe.
  • Rockies (North America): Stretches over 4,800 km across Canada and the US.
  • Urals (Russia): Runs north-south, forming the natural border between Europe and Asia.

🌋 How Mountains Form: The Three Types

  1. Fold Mountains (Compressional Force):
    Formed when two tectonic plates push towards each other, crumpling sediment layers upwards into ridges and valleys.
    • Young Fold Mountains: Formed recently (millions of years ago); have tall, sharp peaks and deep valleys, and are still rising. Examples: Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies.
    • Old Fold Mountains: Formed hundreds of millions of years ago; smoothed and rounded by long erosion. Examples: Aravalli Range (India) and Ural Mountains (Russia).
  2. Block Mountains (Faulting & Cracks):
    Formed when crust fractures along fault lines.
    • Horst: The uplifted block forming the block mountain.
    • Graben: The sunken block forming a rift valley.
    • Examples: Vosges Mountain (Horst) and Rhine Valley (Graben) in Europe; Satpura-Vindhya and Narmada Valley in India.
  3. Volcanic Mountains (Lava Eruptions):
    Formed when molten rock (magma) erupts onto the surface as lava and ash through a vent. Over many eruptions, cooled layers build a conical peak with a summit crater.
    • Examples: Mount Kilimanjaro (Africa), Mount Fuji (Japan), Mount Vesuvius (Italy).

🌲 Mountain Environment & Montane Forests

  • Montane Forest: Grows on mountain slopes, dominated by coniferous trees such as pines, firs, spruce, and deodar.
    • Conical Shape: Flexible sloping branches allow heavy snow to slide off easily.
    • Needle Leaves: Thin waxy needles reduce water loss and withstand freezing winds.
  • Alpine Tundra: Above the tree line, forests yield to cold-hardy grasses, mosses (soft green cushion plants), and lichens (crusty organisms clinging to bare rocks).
Kālidāsa's Kumārasambhava (Verse 1.1 & 1.15):
"In the north rises Himālaya, the Lord of mountains, like a living god, who measures the Earth and stretches from the western to the eastern oceans. … From it the wind comes down, carrying spray from descending Gangā, shaking the deodar trees, opening the peacocks' tail feathers and cooling the mountain people after they hunt deer."

Analysis: The "western to eastern oceans" are the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. The Gangā is celebrated for descending from glaciers to nourish northern India.

🦅 Mountain Fauna (Wildlife)

Mountain animals have thick fur, dense fat, and cloven hooves to survive freezing cold, thin air, and steep cliffs:

  • Textbook Species: Snow leopard ("Ghost of the Mountains"), Peregrine falcon (fastest bird), Canadian lynx, Golden eagle, Himalayan tahr, Ibex (curved horns), Mountain hare, Yak (shaggy coat, provides milk and wool), Grey fox, and Black bear.
🌊 River Ganga in the Mountains

The Ganga originates in the Himalayas, spanning nearly 2,500 km. It forms at Devprayag where the Bhagirathi (from Gangotri Glacier) meets the Alaknanda.

  • Himalayan Tributaries: Yamuna, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi.
  • Peninsular Tributaries: Son (Sone), originating in the Vindhyas.

🌾 Life, Farming, and Hazards in Mountains

  • Terrace Farming: Carving wide flat steps into hillsides with stone retaining walls slows rainwater run-off, prevents soil erosion, and retains water for crops (rice, barley, potatoes, apples).
  • Pastoral Herding: Rearing sheep, goats (Pashmina), and yaks; herders practice transhumance (moving to high alpine meadows in summer and returning to valleys in winter).
  • Tourism & Sports: Pure air and scenery attract visitors for skiing, trekking, mountaineering, and paragliding, as well as pilgrimages (Badrinath, Kedarnath).
  • Mountaineering Icons:
    • Bachendri Pal: First Indian woman to climb Everest (1984); awarded Padma Shri (1984) and Padma Bhushan (2019).
    • Arunima Sinha: Lost a leg in a train accident at 22; trained by Bachendri Pal; conquered Everest in 2013 (first female amputee) and the highest peaks of all 7 continents including Mt Vinson (Antarctica); Padma Shri (2015).
  • Mountain Hazards: Avalanches (sudden snow/rock tumble), Landslides (falling mud/rock), Cloudbursts (violent downpours >100 mm/h), Flash floods, heavy snowfall, and uncontrolled tourism.
  • Sacred Peaks: Mount Everest is revered as Chomolungma ("Mother Goddess of the World") and Sagarmatha ("Goddess of the Sky"). Mount Kailash (Tibet) is sacred to Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Bon.
2.3 Plateaus — The Lofty Tablelands & Mineral Storehouses

A plateau is an elevated landform that rises steeply above the surrounding lower land, featuring a more or less flat or rolling top. Geographers often call them tablelands.

⛰️ Key Characteristics of Plateaus
  • Flat or Rolling Top: Unlike mountains that taper into sharp peaks, plateaus have broad, level surfaces.
  • Steep Slopes (Escarpments): At least one side drops sharply into lower plains as a steep cliff.
  • Variable Elevation: Ranges from a few hundred metres (Chhota Nagpur at 600–900 m) to thousands of metres (Tibetan Plateau at 4,500 m).

🌏 Major Plateaus of the World

  • Tibetan Plateau ("Roof of the World"): The world's highest and largest plateau (average altitude 4,500 m, stretching 2,500 km east-west, equal to Chandigarh to Kanyakumari). It is the source of major Asian rivers (Indus, Brahmaputra, Yangtze).
  • Deccan Plateau (India): One of Earth's oldest plateaus, formed millions of years ago by volcanic basalt lava flows.
  • Chhota Nagpur Plateau (India): Located in eastern India (Jharkhand, Odisha), famous for huge reserves of iron ore, coal, and manganese.
  • East African Plateau: Spans Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda; renowned for gold and diamond mining.

⛏️ "Storehouses of Minerals" & Soils

  • Mineral Storehouses: Ancient volcanic and metamorphic processes concentrated rich ores in plateaus, making mining a dominant economic activity (Chhota Nagpur iron/coal, African gold/diamonds).
  • Rocky Soil vs Black Lava Soil: While many older plateaus have thin, stony, less fertile soils, volcanic lava plateaus (like the Deccan Trap) weathered into deep, moisture-retentive Black Soil (Regur), ideal for cultivating cotton and sugarcane.
  • Spectacular Waterfalls: Rivers flowing across hard tablelands plunge over cliff edges into deep valleys:
    • Nohkalikai Falls: Plunges 340 metres from Cherrapunji Plateau (Meghalaya) — India's tallest plunge waterfall (Fig. 3.7).
    • Jog Falls: Drops 253 metres on the Sharavati River (Karnataka).
    • Hundru Falls: Drops 98 metres on the Subarnarekha River (Chhota Nagpur).
    • Victoria Falls: Massive waterfall on the Zambezi River (southern Africa).
2.4 Plains — The Flat, Fertile Cradles of Humanity

Plains are extensive, low-lying flatlands with gently undulating surfaces, generally not more than 300 metres above sea level, without high hills or deep valleys.

🌊 Formation of River Floodplains
  1. Erosion: Mountain rivers erode rocks into fine sand, clay, and silt called sediments (alluvium).
  2. Transport: Rivers carry millions of tonnes of sediments down into lowlands.
  3. Deposition: In flat plains, water slows down. During seasonal floods, rivers overflow their banks, depositing fresh layers of nutrient-rich silt that create fertile floodplains.

🌾 Life in the Ganga Plain (Gangetic Plain)

  • Dense Population: Home to about 40 crore (400 million) people — over one-fourth of India's population. Flat terrain allows easy construction of homes, roads, and railways.
  • Agriculture & Crops: Major crops include food grains (rice, wheat, maize, barley, millets) and fibre crops (jute, cotton, hemp).
  • Irrigation & Groundwater Challenge: Agriculture has shifted from traditional rainfed farming to canal and tube-well irrigation. While food production soared, excessive pumping has caused severe groundwater depletion and chemical pollution.
  • River Navigation: Gentle gradients allow calm river flow, supporting passenger boats and cargo transport on the Ganga (Fig. 3.10).
🙏 Sacred Confluences: The Prayags of India

A confluence is the meeting point of two or more rivers, revered across India as sacred:

  • The Panch Prayag (Uttarakhand on the Alaknanda):
    1. Vishnuprayag (+ Dhauliganga) • 2. Nandaprayag (+ Nandakini) • 3. Karnaprayag (+ Pindar) • 4. Rudraprayag (+ Mandakini) • 5. Devprayag (+ Bhagirathi = the Ganga).
  • Prayagraj (Triveni Sangam): Confluence of the Ganga, Yamuna, and mythical Saraswati; host to the grand Kumbh Mela.
2.5 Deserts — Lands of Aridity & Human Resilience

A desert is a large, dry expanse of land that receives very little precipitation (typically < 25 cm per year), featuring scarce vegetation and unique wildlife adaptations.

🔥 Hot Deserts vs ❄️ Cold Deserts

  • Hot Deserts: Blazing daytime heat, sand dunes, and high evaporation. Examples: Sahara Desert (Africa, largest hot desert) and Thar Desert (northwestern India).
  • Cold Deserts: Freezing temperatures, high winds, and precipitation falling as light snow. Examples: Gobi Desert (Asia), Ladakh (India), and Antarctica (largest cold desert on Earth).
  • Adaptations: Cacti store water in fleshy stems and use sharp spines; camels ("Ship of the Desert") have humps storing fat, padded feet, and long eyelashes.
  • Human Resilience: Resilience is the capacity to adapt to hardships and overcome difficulties. Thar communities thrive through folk music, colorful crafts, camel pastoralism, and traditional rainwater harvesting (underground tankas and stepwells).
2.6 Coastal Lands & The Five Sangam Tinais (Ancient Indian Ecology)

Over 2,000 years ago, ancient Tamil poet-scholars classified landscapes into Five Tiṇais (Tinais), linking physical geography with deities, livelihoods, emotions, and flowers:

Tiṇai Landscape Deity Chief Occupation Emotional Theme Flower
Kuriñji Mountains & Hills Murugan Hunting, honey gathering, digging roots Lovers' secret union Kurinji (blooms every 12 years)
Mullai Pastoral Forests & Grasslands Mayon (Vishnu) Cattle rearing, dairy farming Patient waiting of the wife Mullai (wild jasmine)
Marudam Fertile Agricultural Plains Vendan (Indra) Settled wet paddy farming, canals Lovers' quarrel & reconciliation Marudam (red water-lily)
Neydal Coastal Shores & Beaches Varunan Fishing, salt pans, seafaring trade Anxious longing & grief at sea Neydal (blue water-lily)
Pālai Parched Desert Wasteland Korravai (Durga) Guarding caravans, travel, soldiering Painful separation & danger Palai (ivory tree blossom)
💡 Ecological Harmony

The Tiṇais illustrate the deep harmony between humanity and nature. Pālai was not a separate static landform, but was formed when summer droughts parched the hills of Kuriñji or pastures of Mullai, showing early insight into dynamic ecological change.

📖 Master Glossary of Geographic Terms

Term Definition & Explanation (The 'Why' & 'How') Real-World Example
Landform A natural physical shape or feature on Earth's surface shaped by internal tectonic and external erosional forces. Mountains, Plateaus, Plains, Deserts.
Altitude The vertical height of an object or landform measured upwards from mean sea level. Mount Everest is at 8,848 m altitude.
Mean Sea Level The average surface level of the world's oceans, used as the global zero baseline (0 m) because all oceans connect. Railway station boards show "Height above MSL".
Precipitation Water falling from the atmosphere to Earth in any form — rain, snow, or hail. Monsoon rain in Odisha; snowfall in Shimla.
Snow Solid crystalline water precipitation formed when water vapour freezes below 0°C in cold air. Snow blankets Himalayan summits.
Perennial River A river flowing with water all year round, fed by melting glaciers in dry summers and monsoons in rainy season. The Ganga, Yamuna, Indus, Brahmaputra.
Mountain Range A long continuous chain of mountains stretching across hundreds or thousands of kilometres. Himalayas (Asia), Andes (South America).
Hill A highland of lower elevation, gentler slopes, and rounded tops compared to a mountain. Rolling hills of Meghalaya or Odisha.
Fold Mountain A mountain formed by tectonic plates pushing together, crumpling rock layers upwards into ridges. Young: Himalayas; Old: Aravalli Range.
Block Mountain A mountain formed when Earth's crust fractures along faults and blocks are vertically displaced. Vosges Mountain (Horst), Rhine Valley (Graben).
Horst & Graben The uplifted block forming a mountain is a Horst; the sunken block forming a rift valley is a Graben. Satpura (Horst) & Narmada Valley (Graben).
Volcanic Mountain A conical mountain formed by layers of erupted magma, lava, and ash cooling around a central vent. Mount Kilimanjaro, Mount Fuji.
Montane Forest Mountain slope forests dominated by conical, needle-leafed evergreen conifers (pine, deodar, fir). Pine and deodar forests in Almora.
Terrace Farming Carving flat steps with stone rims into hillsides to prevent soil erosion and retain irrigation water. Terrace fields in Uttarakhand & Himachal.
Transhumance Seasonal migration of pastoral herders up to alpine meadows in summer and down to warm valleys in winter. Gaddi and Bakarwal pastoralists.
Avalanche The sudden, violent rush of a huge mass of snow, ice, and rock down a steep mountainside. Spring snow avalanches in high Himalayas.
Landslide The sudden downward collapse of saturated soil, mud, and boulders down a mountain slope. Monsoon landslides blocking hill highways.
Cloudburst A violent rainstorm dumping >100 mm of rain over a tiny area in a short time, triggering flash floods. Himalayan cloudbursts.
Plateau (Tableland) An elevated upland rising steeply above surrounding land with a relatively flat top and steep cliffs. Tibetan Plateau, Deccan Plateau.
Black Soil (Regur) Fertile, moisture-retaining soil formed by weathered basaltic volcanic lava, ideal for cotton. Black cotton soil of Maharashtra.
Floodplain A flat, fertile lowland along a river, built by deposited river sediments (silt/alluvium) during floods. Ganga Floodplain of northern India.
Confluence (Sangam) The meeting place where two or more flowing rivers join and merge into a single stream. Devprayag (Alaknanda + Bhagirathi); Prayagraj.
Desert A vast, dry landform receiving < 25 cm of precipitation annually, with sparse xerophytic vegetation. Thar (Hot), Ladakh & Antarctica (Cold).
Tiṇai (Tinai) The ancient Tamil Sangam classification of five eco-zones linking geography, livelihoods, deities, and moods. Kurinji, Mullai, Marudam, Neydal, Palai.

In-Text Page 42 Let's Explore — School Surroundings & Journey Across India

(a) Observe your school's surroundings. What landscape do you see? Will it change a few km away or within 50 km? (b) Discuss a journey through India and list the landscapes seen.

View Complete Topper Answer

(a) Observation of School Surroundings:

  • Immediate Surroundings: If situated in an urban plain (e.g. Bhubaneswar, Cuttack, or Delhi), the ground is flat with roads, buildings, and green parks.
  • A few kilometres away (5–10 km): The landscape stays level, opening into agricultural paddy fields or following a river embankment (such as the Mahanadi or Yamuna).
  • Within 50 kilometres: Significant changes appear! For example, travelling 50 km towards Khordha/Chandaka reveals low rolling lateritic hills and rocky scrub forests, while travelling towards Puri leads to coastal sandy soil.

(b) Journey Across India (e.g. Cuttack to Puri):

  • Cuttack Plain: Flat alluvial river delta with green paddy fields and irrigation canals.
  • Dhauli Hills: An isolated rocky hillock rising above the Daya river plain, site of the Peace Pagoda.
  • Puri Coast: Flat sandy dunes, casuarina groves, and the vast roaring shoreline of the Bay of Bengal.
In-Text Page 43 Think About It — What is Snow?

What is snow? Explain how it forms and why it differs from rain and hail.

View Complete Topper Answer
  • Definition: Snow is water precipitation in a solid crystalline state.
  • How it forms: When warm moist air rises up high mountain slopes into air layers where temperatures are below 0°C, water vapour freezes directly into tiny six-sided ice crystals that clump into soft white snowflakes.
  • Difference from Rain and Hail: Rain is liquid water falling above 0°C; hail consists of hard ice pellets formed inside storm updrafts; snow falls as soft, feathery crystals forming a white blanket.
  • Significance: High-altitude snowpacks melt slowly in summer, feeding perennial rivers like the Ganga and Yamuna.
In-Text Page 46 Literary Appreciation — Kālidāsa's Kumārasambhava Verses

📖 Long Answer Questions (5 Marks Each)

5 Marks Q15. Explain the three types of mountains based on formation with diagrams and examples.
View Model Topper Answer
  1. Fold Mountains (Tectonic Compression): Formed when two tectonic plates push against each other, buckling crustal layers upwards into ridges.
    • Young: Sharp peaks, deep valleys, still rising (Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies).
    • Old: Rounded tops worn down by erosion (Aravalli Range, Urals).
  2. Block Mountains (Faulting): Formed when crust cracks along fault lines.
    • Horst: Uplifted block forming mountain (Vosges, Satpura).
    • Graben: Sunken block forming rift valley (Rhine Valley, Narmada Valley).
  3. Volcanic Mountains (Lava Eruptions): Formed by successive eruptions of molten magma through a vent, cooling into conical layers with a summit crater (Mt Kilimanjaro, Mt Fuji, Mt Vesuvius).

⚡ "Exam Hall Mistakes" & 5-Minute Rapid Revision Cheat Sheet

⚠️ 6 Common "Exam Hall Mistakes" & How to Avoid Them
  1. Mistake 1: Confusing a Mountain Peak with a Mountain Range.
    Correction: A range is a long chain of many mountains stretching for hundreds or thousands of kilometres (e.g. the Himalayas, the Andes). A peak (or summit) is a single pointed mountain top within a range (e.g. Mount Everest, Mount Kanchenjunga). Never write "Everest is a mountain range"!
  2. Mistake 2: Calling a Plateau a "Mountain" or assuming it is flat like a plain.
    Correction: A mountain has a sharp, narrow summit (peak) and steep slopes on all sides. A plateau is an elevated tableland with a broad, relatively flat or rolling top and steep cliffs on one or more sides. It is elevated much higher than plains (often 600 m to 4,500 m).
  3. Mistake 3: Writing that the Ganga is a tributary to the Yamuna.
    Correction: It is the opposite! The Yamuna is the largest tributary of the Ganga. The Yamuna empties its waters into the Ganga at Prayagraj (Triveni Sangam). The Ganga is the parent river.
  4. Mistake 4: Believing that all deserts on Earth are scorching hot sandy places.
    Correction: Deserts are scientifically defined by extreme aridity (dryness) — receiving less than 25 cm of precipitation annually — not by heat! There are hot deserts (like the Sahara and Thar) and cold deserts (like the Gobi Desert, Ladakh, and the icy continent of Antarctica).
  5. Mistake 5: Swapping Horst and Graben in Block Mountains.
    Correction: Remember the simple memory trick: Horst stands High (the uplifted block mountain), while Graben has Gone down (the sunken rift valley)!
  6. Mistake 6: Mixing up the Ancient Tamil Sangam Tinais.
    Correction: Always remember the landscape sequence from high peaks to the sea: Kuriñji = High Mountains; Mullai = Pastoral Forests; Marudam = Agricultural Plains; Neydal = Seashore Coast; and Pālai = Scorched Desert Wasteland.
