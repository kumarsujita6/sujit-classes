1. The Tale of the Spice Ship & What is a Magnet?

Chapter 4 opens with a story: Reshma, a curious young schoolgirl from a coastal town in Kerala, is writing an adventure story for her grandmother's 60th birthday. Her story describes an ancient merchant ship carrying cardamom, cinnamon, and black pepper from the Malabar coast across the choppy Arabian Sea.

How did ancient sailors find their way across vast, featureless oceans on dark, cloudy nights when no stars were visible? They used a magical black stone called a Lodestone (natural magnet of iron oxide / magnetite) suspended from a string, or a floating magnetized needle!

Key Definition: Magnet
A magnet is an object made of certain materials that creates an invisible magnetic field around itself, attracting magnetic materials like iron, nickel, cobalt, and steel, and exerting forces of attraction or repulsion on other magnets.

2. Magnetic vs Non-Magnetic Materials (Activity 4.1)

When you bring a magnet near different objects in your classroom, you immediately observe a sharp divide:

Category Scientific Definition Everyday Examples
Magnetic Materials Materials that are attracted towards a magnet and can be magnetized. Iron nails, steel paperclips, safety pins, nickel coins, cobalt blades, sewing needles.
Non-Magnetic Materials Materials that are NOT attracted towards a magnet and cannot be magnetized. Plastic ruler, wooden pencil, glass tumbler, rubber eraser, aluminum foil, copper wire, paper, cotton cloth.
Common Student Misconception to Avoid:
Many students assume that "all metals are attracted by magnets". This is completely FALSE!
Metals like Aluminum, Copper, Brass, Gold, and Silver are completely NON-MAGNETIC! A magnet only attracts specific ferromagnetic metals (Iron, Nickel, Cobalt, and Steel).

3. Poles of a Magnet: Where is Strength Concentrated?

Is a bar magnet equally strong all over its body? Let us look at Activity 4.2:

Activity 4.2: Rolling a Bar Magnet over Steel U-Clips / Iron Filings

  • Procedure: Spread a large heap of small steel U-clips or fine iron filings uniformly on a flat sheet of white paper. Roll a bar magnet over the heap and gently lift it up.
  • Observation: Almost all the clips cluster in dense, prickly clumps at the two opposite ends of the bar magnet! In the middle section, almost no clips stick (or only 1 or 2).
  • Scientific Conclusion: The magnetic attractive power is concentrated at the two extreme ends of the magnet. These regions of maximum attraction are called the Poles of the Magnet.
The Golden Law of Magnetic Poles:
Magnetic poles ALWAYS exist in inseparable pairs!
Every magnet, regardless of its shape (bar, ring, horseshoe, disc, or button), always has two poles: a North Pole (N) and a South Pole (S).
What if you snap a bar magnet in half? You do NOT get an isolated North pole and an isolated South pole! The moment it breaks, two new poles instantly appear at the broken ends, creating two complete, fully functional smaller magnets! Single magnetic monopoles do not exist in nature.

4. Finding Directions & Earth as a Giant Cosmic Magnet

Tie a cotton thread around the exact centre of a bar magnet and hang it freely from a wooden stand (Activity 4.3). Rotate it gently and let it come to rest.

  • Observation: No matter how many times you spin it, the magnet always settles in the exact same orientation: North-South direction!
  • North-Seeking Pole (North Pole): The end of the magnet pointing towards the Earth's geographic North.
  • South-Seeking Pole (South Pole): The end pointing towards the Earth's geographic South.

Why Does a Freely Suspended Magnet Always Point North-South?

Because planet Earth itself behaves like an enormous, churning bar magnet!

  • Deep inside the Earth's molten outer core, moving liquid iron creates a gigantic magnetic field.
  • Earth's Magnetic South Pole is located near the geographic North Pole, and Earth's Magnetic North Pole is located near the geographic South Pole!
  • Since unlike poles attract, the North pole of our bar magnet is magnetically drawn to the Earth's magnetic South pole located at the top of the globe (geographic North)!

5. How to Care for and Safely Store Magnets

Magnets do not stay strong forever if abused. They can lose their magnetic power (a process called demagnetization).

How Magnets Lose Their Power (Causes of Demagnetization):
  1. Heating: Heating a magnet over a flame randomizes the aligned magnetic atomic domains.
  2. Hammering: Hitting a magnet with a hammer shatters internal magnetic alignment.
  3. Dropping: Dropping magnets repeatedly onto hard stone floors knocks atoms out of alignment.
  4. Near Electronics: Storing magnets near mobile phones, laptops, debit/credit cards, and television sets can corrupt electronic memories and erase magnetic strips.

How to Store Bar Magnets Properly:

  • Always store bar magnets in pairs with unlike poles side-by-side (North pole beside South pole).
  • Place a non-magnetic piece of wood between the two magnets to prevent direct clash.
  • Place two pieces of soft iron (called keepers) across both ends. The keepers channel magnetic field lines into a complete closed loop, preserving magnetic strength for decades!

  • (iii) Iron filings mostly stick in the middle of a bar magnet when brought near them: FALSE [ F ]
    Correction: Filings cluster mostly at the two end poles where magnetic attraction is concentrated. The middle has virtually zero attraction.
  • (iv) A freely suspended bar magnet always aligns with the north-south direction: TRUE [ T ]
    Reason: Earth's geomagnetic field guides the magnetic axis along the geographic North-South direction.

NCERT Q4 (Page 75): Atharv rolled a bar magnet over U-clips. Which option in Table 4.3 is likely to be his observation?

Correct Option: Option (i) — Position A: 10, Position B: 2, Position C: 10.

Scientific Justification:

  • Positions A and C represent the two extreme Poles of the bar magnet (North and South), where the magnetic attractive force is at its maximum intensity. Hence, a high number of clips (10 clips each) cling to A and C.

NCERT Q5 (Page 75): Reshma has three identical metal bars (two magnets, one iron piece). How to identify which two are magnets without other tools?

Method (Using the "Repulsion is the Sure Test" Principle):

  1. Label the three bars Bar 1, Bar 2, and Bar 3. Each bar has two ends (let's call them End A and End B).
  2. Take Bar 1 and bring its End A close to both ends of Bar 2, and then close to both ends of Bar 3. Repeat by testing every pair against each other.
  3. How to identify:
    • The plain iron bar will show attraction to both ends of any magnet and will NEVER show repulsion to anything!
    • When the two true magnets are brought together, one combination of ends will show REPULSION (pushing away) because like poles repel!
  4. Conclusion: The two bars that exhibit mutual repulsion at one of their end pairings are the two magnets. The third bar, which only ever gets attracted and never repels, is the plain iron piece!
NCERT Q6 (Page 75): How can you find the poles of an unmarked magnet using a marked magnet?

Step-by-Step Procedure:

  1. Take the marked magnet and identify its known North pole (N).
  2. Bring the marked North pole close to one end (End 1) of the unmarked magnet:
    • Case A (Repulsion): If End 1 is repelled (pushed away) by the marked North pole, then End 1 must be the North Pole (since like poles repel). Consequently, the opposite end (End 2) is the South Pole.
    • Case B (Attraction): If End 1 is attracted, then test End 2. End 2 will be repelled by the marked North pole, proving that End 2 is the North Pole and End 1 is the South Pole.
NCERT Q7 (Page 76): A bar magnet has no markings. How would you find its North pole without using another magnet?

Procedure (Using Earth's Geographic Field):

  1. Tie a thin piece of cotton thread around the exact middle of the unmarked bar magnet so that it balances horizontally.
  2. Suspend the thread freely from a wooden stand in an open room far away from iron objects or electrical appliances.
  3. Gently disturb the magnet and allow it to oscillate until it comes to a complete rest.
  4. Determine geographic directions using the morning sunrise (Sun rises in the East, so your left hand points North when facing East).
  5. Identification: The end of the magnet that consistently points towards the geographic North is its North Pole! The opposite end pointing south is its South Pole.
NCERT Q8 (Page 76): If Earth is a magnet, guess its magnetic poles by looking at a magnetic compass needle direction.

Deduction:

  • We know that the North pole of a compass needle points towards geographic North.
  • Under the cardinal law of magnetism, unlike poles attract each other. Therefore, the North pole of the compass can only be pulled towards a magnetic South pole!
  • Conclusion: The Earth's Magnetic South Pole is located near the geographic North Pole, and the Earth's Magnetic North Pole is located near the geographic South Pole!
NCERT Q9 (Page 76): Steel screws keep falling while repairing a gadget. Suggest a solution to the mechanic.

Scientific Solution: Magnetize the Screwdriver Tip!

  • The mechanic can stroke the steel shaft and tip of the screwdriver with one pole of a strong permanent magnet 30 to 40 times in one continuous direction (from handle towards the tip).
  • Alternatively, the mechanic can attach a small strong neodymium/disc magnet directly onto the metal shaft of the screwdriver.
  • Result: The steel tip turns into a temporary magnet. Because steel screws are a magnetic material, they will cling firmly to the screwdriver tip and will not slip or fall into tiny gadget compartments!
NCERT Q10 (Page 76): Ring magnet X floats above Y on a rod (Fig 4.16). Why? Suggest a way to make them contact without pushing.
  • Reason for Floating: Ring magnet X does not slide down to touch Y because their like poles face each other (either North facing North, or South facing South). The strong upward magnetic force of repulsion counteracts gravity, causing magnet X to levitate in mid-air!
  • How to bring them into contact without pushing:
    1. Lift ring magnet X off the wooden rod.
    2. Flip magnet X upside down (invert its faces) and slide it back onto the rod!
    3. Now, unlike poles face each other (North facing South). The magnetic force of attraction will immediately snap magnet X down to stick firmly onto magnet Y without any pushing!

Part C: 5 Short Answer Questions (2M & 3M)

Q1 (2 Marks): Why does a freely suspended bar magnet point North-South?

Answer:

  • Earth's Magnetic Field: The planet Earth behaves like a giant bar magnet with magnetic poles deep within its core.
  • Unlike Poles Attract: Earth's magnetic South pole lies near geographic North. It pulls the magnet's North pole toward geographic North, while Earth's magnetic North pole pulls the magnet's South pole toward geographic South.
Q2 (2 Marks): Why is repulsion called the "sure test" of magnetism?

Answer:

  • A magnet attracts both unlike magnetic poles AND unmagnetized iron pieces. Thus, attraction cannot prove if an object is itself a magnet.
  • However, repulsion occurs ONLY between two like magnetic poles. If an object is repelled by a magnet, it is guaranteed to be a magnet!
Q3 (3 Marks): How can you make your own temporary magnetic compass at home using a sewing needle?

Method (Activity 4.4):

  1. Take an iron sewing needle and stroke it with one pole of a bar magnet 30 to 40 times in one direction (never back and forth).
  2. Pass the magnetized needle through a small piece of cork or flat foam disc.
  3. Float the cork in a wide bowl of water so that the needle stays horizontal without touching the bowl sides.
  4. Result: When spun, the floating needle will always come to rest pointing in the North-South direction, acting as a true compass!
Q4 (3 Marks): List three precautions to keep permanent magnets safe from losing their strength.

Precautions:

  1. Never heat magnets over a gas stove or open flame.
  2. Never drop them from heights onto hard floors or hit them with hammers.
  3. Store in pairs with opposite poles adjacent, a wooden spacer between them, and soft iron keepers bridging both ends.
Q5 (3 Marks): Explain how Maglev trains use magnetic forces to achieve extreme high speeds.

Answer:

  • Magnetic Levitation: Powerful repelling electromagnets on the train and guideway track push against each other, lifting the train 10 to 15 mm into the air.
  • Zero Track Friction: Because the train has no wheels touching the ground, physical rolling friction is completely eliminated.
  • Propulsion: Shifting magnetic polarities pull and push the train forward smoothly, allowing quiet speeds above 450 km/h.

Part D: 3 Long Answer Questions (5 Marks Each)

Q1 (5 Marks): Describe the properties of magnets in detail. Support each property with an experimental activity.

Model Answer (Structured 5-Mark Marking Breakdown):

  1. Attractive Property (1 Mark): Magnets attract ferromagnetic materials like iron, nickel, cobalt, and steel. Experiment: Rolling a magnet picks up iron nails while leaving plastic beads behind.
  2. Polar Concentration (1 Mark): Magnetic attraction is strongest at the two ends (poles) and nearly zero in the middle. Experiment: In Activity 4.2, iron filings clump densely at the ends, leaving the centre bare.
  3. Directive Property (1 Mark): A freely suspended magnet always aligns itself along the geographic North-South line. Experiment: A bar magnet hung from a thread consistently points North-South when rotated.
  4. Law of Poles (1 Mark): Like poles repel each other; unlike poles attract. Experiment: Bringing North to North on toy cars pushes them backward, while North to South pulls them together.
  5. Inseparable Dipoles (1 Mark): Isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist. Snapping a magnet in half immediately creates two smaller complete magnets with paired N and S poles.
Q2 (5 Marks): What is a Magnetic Compass? Describe its internal construction, working principle, and historical importance in ocean navigation.

Model Answer:

1. Construction of a Magnetic Compass:

  • Consists of a small circular casing made of non-magnetic material (such as brass, aluminum, or clear plastic) covered with a transparent glass top.
  • A light, magnetized steel needle is balanced on a sharp friction-free central pivot so it can rotate freely in the horizontal plane.
  • The North pole of the needle is painted with a bright red tip or luminous dot.
  • A circular dial underneath is marked with cardinal directions: North (N), South (S), East (E), West (W), and intermediate headings (NE, NW, SE, SW).

2. Working Principle:

Earth acts as an enormous magnet whose magnetic South pole is situated near the geographic North Pole. When the compass is placed flat, Earth's geomagnetic field exerts torque on the magnetized needle, pulling the red North-seeking tip to align with true geographic North.

3. Historical Importance in Maritime Navigation:

Before the compass, ancient sailors could only navigate near coastlines or by looking at the Sun and the Pole Star. During monsoon storms or overcast nights, ships frequently became lost and wrecked. The magnetic compass allowed explorers to venture fearlessly into open oceans (like the spice trade route from Kerala to Arabia and Europe), transforming global commerce!

Q3 (5 Marks): Explain why a magnet loses its magnetism if mistreated. How must bar magnets and horseshoe magnets be stored for long-term preservation?

Model Answer:

1. Why Magnets Lose Magnetism (Demagnetization):
Inside magnetic metals, microscopic groups of atoms act like tiny magnets called domains. In a strong magnet, all these domains point in the same direction. When a magnet is subjected to severe physical stress (hammering, dropping) or heat, the intense vibrations jumble and randomize these domains. When the domains point in all directions, their magnetic effects cancel out, and the magnet dies.

2. Proper Storage of Bar Magnets:

  • Bar magnets must always be stored in pairs with opposite poles placed side-by-side (North pole beside South pole).
  • A strip of dry wood must be placed between them to keep them physically separated.
  • Two thick pieces of soft iron (magnetic keepers) must be placed across both open ends. This guides magnetic field lines into a continuous closed loop, preventing free poles from neutralizing themselves over time.

3. Proper Storage of a Horseshoe Magnet:
A single horseshoe (U-shaped) magnet only needs a single flat soft iron keeper placed across its two adjacent poles (bridging N and S) to lock the magnetic loop securely.

Case Study 1: The Floating Ring Toy

"Aarav received a science toy consisting of a vertical smooth plastic rod fixed to a wooden base. The kit had three identical ceramic ring magnets painted red on one face and blue on the other. Aarav slid Ring 1 down the rod with its red face pointing up. Next, he slid Ring 2 with its red face pointing down. Ring 2 did not drop onto Ring 1; instead, it bounced and hovered in mid-air with a 2 cm visible air gap! When Aarav placed a 50-gram weight on Ring 2, it moved down slightly but continued to float without touching."

Sub-Questions & Detailed Answers:

  1. What phenomenon explains why Ring 2 hovered in mid-air?
    Answer: Magnetic Levitation caused by Repulsion between like magnetic poles (both facing red surfaces have identical polarity, e.g., North facing North).
  2. What will happen if Aarav removes Ring 2, flips it over so the blue face points down, and slides it back?
    Answer: Ring 2 will immediately accelerate downward and snap tightly onto Ring 1 due to magnetic attraction between unlike poles (Red North attracts Blue South).
  3. How does this toy demonstrate the technology used in Maglev bullet trains?
    Answer: It shows that strong repelling magnetic forces can overcome gravity and support heavy weights in mid-air, eliminating friction between moving surfaces.

Case Study 2: The Junkyard Electromagnetic Crane

"At a municipal metal recycling yard in Cuttack, a large crane with a flat circular disc moves over heaps of mixed city waste. When the crane operator presses a switch inside his cabin, hundreds of crushed tin cans, iron rods, rusted steel car doors, and engine blocks jump through the air and stick to the disc. The operator swings the crane arm over a waiting transport truck and flips the switch off. Instantly, all the heavy metal objects drop cleanly into the truck bed without anyone touching them."

Sub-Questions & Detailed Answers:

  1. Why did aluminum beverage cans and copper wires in the garbage heap remain behind on the ground?
    Answer: Because aluminum and copper are non-magnetic materials that experience no attractive force from a magnet.
  2. Why did all the iron objects drop into the truck bed when the operator flipped the switch off?
    Answer: The crane uses an electromagnet that only possesses magnetism when electric current flows. Turning off the switch cuts the current, switching off the magnetic field and releasing the scrap.
  3. Identify two key advantages of using a magnetic crane over manual labour in a scrap yard.
    Answer: It moves tons of heavy, sharp iron scrap rapidly without human injury and instantly separates magnetic iron from non-magnetic debris.

Exam Hall Traps & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Common mistakes made by Class 6 students in exams, how to avoid them, and a crisp 15-point memory checklist.

5 Common Student Mistakes to Avoid

Common Student Mistake Why Students Make This Mistake Correct Scientific Understanding to Write
Mistake 1: Claiming that all shiny metals are magnetic. Students associate metal lustre with magnetism. Copper, Aluminum, Gold, and Brass are metals, but they are NON-MAGNETIC! Only iron, nickel, cobalt, and steel are magnetic.
Mistake 2: Believing breaking a magnet isolates single poles. Thinking cutting separates the North half from the South half. Breaking creates two complete new magnets. Magnetic monopoles DO NOT exist in nature!
Mistake 3: Using attraction as proof that an unknown bar is a magnet. Forgetting that a magnet attracts both opposite poles and plain iron. Repulsion is the ONLY sure test of a magnet, because only two like poles can repel.
Mistake 4: Assuming Earth's magnetic North is at geographic North. Confusing geographic names with magnetic polarity. Earth's magnetic SOUTH pole lies near geographic North (which is why the North tip of a compass is attracted there!).
Mistake 5: Storing bar magnets with like poles side-by-side. Forgetting proper storage configuration. Bar magnets must be stored with unlike poles side-by-side, separated by wood, and closed with soft iron keepers.

15 Ultra-Crisp Revision One-Liners (Memorize for 100%)

  1. Lodestone (Magnetite): The naturally occurring magnetic iron ore used by ancient maritime navigators.
  2. Magnetic Metals: Iron, Nickel, Cobalt, and Steel are strongly attracted to magnets.
  3. Non-Magnetic Materials: Wood, plastic, glass, rubber, paper, copper, and aluminium are not attracted by magnets.
  4. Poles: Every magnet has two poles — North (N) and South (S) — where magnetic force is strongest.
  5. Middle Region: The central section of a bar magnet exhibits almost zero magnetic attractive force.
  6. Inseparable Pairs: Cutting a magnet produces smaller complete magnets; a solitary magnetic pole cannot exist.
  7. Directive Property: A freely suspended magnet always settles pointing along the Earth's geographic North-South line.
  8. Earth's Magnetism: Earth acts as a giant magnet with its magnetic South pole situated near geographic North.
  9. Magnetic Compass: Uses a pivoted magnetized needle to indicate cardinal directions (N, S, E, W).
  10. Like Poles Repel: North repels North, and South repels South.
  11. Unlike Poles Attract: North attracts South, and South attracts North.
  12. Sure Test: Repulsion is the only foolproof test of magnetism.
  13. Demagnetization: Magnets lose their strength when heated, hammered, or dropped from heights.
  14. Safe Storage: Keep bar magnets in pairs with opposite poles adjacent, wooden spacers, and soft iron keepers.
  15. Maglev Trains: Magnetic levitation eliminates wheel-rail friction, enabling speeds over 450 km/h.
