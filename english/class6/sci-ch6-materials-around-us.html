📖 Section 2: Exhaustive Topic-by-Topic Concept Notes

6.1 Observing Objects Around Us

Look around you right now: classroom desks, water bottles, notebooks, window panes, clothes, and school bags. All these items are called objects. But what are objects made of? Objects are made of substances called materials!

  • One object made of multiple materials: A pencil is made of wood (casing), graphite mixed with clay (lead), aluminium/brass (ferrule band), and synthetic rubber (eraser tip).
  • One material used to make many different objects: Wood can be used to carve a dining chair, study table, cricket bat, doors, bullock cart wheels, and pencil bodies. Glass is used to make window panes, drinking glasses, mirrors, spectacles, and test tubes.
  • Same object made from different materials: A drinking cup or tumbler can be made from glass, stainless steel, plastic, ceramic (clay), or paper!

💡 Object vs Material — Never Confuse the Two!

An object is a finished item designed for a specific human use (e.g., spoon, shoe, plate). A material is the specific matter or substance out of which the object is constructed (e.g., steel, leather, ceramic, plastic).

6.2 How Do We Group Materials? (The Science of Classification)

Why do supermarkets sort soaps in one aisle, biscuits in another, and fresh fruits in a separate section? Imagine if a shopkeeper dumped all pulses, shampoos, spices, and candies into one giant heap — finding a single pack of turmeric would take hours!

Similarly, scientists group materials based on shared characteristics. Classification is the systematic arrangement of objects or materials into categories based on similarities and differences in their observable properties.

🧪 Activity 6.3: Why Can't a Tumbler be Made of Cloth?

Thought Experiment: Could you make a water-carrying tumbler using a piece of woven cotton bedsheet or silk cloth?

Observation & Reasoning: Cotton cloth is porous — it has microscopic open pores between the woven threads. When you pour water into a cloth pouch, the water leaks straight through!

Scientific Conclusion: We choose a material to make an object based on its properties and the intended purpose of that object. To hold liquids, the material must be waterproof and non-porous (such as glass, plastic, or metal).

6.3 Physical Properties of Materials

In Grade 6 Science (NEP Curiosity), we systematically explore six fundamental physical properties that define materials:

6.3.1 Appearance: Lustrous vs Non-Lustrous Materials

  • Lustre: Lustre refers to the special shiny glow or metallic sheen seen on the freshly cut or polished surface of a material.
  • Lustrous Materials: Freshly cut metals such as gold, silver, copper, iron, aluminium, and shiny metallic foils exhibit bright lustre.
  • Non-Lustrous (Dull) Materials: Wood, paper, chalk, rubber, cardboard, and dry soil do not shine and have a dull appearance.
  • Why do old iron gates or copper vessels lose their shine? Metals react slowly with atmospheric oxygen, moisture, and carbon dioxide over weeks and months, forming a dull tarnish or oxide layer (corrosion/rust). When you rub the surface with sandpaper, the fresh metal beneath is exposed, and the lustre reappears immediately!

6.3.2 Hardness: Compressibility and Scratch Resistance

  • Soft Materials: Materials that can be easily compressed, bent, deformed, or scratched with our fingernails are called soft materials (e.g., sponge, cotton wool, fresh dough, soap, candle wax).
  • Hard Materials: Materials that cannot be compressed by hand and resist being scratched by an iron nail or key are called hard materials (e.g., iron rod, stone pebble, diamond, brick, glass, hardened steel).
  • The Scratch Test: If material A can make a scratch mark on material B, material A is harder than material B. For example, an iron nail easily scratches a wooden block, proving iron is harder than wood.

6.3.3 Optical Property: Transparency, Translucency, and Opacity

How materials interact with light rays determines whether we can see through them:

Property Definition Light Transmission Clear Real-Life Examples
Transparent Substances through which objects can be seen clearly and distinctly. Allows almost all light to pass straight through without scattering. Clear glass, clean air, pure water, clear cellophane sheet.
Translucent Substances through which objects can be seen, but NOT clearly (blurred or hazy). Allows light to pass partially while scattering the rays. Butter paper, frosted glass, oiled paper, thin tracing paper, fog/mist.
Opaque Substances through which we cannot see at all. Completely blocks, absorbs, or reflects light; allows zero light to pass. Wooden door, brick wall, cardboard sheet, iron plate, thick book.

6.3.4 Solubility in Water: Soluble vs Insoluble

Water is known as the universal solvent because it dissolves more substances than any other liquid on Earth. This property is crucial for digestion, blood transport, and plant sap circulation.

  • Soluble Substances: Substances that completely dissolve and disappear in water to form a uniform, single-phase solution.
    Examples: Common table salt (sodium chloride), cane sugar, glucose powder, alum, lemon juice, vinegar.
  • Insoluble Substances: Substances that do not dissolve in water even after vigorous stirring for several minutes.
    Examples: Sand, chalk powder, sawdust, iron filings, sulfur powder, wheat flour (suspension).
  • Miscible vs Immiscible Liquids:
    • Miscible liquids: Liquids that mix completely with water (e.g., vinegar, alcohol, honey, lemon juice).
    • Immiscible liquids: Liquids that do NOT mix with water and form a separate layer on standing (e.g., mustard oil, coconut oil, kerosene, diesel). Since oils are less dense than water, they float on top as a distinct upper layer!

6.3.5 Buoyancy: Floating vs Sinking in Water

Why does a giant wooden log float on a river, while a tiny iron pin drops straight to the riverbed? This depends on whether the material is lighter or heavier than an equal volume of water (the concept of density):

  • Objects that Float in Water: Materials that are less dense than water stay on the surface (e.g., dry fallen leaves, cork, thermocol, dry wood, ice, plastic bottle caps, drops of oil).
  • Objects that Sink in Water: Materials that are denser than water sink straight to the bottom (e.g., iron nail, stone pebble, coin, glass marble, brass key).

6.3.6 Space and Volume: The Displacement Principle

Every object occupies a specific amount of three-dimensional space. The amount of space occupied by a three-dimensional object is called its volume.

🧪 The Crow and the Pitcher Experiment Decoded

Take a graduated measuring cylinder filled with 100 mL of water. Gently drop a small stone pebble into the cylinder using a thread.

Observation: The water level immediately rises from 100 mL to 125 mL!

Scientific Reason: Two objects cannot occupy the exact same physical space at the same time. The submerged pebble pushes aside (displaces) a volume of water equal to its own volume (125 − 100 = 25 mL).

6.4 What is Matter?

By connecting all our observations across this chapter, science arrives at one grand conclusion:

⚗️ Scientific Definition of Matter

Matter is defined as anything that has mass (it can be weighed) and occupies space (it has volume).

  • Examples of Matter: Water, air, ice, desks, apples, dust particles, planets, and our own bodies.
  • What is NOT Matter? Non-material concepts that do NOT occupy space and have zero mass — such as thoughts, feelings, love, friendship, shadow, heat, sound, and sunlight. These are forms of energy or mental sensations, not matter!

Exercise 3 Why are containers used to store materials in shops and at home usually transparent?

Shopkeepers and homemakers choose transparent glass or clear plastic containers because:

  1. Instant Identification: Transparent walls allow light to pass through, making the stored contents (candies, pulses, spices) clearly visible from the outside without needing to open the container.
  2. Saves Time and Effort: Customers can see available goods at a glance, and cooks don't waste time unsealing multiple lids while cooking.

Exercise 4 State whether True [T] or False [F] and correct false statements

(i) Wood is translucent while glass is opaque.
Answer: FALSE [F]
Correction: Wood is opaque (blocks all light) while clean glass is transparent (allows light to pass completely).

(ii) Aluminium foil has lustre while an eraser does not.
Answer: TRUE [T]
Justification: Aluminium is a metal with a shiny metallic surface (lustre), whereas rubber is non-metallic and has a dull, non-lustrous appearance.

(iii) Sugar dissolves in water whereas sawdust does not.
Answer: TRUE [T]
Justification: Sugar is highly soluble in water forming a clear homogeneous solution, while insoluble sawdust floats or remains suspended.

(iv) An apple is a matter because it occupies no space and has mass.
Answer: FALSE [F]
Correction: An apple is matter because it occupies space (has volume) AND has mass.

Exercise 10 Solubility pairing with vinegar, honey, mustard oil, water, glucose, and wheat flour

Pairs where one material is SOLUBLE in the other:

  1. Glucose in Water: Glucose powder dissolves rapidly and completely in water to make an energising solution.
  2. Honey in Water (or Vinegar in Water): Honey and vinegar are miscible in water, mixing uniformly without forming a separate boundary.

Pairs where one material remains INSOLUBLE in the other:

  1. Mustard oil in Water: Oil is immiscible; it floats on the water surface and forms a distinct separate layer.

Air qualifies as matter because it satisfies both criteria of the scientific definition:

  1. Air occupies space (Volume): Inflating a balloon causes it to expand, proving air takes up volume.
  2. Air has mass: An inflated football weighs noticeably more on a sensitive balance than a deflated football.
SA 5 What happens when you test the hardness of a piece of chalk and an iron nail using a scratch test? (2 Marks)

When an iron nail is rubbed against a piece of chalk, the sharp nail easily carves a deep groove into the chalk. In contrast, the chalk cannot scratch the iron nail. This proves that the iron nail is hard, while the chalk is relatively soft.

Long Answer Questions (5 Marks)

LA 1 Explain five major physical properties used to classify materials around us, giving one real-life application for each. (5 Marks)

The five core physical properties are:

  1. Appearance (Lustre): Materials can be shiny (lustrous) or dull.
    Application: Gold and silver are crafted into jewellery because of their lasting metallic lustre.
  2. Hardness: Materials can be compressible (soft) or rigid (hard).
    Application: Steel and granite are used in building pillars and bridges for high mechanical hardness.
  3. Transparency: Materials are transparent, translucent, or opaque.
    Application: Car windscreens are made of transparent toughened glass so drivers have an unobstructed view.
  4. Solubility: Ability to dissolve in water.
    Application: Sugar and salt dissolve in water, making ORS drinks and everyday cooking possible.
  5. Floating / Sinking (Density): Behavior in water.
    Application: Thermocol and hollow plastics are used in life jackets because they float reliably on water.
LA 2 Describe an activity to prove that liquids can be miscible or immiscible with water. (5 Marks)

Aim: To investigate how different liquids mix with water.

Materials Required: Four test tubes, test tube stand, water, vinegar, lemon juice, mustard oil, and kerosene.

Procedure:

  1. Fill each test tube half-full with clean tap water.
  2. Add 5 mL of vinegar to Tube A, lemon juice to Tube B, mustard oil to Tube C, and kerosene to Tube D.
  3. Shake each test tube vigorously for 30 seconds.
  4. Place all tubes in the stand and let them sit undisturbed for 10 minutes.

Observations:

  • Tubes A & B (Vinegar & Lemon Juice): The liquids mix completely and disappear into the water, forming a single uniform solution. They are miscible.
  • Tubes C & D (Mustard Oil & Kerosene): The oils do not mix. They separate out and float on top as a distinct, separate oily layer. They are immiscible.

Conclusion: Some liquids dissolve uniformly in water (miscible), while others separate into two distinct layers (immiscible).

LA 3 "An object can be made from a combination of several materials." Justify this with the example of a bicycle and a cooking pan. (5 Marks)

Objects are rarely made of a single material because different parts of an object must fulfill completely different functions:

  1. The Bicycle:
    • Frame: Made of high-strength steel or aluminium alloy — rigid, hard, and bears the rider's weight.
    • Tires & Tubes: Made of vulcanised elastic rubber — flexible, grips the asphalt, and absorbs road shocks.
    • Seat (Saddle): Padded with soft foam and synthetic leather — compressible, comfortable for sitting.
    • Reflector / Headlamp Cover: Made of transparent/translucent acrylic plastic — lets light through for night road safety.
  2. The Cooking Pan:
    • Base & Body: Made of aluminium, iron, or copper — excellent conductors of heat that cook food rapidly and withstand open stove flames.
    • Handle: Made of Bakelite plastic or treated wood — poor conductors of heat (insulators), allowing cooks to lift hot pans safely without burning their hands.

📜 Case Study 1: The Kitchen Storage Dilemma

Context: Mrs. Verma moved into a new apartment and purchased two types of kitchen jars: one set of transparent borosilicate glass jars and another set of heavy stainless steel canisters. She decided to store whole spices, breakfast cornflakes, and colorful pulses in the glass jars, while storing cooking oil and whole wheat flour in the steel canisters.

Questions:

  1. What scientific property made glass jars the ideal choice for pulses and cornflakes?
  2. Why did Mrs. Verma choose stainless steel rather than unbaked clay pots for storing cooking oil?
  3. If a cook accidentally drops an iron spoon and a dry bay leaf into a basin of water, what will happen to each? Explain why.
View Model Answers
  1. Transparency. Glass allows light to pass through, enabling instant identification of the contents and monitoring of food quantities without opening lids.
  2. Stainless steel is non-porous, waterproof, and non-reactive. Unbaked clay is porous; cooking oil would seep through its micropores, making the pot greasy and unhygienic.
  3. The iron spoon will sink straight to the bottom because iron is denser than water. The dry bay leaf will float on the surface because dry leaves are porous, light, and less dense than water.

📜 Case Study 2: The Junk Yard Science Investigation

Context: Kabir visited a local scrap recycling center with his science teacher. He observed workers using a massive circular crane that picked up heaps of old vehicle chassis, iron pipes, and steel drums effortlessly, while ignoring nearby heaps of broken plastic chairs, discarded cardboard boxes, and copper wiring.

Questions:

  1. What device was mounted on the crane to pick up specific scrap materials, and what property did it exploit?
  2. Why were copper wires not attracted to the crane?
  3. Are old plastic chairs and cardboard boxes classified as matter? Justify your answer using the scientific definition.
View Model Answers
  1. The crane was equipped with a high-power electromagnet that exploited the magnetic property of ferrous metals (iron and steel).
  2. Copper is a non-magnetic metal. Electromagnets only attract ferromagnetic materials like iron, cobalt, and nickel.
  3. Yes, absolutely. Both plastic chairs and cardboard boxes have measurable mass (they can be weighed on a scale) and occupy three-dimensional space (volume), which satisfies the scientific definition of matter.

🚨 Section 6: Exam Hall Mistakes & 15 Rapid Revision Points

❌ 5 Common Exam Hall Mistakes to Avoid

  • Mistake 1: Confusing an object with a material: Students often write "pencil is a material" or "wood is an object". Remember: Wood is the material; the pencil is the finished object.
  • Mistake 2: Calling shadow, heat, or sunlight "matter": Shadows have zero mass and occupy no space. Sunlight and heat are forms of energy, not matter!
  • Mistake 3: Believing all metals remain lustrous forever: Metals lose their lustre over time when exposed to moist air due to surface oxidation (tarnishing). Scraping with sandpaper restores the shine.
  • Mistake 4: Confusing translucent with transparent: Transparent gives a clear, crisp view; translucent allows light through but gives a blurred, indistinct view.
  • Mistake 5: Assuming heavier objects always sink: A giant 10,000-tonne hollow steel ship floats on the ocean, while a tiny 1-gram iron pebble sinks. Sinking and floating depend on density, not just total mass!

🚀 15 Ultra-Crisp Rapid Revision One-Liners

  1. An object is an article made for a specific use; a material is the substance out of which it is made.
  2. One object can be made of multiple materials (e.g., pencil = wood + graphite + metal + rubber).
  3. One material can be used to make many different objects (e.g., wood = chair, table, door, bat).
  4. Classification is the systematic grouping of materials based on similarities and differences in properties.
  5. Lustre is the shiny metallic sheen present on the freshly cut surface of metals.
  6. Metals lose their lustre in humid air due to the formation of a dull oxide/tarnish coating.
  7. Hard materials resist being compressed or scratched (e.g., iron, stone, diamond).
  8. Soft materials can be easily compressed or scratched with fingernails (e.g., sponge, cotton, wax).
  9. Transparent materials transmit light completely, providing clear visibility (e.g., clear glass, air).
  10. Translucent materials scatter light partially, providing blurred or hazy visibility (e.g., butter paper).
  11. Opaque materials block light completely, through which nothing can be seen (e.g., wood, brick).
  12. Substances that dissolve completely in water are soluble (e.g., salt, sugar).
  13. Liquids that mix completely with water are miscible (e.g., vinegar); those that form a separate layer are immiscible (e.g., oil).
  14. Materials less dense than water float; materials denser than water sink.
  15. Matter is defined as anything that has mass and occupies space (volume).
