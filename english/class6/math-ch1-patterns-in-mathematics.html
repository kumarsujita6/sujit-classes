Section 2: Line-by-Line Conceptual Notes

📘 What is a Number Sequence?

A number sequence is an ordered list of numbers formed according to a specific, definite mathematical rule. Each number in the sequence is called a term. The branch of mathematics that explores patterns in whole numbers is known as Number Theory.

1. Primary Number Sequences in Ganita Prakash

The NCERT textbook introduces nine fundamental sequences that form the building blocks of mathematics:

  • Counting Numbers (Natural Numbers): 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, ...
    Rule: Start with 1, and add 1 to get each subsequent term (Term n = n).
  • Odd Numbers: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, ...
    Rule: Start with 1, and add 2 repeatedly. Every term has the algebraic form 2n − 1.
  • Even Numbers: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, ...
    Rule: Start with 2, and add 2 repeatedly. Every term has the algebraic form 2n.
  • Square Numbers (Perfect Squares): 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, ...
    Rule: Multiply a counting number by itself (Term n = n × n = n²). In geometry, this represents the total number of unit dots arranged in an n × n square grid.
  • Triangular Numbers: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 28, ...
    Rule: The n-th triangular number is the sum of the first n counting numbers: T(n) = 1 + 2 + 3 + ... + n = n(n + 1)⁄2. Visually, these dots arrange into an equilateral triangle.
  • Powers of 2: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, ...
    Rule: Start with 1, and double (multiply by 2) each time (2⁰=1, 2¹=2, 2²=4, 2³=8, 2⁴=16, ...). This models cell division, binary computing, and tree branching.
  • Powers of 3: 1, 3, 9, 27, 81, 243, ...
    Rule: Start with 1, and multiply by 3 each time (Term n = 3ⁿ⁻¹).
  • Powers of 10: 1, 10, 100, 1000, 10000, ...
    Rule: Multiply by 10 each time. This forms the foundation of our decimal place-value system.
  • Cube Numbers: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, ...
    Rule: The product of three identical integers (Term n = n × n × n = n³).

2. Remarkable Inter-Sequence Relations

💡 The Odd-Square Relationship

The sum of the first n odd numbers is always equal to n² (the n-th square number):

  • 1st odd number: 1 = 1²
  • Sum of first 2 odds: 1 + 3 = 4 = 2²
  • Sum of first 3 odds: 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = 3²
  • Sum of first 4 odds: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 = 4²
  • Sum of first 5 odds: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25 = 5²

Geometric Explanation: In an n × n square dot array, adding an L-shaped border of 2n + 1 dots expands the square from size n × n to (n+1) × (n+1)!

🧪 Up and Down Addition Sequence

Adding the counting numbers up to n and then back down to 1 always yields n²:

  • 1 = 1² = 1
  • 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 = 2²
  • 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 9 = 3²
  • 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 16 = 4²
  • 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 25 = 5²

Why? Because counting columns in a diagonally tilted square of dots counts exactly this up-and-down sequence!

3. Patterns in Shapes and Geometry

Geometry is the study of patterns in spatial figures. The NCERT textbook highlights four core shape sequences:

  1. Regular Polygons: Triangle (3 sides), Square (4 sides), Regular Pentagon (5 sides), Regular Hexagon (6 sides), Regular Octagon (8 sides). Both the number of sides and the number of vertices (corners) follow the counting number sequence: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, ...
  2. Complete Graphs (The Handshake Problem): Given n points on a circle, connect every pair with a straight line.
    • 2 points → 1 line
    • 3 points → 3 lines
    • 4 points → 6 lines
    • 5 points → 10 lines
    • 6 points → 15 lines
    The number of connecting lines generates the Triangular Number sequence: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, ...! Formula: Lines = n(n − 1)⁄2.
  3. Stacked Squares: Stacking square tiles row by row produces total tile counts of 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ... (Square numbers).
  4. Koch Snowflake (Fractals): Start with an equilateral triangle (3 line segments). Replace the middle third of each side with two segments of a smaller triangle.
    • Iteration 0: 3 segments
    • Iteration 1: 3 × 4 = 12 segments
    • Iteration 2: 12 × 4 = 48 segments
    • Iteration 3: 48 × 4 = 192 segments
    The number of segments follows the sequence 3 × 4ⁿ, quadrupling at every step!

Figure it Out (NCERT Pages 8–9): Inter-Sequence Operations & Sums

Question 1: Which sequence do you get when you start adding the "All 1's" sequence up? What sequence do you get when you add it up and down?

Step 1: Given: All 1's sequence: 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1...
Step 2: Operation A (Adding Up):
  • 1 = 1
  • 1 + 1 = 2
  • 1 + 1 + 1 = 3
  • 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4
This generates the sequence: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ..., which is the Counting Numbers sequence!
Step 3: Operation B (Adding Up and Down):
  • 1 = 1
  • 1 + 1 + 1 = (Wait, counting numbers up and down: 1 = 1; 1 + 2 + 1 = 4; 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 9; 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 16).
Summing the counting numbers up and down yields 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ..., which is the Square Numbers sequence!
Step 4: Results: Adding All 1's gives Counting Numbers; adding Counting Numbers up and down gives Square Numbers.
Hence, simple cumulative addition transitions from a constant sequence to counting numbers, and then to square numbers.

Question 2: Which sequence do you get when you add consecutive triangular numbers: 1 + 3, 3 + 6, 6 + 10, 10 + 15, ...? Explain why.

Step 1: Given Pairs of Consecutive Triangular Numbers: T₁ = 1, T₂ = 3, T₃ = 6, T₄ = 10, T₅ = 15.
Step 2: Summing Consecutive Pairs:
  • T₁ + T₂ = 1 + 3 = 4 = 2²
  • T₂ + T₃ = 3 + 6 = 9 = 3²
  • T₃ + T₄ = 6 + 10 = 16 = 4²
  • T₄ + T₅ = 10 + 15 = 25 = 5²
Step 3: Algebraic Proof:
T(n−1) + T(n) = (n−1)n⁄2 + n(n+1)⁄2 = n(n − 1 + n + 1)⁄2 = n(2n)⁄2 = n².
Step 4: Result: We obtain the Square Numbers sequence: 4, 9, 16, 25, 36...
Therefore, the sum of any two consecutive triangular numbers always produces a perfect square number.

Question 3: Investigate the sequence of cube differences: 1, 1 + 7, 1 + 7 + 19, 1 + 7 + 19 + 37, ... Which sequence do you get?

Step 1: Given Progressive Sums: 1, (1 + 7), (1 + 7 + 19), (1 + 7 + 19 + 37)...
Step 2: Arithmetic Computation:
  • Term 1 = 1 = 1³
  • Term 2 = 1 + 7 = 8 = 2³
  • Term 3 = 1 + 7 + 19 = 27 = 3³
  • Term 4 = 1 + 7 + 19 + 37 = 64 = 4³
Step 3: Explanation: The numbers being added (1, 7, 19, 37, 61...) are called Centered Hexagonal Numbers, representing the concentric layers of a hexagon of dots. Mathematically, they are consecutive differences between cubes: n³ − (n−1)³. When summed telescopingly, all middle terms cancel, leaving n³!
Step 4: Resulting Sequence: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125... (The Cube Numbers).
Thus, summing centered hexagonal differences sequentially reconstructs the perfect cube numbers.

Question 2: Count the number of connecting lines in the sequence of Complete Graphs. Which number sequence does this give and why?

Step 1: Given Data: Complete graphs with n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 points on a circle.
Step 2: Formula: Every pair of points determines exactly 1 unique line segment. The number of ways to choose 2 points from n points is: Total Lines = n(n − 1)⁄2.
Step 3: Evaluating for each n:
  • For n = 2: 2 × 1⁄2 = 1 line
  • For n = 3: 3 × 2⁄2 = 3 lines
  • For n = 4: 4 × 3⁄2 = 6 lines
  • For n = 5: 5 × 4⁄2 = 10 lines
  • For n = 6: 6 × 5⁄2 = 15 lines
Step 4: Identification: The sequence of lines is 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, ..., which is exactly the Triangular Numbers sequence!
Therefore, complete graph lines generate the Triangular Numbers sequence because each new point connects to all existing points, adding successive natural numbers.

Question 3: How many little squares and little triangles are in Stacked Squares and Stacked Triangles?

Step 1: Given: Shapes of stacked squares (1, 2×2, 3×3...) and stacked triangles (rows of 1, 3, 5, 7 triangles).
Step 2: Analysis of Stacked Squares: Shape 1 has 1 square; Shape 2 has 4 squares; Shape 3 has 9 squares; Shape 4 has 16 squares. Sequence: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ... → Square Numbers (n²).
Step 3: Analysis of Stacked Triangles:
  • Row 1 has 1 triangle → Total = 1 = 1²
  • Row 2 adds 3 triangles → Total = 1 + 3 = 4 = 2²
  • Row 3 adds 5 triangles → Total = 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = 3²
  • Row 4 adds 7 triangles → Total = 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 = 4²
Each row has consecutive odd numbers of unit triangles. As established earlier, the sum of the first n odd numbers is n².
Step 4: Conclusion: Both Stacked Squares and Stacked Triangles generate the exact same Square Numbers sequence (n²).
This confirms the geometric theorem that stacking consecutive odd rows of triangles forms a larger similar triangle whose area equals n².

Question 4: In the Koch Snowflake, how many line segments are in each shape? Determine the sequence.

Step 1: Given: Initial shape is an equilateral triangle with 3 sides. In each step, every line segment is replaced by 4 smaller segments.
Step 2: Formula: Let S₀ = 3. In step k, every segment becomes 4 segments: Sₖ = Sₖ₋₁ × 4 = 3 × 4ᵏ.
Step 3: Calculations:
  • Initial (k = 0): 3 segments
  • Iteration 1 (k = 1): 3 × 4 = 12 segments
  • Iteration 2 (k = 2): 12 × 4 = 48 segments
  • Iteration 3 (k = 3): 48 × 4 = 192 segments
  • Iteration 4 (k = 4): 192 × 4 = 768 segments
Step 4: Sequence: 3, 12, 48, 192, 768, 3072, ... (described as 3 times the Powers of 4).
Thus, the number of line segments quadruples at each iteration, forming an infinite perimeter enclosing a finite fractal area.

Q3. Mathematical Induction and Proofs: Prove algebraically and visually why the up-and-down sum 1 + 2 + ... + n + ... + 2 + 1 must always equal n². Then calculate the sum up to n = 50. (5 Marks)

View Marking Breakdown & Solution

1. Algebraic Decomposition (2 Marks):

  • Split the sum into two parts: Ascending part [1 + 2 + ... + (n−1)] and Descending part [n + (n−1) + ... + 2 + 1].
  • Notice that 1 + 2 + ... + (n−1) = T(n−1) (the (n−1)-th triangular number).
  • Notice that 1 + 2 + ... + n = T(n) (the n-th triangular number).
  • Therefore, total sum = T(n−1) + T(n).
  • As proven in NCERT Table 2, the sum of two consecutive triangular numbers is identical to n²!

2. Visual Proof via Matrix Grid (2 Marks):

  • In an n × n square of dots, count the dots along diagonal stripes.
  • The first diagonal has 1 dot, the second has 2, the third has 3, up to the main diagonal having n dots, and then 4 dots, 3, 2, 1 dots on subsequent diagonals.
  • Since all dots in the n × n square are accounted for, the total number of dots is both the up-and-down sum and n².

3. Application for n = 50 (1 Mark):

  • 1 + 2 + ... + 50 + ... + 2 + 1 = 50² = 2500.

HOTS Problem 10: Fractal Perimeter Paradox

The perimeter expands from 27 cm to 36 cm and then to 48 cm, multiplying by 4/3 at every iteration.

Section 6: Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 Rapid Revision One-Liners

Top 5 Common Student Exam Mistakes

  • Trap 1: Confusing Term Index (n) with the Value (aₙ). When asked for the "8th odd number", students often write 8. Remember: n = 8, but the term value is 2(8) − 1 = 15!
  • Trap 2: Forgetting to Divide by 2 in Triangular Numbers. Students often multiply n(n+1) and forget the denominator 2. T₁₀ = 10 × 11 / 2 = 55, NOT 110!
  • Trap 3: Counting Handshakes as n × (n−1) Without Halving. In a handshake, person A shaking hands with person B is the exact same event as person B shaking hands with person A. Always divide by 2!
  • Trap 4: Missing the Starting Index in Exponential Sequences. In Powers of 2, the first term is 2⁰ = 1, the second is 2¹ = 2, and the n-th term is 2ⁿ⁻¹. Do not write the first term as 2!
  • Trap 5: Confusing Polygon Sides with Diagonals. A polygon of n sides has n sides and n vertices. The total lines in a complete graph include both the perimeter sides and the internal diagonals!
