2.1 The Concept of Force: Intuition vs Physical Reality

In our daily life, we constantly push, pull, lift, twist, and throw objects. We push open a wooden door, pull a window shutter, hit a tennis ball with a racket, stretch a rubber band, or squeeze a piece of clay. We say that a force has been exerted.

However, force cannot be seen, tasted, or touched directly. We cannot photograph "force" itself; we can only witness, observe, and measure its effects on matter. In physics, force is rigorously defined as:

Scientific Definition of Force Force is an external agency (a push or a pull) resulting from the mutual interaction between two physical bodies that changes, or tends to change, the state of rest, the state of uniform motion along a straight line, or the physical dimensions (shape and size) of a body.

The Four Primary Effects of Force on Matter

Whenever an external unbalanced force acts upon an object, it can induce one or more of four fundamental physical modifications:

  1. Change an object from rest into motion: Kicking a resting football makes it zoom across the grass; releasing the clutch of a car accelerates it from stationary parking.
  2. Change the speed of a moving object: Pressing the accelerator increases a motorcycle's speed; pressing the brakes slows down the vehicle to a gentle halt.
  3. Change the direction of motion: A batsman strikes a fast bowler's delivery to redirect the ball towards the boundary line; the gravitational pull of the Sun constantly bends Earth's trajectory into an elliptical orbit.
  4. Change the shape and size (deformation) of an object: Squeezing a rubber sponge, hammering a heated lump of metal, or pulling on a helical spring alters its geometric dimensions.
Vector Nature of Force Force is a vector quantity. It possesses both magnitude (how many Newtons of push/pull are exerted) and direction (the line along which the force acts). You cannot add forces like ordinary numbers! Two forces of 5 N each acting in opposite directions cancel out to 0 N, whereas acting in the same direction they combine to 10 N.

2.2 Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

When multiple forces act simultaneously on an object, we calculate their resultant (or net force, denoted as ΣF). The kinematic destiny of the object depends strictly on whether this net force is zero or non-zero.

1. Balanced Forces (ΣF = 0)

If the resultant of all forces acting upon a body equals zero, the forces are termed balanced forces.

  • Balanced forces cannot change the state of rest of an object. A book resting on a table stays stationary because the downward gravitational pull (Weight, W = mg) is perfectly balanced by the upward Normal Reaction force (N) of the tabletop (W = N ⇒ Net force = 0).
  • Balanced forces cannot change the velocity of a moving body. An aeroplane cruising at constant altitude and steady speed has its engine thrust perfectly balancing air drag, while aerodynamic lift balances gravity.
  • Balanced forces can change the shape and volume of an object! For example, pressing an inflated rubber balloon between both palms exerts equal and opposite compressive forces. The balloon remains stationary (it does not accelerate left or right), but it flattens and deforms dramatically.

2. Unbalanced Forces (ΣF ≠ 0)

If the resultant of all concurrent forces acting upon a body is greater than zero, the forces are termed unbalanced forces.

  • An unbalanced force acts as the sole physical cause of acceleration. If a net force exists, the object's velocity MUST change — it must speed up, slow down, or alter its path.
  • When children play Tug of War, if Team A pulls with 500 N to the left and Team B pulls with 500 N to the right, the rope stays stationary (balanced). But if Team A pulls with 650 N and Team B pulls with 500 N, the net unbalanced force is 150 N to the left. The rope instantly accelerates towards Team A!

2.3 Friction: The Ever-Present Opposing Force

Why does a rolling ball on a tiled floor gradually slow down and stop? Why doesn't a bicycle keep rolling forever when you stop pedaling? The ancient Greeks mistakenly believed that objects possessed a "natural tendency" to come to rest. The truth is that an unseen contact force called friction acts continuously against the motion.

What is Friction? Friction is an opposing contact force that arises tangentially at the interface between two surfaces whenever one surface moves or attempts to move across the other. It acts parallel to the contact surfaces in a direction exactly opposite to the relative motion.

The Microscopic Origin of Friction: Even surfaces that appear mirror-smooth to the naked eye are covered with microscopic hills and valleys (called asperities). When two surfaces touch, their asperities interlock like jigsaw puzzle teeth, forming microscopic atomic bonds (cold welds). To slide one object over another, an external force must shear these interlocked junctions.

S
Static Friction (f_s): The friction that exists between stationary surfaces. It is a self-adjusting force: if you push a heavy wooden box with 5 N, friction pushes back with 5 N. If you push with 15 N, friction pushes back with 15 N. It increases until it reaches a maximum threshold called limiting friction.
K
Kinetic / Sliding Friction (f_k): Once the applied force overcomes limiting friction, the box begins to slide. Asperities do not get enough time to deeply interlock, so sliding friction is always slightly less than limiting static friction.
R
Rolling Friction (f_r): When a wheel or ball rolls, the contact area at any instant is minute, and the surfaces do not rub sideways. Rolling friction is roughly 100 to 1000 times smaller than sliding friction, which is why humanity invented wheels and ball bearings!

2.4 Galileo's Incline Plane Experiments & The Historic Fallacy of Aristotle

For nearly two thousand years, Western natural philosophy was dominated by the teachings of the Greek philosopher Aristotle (384–322 BCE). Aristotle posited what seemed like common sense: "An external force is required to keep a body in uniform motion; when the force ceases, the motion ceases."

This was an enormous fallacy. Aristotle failed to recognize that a rolling cart stops not because of a lack of force, but because of the active presence of an opposing resistive force: friction! In the early 1600s, the Italian polymath Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) shattered this dogma using a series of brilliant thought experiments with inclined planes.

Figure 2.1: Galileo's Double Inclined Plane Thought Experiment. When the opposing plane is made horizontal, the ball must travel forever without needing an external force, proving that uniform motion is a natural, unforced state.

Galileo's Three Step Deduction:

  1. A marble rolling down an inclined plane experiences a downward component of gravity and accelerates (speed increases).
  2. A marble rolling up an inclined plane works against gravity and decelerates (speed decreases).
  3. When a marble rolls down an incline and up an identical smooth slope, it climbs until it reaches nearly the original vertical height from which it was released.
  4. If the slope of the second plane is reduced, the marble has to roll a longer distance to attain that same vertical height.
  5. The Master Deduction: If the second plane is made completely horizontal (slope = 0°), the marble can never reach its original release height! Therefore, in the ideal absence of friction, it will continue rolling indefinitely at a constant velocity along the straight horizontal surface!
Galileo's Revolutionary Conclusion (CBSE Core Focus) The natural state of an object is either rest OR uniform motion along a straight line. An external unbalanced force is NOT required to maintain uniform motion; a force is required ONLY to overcome friction or to change the speed or direction of motion!

2.5 Newton's First Law of Motion: The Law of Inertia

Building upon Galileo's revolutionary insights, Sir Isaac Newton (1642–1727) formulated the fundamental laws of classical mechanics in his monumental 1687 treatise, Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica. His First Law of Motion formally codifies the concept of inertia.

Newton's First Law of Motion (Statement) An object continues to remain in its state of rest or of uniform motion along a straight line unless it is compelled to change that state by the action of an applied external unbalanced force.

Understanding "Inertia" — The Inherent Laziness of Nature

The term inertia originates from the Latin word iners, meaning idle, inactive, or lazy. Inertia is the intrinsic property of all matter that causes it to resist any change in its velocity. Matter is stubbornly obedient: if left undisturbed, whatever it is doing, it keeps doing!

The Three Distinct Types of Inertia

Physics classifies inertia into three observable manifestations based on what state is resisting change:

Figure 2.2: Dual Manifestations of Inertia — (Left) Inertia of rest in the coin-cardboard-tumbler experiment; (Right) Inertia of motion pitching passenger forward when bus brakes suddenly.
A
1. Inertia of Rest: The resistance of a body to being set into motion.
• Dust from carpet: When a hanging carpet is beaten with a stick, the carpet fabric rapidly accelerates away. The dust particles remain at rest due to inertia of rest and drop down under gravity.
• Bus starting suddenly: Standing passengers lean backwards because their feet touch the bus floor and move forward instantly with the bus, while their upper torso stays behind due to inertia of rest.
• Detachment of tree leaves: When a branch is vigorously shaken, the branch moves rapidly back and forth, while leaves remain at rest and snap off at their fragile petioles.
B
2. Inertia of Motion: The resistance of a moving body to coming to rest.
• Emergency braking in a car: When a high-speed vehicle brakes violently, the passengers' feet and hips are locked by seat cushions, but the upper torso lurches violently forward due to inertia of motion (necessitating seatbelts).
• Luggage on bus roof: During rapid deceleration, unsecured luggage continues moving forward and flies off the front roof.
• Long jump athlete: An athlete sprints before taking off so that their high forward running momentum is carried into their airborne trajectory.
C
3. Inertia of Direction: The resistance of a moving body to changing its straight path.
• Car negotiating a sharp bend: When a car turns sharply to the left, passengers lean outward to the right because their bodies try to continue along their original straight line.
• Sparks from a grinding stone: Glowing iron particles sheared off a rotating abrasive wheel shoot out along the instantaneous tangent.
• Mudguards on bicycles: Mud flying off spinning bicycle tires flies straight along the tangential line of motion; mudguards intercept these droplets to protect the rider.

Inertia and Mass: The Quantitative Relationship

Does every object possess the same amount of inertia? Obviously not! If you gently kick an empty plastic football, it flies into the air. If you kick a stone of the exact same size with the same force, your foot will suffer severe pain while the stone barely budges! Pushing a toy cart requires a tiny finger push; pushing a real 100-tonne railway locomotive requires a giant diesel engine.

Mass is the True Quantitative Measure of Inertia Mass is the intrinsic scalar measure of an object's inertia. The larger the mass of a body, the greater is its inertia (the harder it is to accelerate, decelerate, or change its direction). A 5-rupee coin has more mass and therefore more inertia than a 1-rupee coin. A train has immensely more inertia than a bicycle.

2.6 Linear Momentum (p = mv)

Why does a small metallic bullet (mass = only 10 grams) fired from a gun penetrate a solid wooden plank, while an identical bullet thrown gently with your bare hand bounces off harmlessly? Conversely, why does a massive cargo truck moving at a sluggish speed of just 5 km/h crush a brick boundary wall, while a lightweight bicycle moving at the same speed merely scratches the paint?

These observations prove that neither mass alone nor velocity alone is sufficient to quantify the physical impact or "punch" of a moving body. Nature couples both into a single fundamental dynamic quantity: Linear Momentum.

Definition and Mathematical Formulation of Momentum The momentum of a moving body is defined as the total quantity of motion contained within it, quantitatively equal to the product of its mass (m) and its instantaneous velocity (v):

p = m × v

• Physical Nature: Vector quantity (possesses both magnitude and direction).
• Direction: Always points in the exact same direction as the body's velocity vector (v).
• SI Unit: kilogram-metre per second (kg·m/s or kg m s⁻¹).
• CGS Unit: gram-centimetre per second (g·cm/s). (1 kg·m/s = 10⁵ g·cm/s).

Extreme Boundary Cases of Momentum:

  • Stationary Body (v = 0): Even if an aircraft carrier has a colossal mass of 100,000 tonnes, if it is moored at the dock (v = 0), its momentum is exactly zero (p = m × 0 = 0).
  • Minute Mass at Hyper-velocity: A micrometeorite in space weighing only 0.001 kg traveling at 30,000 m/s possesses a dangerous momentum of 30 kg·m/s, capable of piercing titanium spacecraft shielding.
  • Colossal Mass at Micro-velocity: An oil tanker of mass 200,000,000 kg creeping towards a jetty at 0.05 m/s possesses an immense momentum of 10,000,000 kg·m/s, requiring heavy tugboats to safely decelerate it.

2.7 Newton's Second Law of Motion: The Quantitative Law

While the First Law gave us a qualitative definition of force, Newton's Second Law of Motion provides the rigorous mathematical apparatus to quantify and calculate force.

Newton's Second Law of Motion (Formal Statement) The rate of change of momentum of an object is directly proportional to the applied unbalanced force, and takes place in the direction in which the force acts.

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation of F = ma

Let an object of mass m move along a straight line with an initial velocity u. Suppose a constant external unbalanced force F is applied uniformly on it for a duration of time t, causing its velocity to change to v.

  1. Initial momentum of the body: p₁ = m × u
  2. Final momentum of the body: p₂ = m × v
  3. Total change in momentum: Δp = p₂ - p₁ = m·v - m·u = m(v - u)
  4. Rate of change of momentum: Rate = Δp / t = m(v - u) / t
  5. From kinematics (Chapter 4), the rate of change of velocity is acceleration: a = (v - u) / t.
  6. Therefore, rate of change of momentum = m × a.
  7. According to Newton's Second Law:
    F ∝ m(v - u) / t  ⇒  F ∝ m × a
  8. Introducing a constant of proportionality k:
    F = k × m × a
Choice of the Constant k & Definition of One Newton The SI unit of force is deliberately chosen such that the proportionality constant k = 1.

One Unit of Force (1 Newton): Defined as that amount of force which produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² in an object of mass 1 kg.

Substituting F = 1, m = 1 kg, and a = 1 m/s² into the equation:
1 = k × (1 kg) × (1 m/s²)  ⇒  k = 1.

Hence, the fundamental equation of dynamics is established:
F = m × a  =  m(v - u) / t  =  dp / dt

Deriving Newton's First Law from the Second Law

A classic exam question asks: "Show that Newton's First Law is a special case of the Second Law."

From the second law, we know that F = m(v - u) / t, which can be rearranged as: F × t = m(v - u).

  • If no external unbalanced force acts on the body, then F = 0.
  • Substituting F = 0 gives: 0 = m(v - u).
  • Since mass m cannot be zero (m ≠ 0), we must have: v - u = 0 ⇒ v = u.
  • Physical Interpretation:
    • If the object was initially at rest (u = 0), then its final velocity v = 0. It remains at rest!
    • If the object was moving with initial velocity u, it continues moving with the exact same velocity v = u indefinitely.
    • This is the exact statement of Newton's First Law!

Impulse of Force & Peak Impact Reduction

When a force acts over a time interval Δt, we define Impulse (J) as the total effect of the force:

Impulse (J) = F × Δt = Δp = m(v - u)

Rearranging for Force: F = Δp / Δt. Notice the crucial relationship: Force is inversely proportional to time (Δt)! For a given change in momentum (Δp):

  • If you make the stopping time Δt very small, the impact force F becomes astronomically large.
  • If you make the stopping time Δt longer, the impact force F becomes cushioned and small!

2.8 Newton's Third Law of Motion: Mutual Action and Reaction

Have you ever tried jumping off a small rowboat onto the riverbank? As your body leaps forward onto dry land, the wooden boat kicks backwards away from the bank! When a rifle fires a bullet forward at supersonic speed, the heavy rifle recoils backwards into the soldier's shoulder. Why? Because forces in nature never exist in isolation; they always occur in equal, opposite pairs!

Newton's Third Law of Motion (Formal Statement) To every action, there is always an equal and opposite reaction.

In other words: Whenever Body A exerts a force on Body B (Action), Body B simultaneously exerts an equal and oppositely directed force back on Body A (Reaction):
FAB = - FBA

The Five Inviolable Truths of Newton's Third Law

CBSE exam traps frequently test these five fundamental principles:

  1. Forces ALWAYS exist in matched pairs: An isolated single force (a "force monopole") cannot exist anywhere in the universe. If you push a wall, the wall pushes you back.
  2. Action and Reaction act on TWO DIFFERENT bodies: Action acts on Body B; Reaction acts on Body A.
  3. Action and Reaction NEVER cancel each other out: Forces can cancel each other into equilibrium only if they act on the same body! Because action and reaction act on two distinctly different objects, they cannot produce equilibrium or cancel out.
  4. Action and Reaction are strictly SIMULTANEOUS: There is zero time delay. Neither force is the "cause" of the other; they are simultaneous manifestations of a single mutual interaction. You cannot touch without being touched!
  5. Equal forces can produce radically UNEQUAL accelerations: Since acceleration is inversely proportional to mass (a = F / m), when equal forces act on two bodies of vastly different masses, the lighter body accelerates violently while the heavier body barely budges!
Classic Student Blunder: The Horse and Cart Paradox Question: "If the horse pulls the cart with force F, and by Newton's Third Law the cart pulls the horse back with equal force -F, why does the cart move at all? Shouldn't the forces cancel out?"

Scientific Resolution: The cart's forward motion is determined ONLY by the forces acting on the cart! The horse exerts a forward force on the cart; if this forward pull exceeds the friction between the cart wheels and the road, the cart accelerates forward. Meanwhile, the horse accelerates forward because its hooves push backward against the Earth (Action), and the Earth pushes the horse's hooves forward with equal force (Reaction)! The forces act on different bodies, so they do NOT cancel!

2.9 The Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum

One of the most sacred, universal conservation principles in physics is the Conservation of Linear Momentum. It governs every interaction across reality — from subatomic quark collisions inside CERN's Large Hadron Collider to the violent collision of galaxies.

The Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum (Statement) In an isolated system (a system upon which no net external unbalanced force acts), the total linear momentum of the interacting bodies remains constant in magnitude and direction, regardless of the nature of the internal interactions or collisions between them.

Total Initial Momentum Before Collision = Total Final Momentum After Collision
Figure 2.4: Step-by-Step Collision Architecture — Demonstrating how mutual Newton's Third Law forces F_AB and F_BA strictly enforce the Universal Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum.

Rigorous Mathematical Proof of Momentum Conservation

Consider two spherical bodies A and B of masses mA and mB traveling in the same straight line with initial velocities uA and uB (where uA > uB). They collide for a brief duration t.

  1. During collision, sphere A exerts a force FAB on sphere B, and sphere B exerts a force FBA on sphere A.
  2. After the collision, let their final velocities along the straight line be vA and vB.
  3. From Newton's Second Law:
    • The force exerted by sphere A on sphere B is the rate of change of momentum of B:
    FAB = mB × aB = mB (vB - uB) / t
    • The force exerted by sphere B on sphere A is the rate of change of momentum of A:
    FBA = mA × aA = mA (vA - uA) / t
  4. According to Newton's Third Law, action and reaction forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction:
    FAB = - FBA
  5. Substituting the momentum rate expressions into the third law relation:
    mB (vB - uB) / t = - [mA (vA - uA) / t]
  6. Multiplying both sides by the collision duration t:
    mB (vB - uB) = - mA (vA - uA)
    mB vB - mB uB = - mA vA + mA uA
  7. Rearranging the negative terms onto their respective sides:
    mA uA + mB uB  =  mA vA + mB vB

Conclusion: The total momentum of the two bodies before collision is identically equal to the total momentum of the two bodies after collision! Momentum is strictly conserved.

Recoil Velocity of a Firearm

A classic application of momentum conservation is calculating how fast a pistol, rifle, or artillery cannon kicks backwards upon firing.

  • Before firing: Both rifle (mass M) and bullet (mass m) are at rest: Initial Momentum = 0.
  • After firing: Bullet flies forward with velocity v; gun recoils backward with velocity V.
  • By Conservation of Momentum:
    0 = m·v + M·V  ⇒  M·V = - m·v  ⇒  V = - (m × v) / M
  • Physical Insights:
    • The negative sign proves that the recoil velocity (V) is directed opposite to the bullet's velocity.
    • Because the gun mass M is roughly 100 to 500 times larger than the bullet mass m, the recoil velocity V is small and manageable, but still strong enough to bruise a shooter who does not hold the rifle firmly against their shoulder!
Newton's Third Law and the Conservation of Linear Momentum. • Fuel combusts in the combustion chamber at tremendous pressure and temperature. • Hot gases escape from the nozzle at supersonic exhaust speed (2,000 to 4,500 m/s) downward. • Total Momentum Balance (Isolated System): Δp_rocket + Δp_exhaust = 0 • Myth Debunked: Rockets do NOT push against air or ground! They work even better in the vacuum of space!

Activity 6.1: The Coin, Stiff Card & Glass Tumbler Experiment (Inertia of Rest)
Step 1: Objective & Experimental Setup

Objective: To demonstrate that a stationary object possesses inertia of rest and resists being set into motion when a sudden horizontal force acts on its support.
Apparatus: A clean glass tumbler, a stiff square postcard or playing card, and a heavy metallic coin (a 5-rupee coin is ideal due to its higher mass).

Step 2: Experimental Procedure

1. Place the glass tumbler on a stable horizontal table.
2. Cover the mouth of the tumbler with the stiff card.
3. Place the 5-rupee coin at the exact geometric center of the card, directly above the opening.
4. Give a sharp, swift horizontal flick to the card with your forefinger.

Step 3: Scientific Observations

The stiff card flies away horizontally across the room, while the metallic coin does not fly away with the card. Instead, the coin drops straight down into the glass tumbler with a distinct clinking sound.

Step 4: Underlying Dynamic Physics

The sharp finger flick applies an instantaneous external unbalanced force solely to the card. The card accelerates horizontally away in a fraction of a millisecond. Because the contact time is minuscule and the card surface is smooth, the frictional force transferred to the coin is negligibly small. Consequently, the heavy coin retains its inertia of rest. Once the supporting card is removed from underneath, the unbalanced downward force of gravity pulls the coin vertically into the tumbler.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway & Key Insight

Topper Insight: If you pull the card slowly instead of flicking it sharply, static friction has sufficient time to accelerate the coin, and the coin slides along with the card! High acceleration (sharp flick) minimizes interaction time Δt, keeping the coin at rest.

Activity 6.2: Stack of Carrom Coins & Fast Striker (Inertia of Rest vs Shear Force)
Step 1: Objective & Experimental Setup

Objective: To investigate how inertia of rest maintains the vertical stability of a stack when an instantaneous shear force acts on its base.
Apparatus: A smooth carrom board, a vertical stack of 10 to 12 carrom coins, fine boric acid powder, and a heavy plastic striker.

Step 2: Experimental Procedure

1. Arrange the carrom coins vertically into a neat, stable cylindrical pile at the center of the board.
2. Aim a heavy striker at the very bottom-most coin of the stack.
3. Strike the bottom coin with a sharp, forceful horizontal flick.

Step 3: Scientific Observations

The bottom coin shoots out cleanly across the board, while the remaining column of coins drops almost perfectly vertically down to rest on the board, remaining intact without toppling.

Step 4: Underlying Dynamic Physics

The striker delivers a large impulsive contact force exclusively to the lowest coin, accelerating it horizontally. The duration of contact is extremely brief (Δt ≈ 0.005 s). The friction between the lowest coin and the coin directly above it is unable to transfer significant horizontal momentum to the upper stack. Owing to their inertia of rest, the coins in the column remain at their horizontal position, and gravity pulls them vertically downward into the vacated space.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway & Key Insight

Exam Hack: If the striker is hit with low velocity (weak hit), the duration of contact is long, friction drags the upper coins horizontally, and the entire pile collapses chaotically.

Activity 6.3: Glass of Water & Sudden Turn / Braking (Inertia of Motion & Direction)
Step 1: Objective & Experimental Setup

Objective: To demonstrate liquid inertia of motion and inertia of direction during abrupt velocity alterations.
Apparatus: A wide-mouthed plastic beaker filled nearly to the brim with colored water.

Step 2: Experimental Procedure

1. Hold the beaker firmly in both hands and walk forward at a brisk, steady pace.
2. Stop abruptly without warning. Observe the water surface.
3. Walk briskly again, and this time, execute a sharp 90° right turn while maintaining forward speed.

Step 3: Scientific Observations

• Upon sudden stopping, water surges forward and spills over the front rim of the beaker.
• Upon executing a sharp right turn, water surges towards the left rim and spills outward.

Step 4: Underlying Dynamic Physics

• During Sudden Stop: The beaker stops due to the muscular force of your hands, but the water continues moving forward at its previous velocity due to inertia of motion.
• During Sudden Turn: The beaker turns right, but the water attempts to maintain its original straight-line trajectory due to inertia of direction, causing it to pile up against the outer (left) wall.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway & Key Insight

Real-World Application: Liquid fuel tanks in supertankers and space rockets have internal partitions (called baffles) to suppress dangerous liquid sloshing caused by fluid inertia!

Activity 6.4: Two Interconnected Spring Balances (Action-Reaction Equality)
Step 1: Objective & Experimental Setup

Objective: To verify experimentally that action and reaction forces are strictly equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
Apparatus: Two identical calibrated spring balances (Balance A and Balance B), a rigid wall hook.

Step 2: Experimental Procedure

1. Attach the ring of Balance A securely to a rigid wall hook.
2. Interlock the hook of Balance B with the hook of Balance A.
3. Pull firmly on the free ring of Balance B so both springs stretch.

Step 3: Scientific Observations

Both spring balances show the exact same numerical reading (e.g., if Balance B reads 15 N, Balance A also reads exactly 15 N), regardless of how hard or gently you pull!

Step 4: Underlying Dynamic Physics

When you pull Balance B, it exerts a force on Balance A (Action). Simultaneously, Balance A pulls back on Balance B with an equal force (Reaction). By Newton's Third Law, FA on B = - FB on A. Since each balance measures the tension passing through its own spring, both pointers indicate identical magnitudes.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway & Key Insight

CBSE Question: Even if Balance A is held by a 100 kg weightlifter and Balance B is pulled by a 30 kg child, both balances will show the EXACT same reading! Forces between two bodies are always equal.

Activity 6.5: Balloon Rocket on String (Conservation of Momentum & Reaction Thrust)
Step 1: Objective & Experimental Setup

Objective: To demonstrate linear momentum conservation and reaction propulsion using escaping pressurized air.
Apparatus: A long party balloon, a straight plastic drinking straw, adhesive tape, a long nylon thread tied tautly across two distant chairs.

Step 2: Experimental Procedure

1. Thread the nylon string through the plastic straw and stretch it tightly horizontally.
2. Inflate the balloon fully and hold its mouth closed with your fingers (do not tie it).
3. Tape the inflated balloon firmly along the length of the straw.
4. Release the neck of the balloon.

Step 3: Scientific Observations

As pressurized air rushes out backwards through the balloon's mouth, the straw-balloon assembly rockets forward along the string with high velocity in the opposite direction.

Step 4: Underlying Dynamic Physics

Before release, the system momentum is zero. When released, escaping air carries high backward momentum: pair = mair × (-vair). To conserve total momentum (ptotal = 0), the balloon acquires an equal forward momentum: pballoon = Mballoon × (+vballoon). Hence: Mballoon vballoon = mair vair.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway & Key Insight

Rocket Analogy: This activity is a miniature model of ISRO's PSLV or GSLV rocket! The escaping air represents the high-speed combustion exhaust gases.

Activity 6.6: Boiling Test Tube with Cork (Steam Recoil & Momentum Conservation)
Step 1: Objective & Experimental Setup

Objective: To demonstrate recoil velocity and momentum conservation in a closed thermal reaction system.
Apparatus: A strong borosilicate boiling test tube, water, a tight-fitting rubber cork, two long strings to suspend the tube horizontally, a Bunsen burner.

Step 2: Experimental Procedure

1. Put 10 mL of water into the boiling tube and seal it securely with the rubber cork.
2. Suspend the test tube horizontally from a stand using the two strings.
3. Gently heat the tube with the burner until the water boils vigorously and produces high-pressure steam.

Step 3: Scientific Observations

When steam pressure reaches a critical threshold, the cork pops out violently, shooting forward at high speed. Simultaneously, the suspended test tube kicks strongly backwards, swinging like a pendulum in the opposite direction.

Step 4: Underlying Dynamic Physics

The steam exerts a forward force on the cork (Action). By Newton's Third Law, the cork exerts an equal backward force on the steam and test tube (Reaction). By momentum conservation: mcork vcork + Mtube Vrecoil = 0 ⇒ Vrecoil = - (mcork vcork) / Mtube. The backward kick is the exact mechanical analog of the recoil of a firearm.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway & Key Insight

Safety Note: Always point the mouth of the boiling tube away from students. The high forward momentum of the cork can cause eye injury.

In-Text Set 1, Q1: Which of the following has more inertia: (a) a rubber ball and a stone of the same size? (b) a bicycle and a train? (c) a five-rupee coin and a one-rupee coin?
Step 1: Physical Identification

We are asked to compare the inertia of three pairs of everyday objects.

Step 2: Underlying Principle

Inertia is quantitatively measured by mass. Greater mass directly implies greater inertia (Inertia ∝ Mass).

Step 3: Comparative Analysis

(a) A rubber ball vs a stone of same size: A stone is much denser than a rubber ball of identical volume. Therefore, the mass of the stone is greater than the mass of the rubber ball. Hence, the stone has more inertia.

(c) A five-rupee coin vs a one-rupee coin: A five-rupee coin is thicker, heavier, and has more metallic mass than a one-rupee coin. Hence, the five-rupee coin has more inertia.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Objects with more inertia: (a) Stone, (b) Train, (c) Five-rupee coin.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Always cite the fundamental definition: "Mass is the measure of inertia." You will secure full marks on this standard 1-mark question.

In-Text Set 1, Q2: Football kicks trajectory: Identify the number of times velocity changes and identify the agent supplying the force in each case.
Step 1: Given Scenario

"A football player kicks a football to another player of his team who kicks the football towards the goal. The goalkeeper of the opposite team collects the football and kicks it towards a player of his own team."

Step 2: Underlying Principle

A change in velocity occurs whenever the speed changes, the direction of motion changes, or both change. According to Newton's First and Second Laws, every change in velocity requires an external agent applying a force.

Step 3: Event-by-Event Analysis

1. First Change in Velocity: The ball is set into motion from rest towards teammate.
• Agent supplying force: The foot of the first football player.
2. Second Change in Velocity: The teammate kicks the moving ball towards the goal, changing its direction and speed.
• Agent supplying force: The foot of the second player (teammate).
3. Third Change in Velocity: The goalkeeper collects (catches and stops) the ball, bringing its velocity to zero.
• Agent supplying force: The hands / body of the goalkeeper.
4. Fourth Change in Velocity: The goalkeeper kicks the stationary ball towards a player of his team, accelerating it.
• Agent supplying force: The foot of the goalkeeper.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

The velocity of the football changes a total of four (4) times.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Common student blunder: Forgetting that stopping the ball (reducing speed to zero) IS a change in velocity! Never skip the goalkeeper's catch.

In-Text Set 1, Q3: Explain why some of the leaves may get detached from a tree if we vigorously shake its branch.
Step 1: Physical Scenario

A tree branch with attached leaves is initially at rest, and is then subjected to rapid, vigorous shaking back and forth.

Step 2: Underlying Principle

Newton's First Law of Motion: Inertia of Rest. Stationary bodies tend to remain at rest unless acted upon by an external force.

Step 3: Mechanism of Detachment

When the branch is vigorously shaken, our hands exert a direct external force on the branch, setting it into rapid oscillating motion. However, the leaves attached to the branch possess inertia of rest and resist this sudden motion, attempting to remain in their original stationary position. This creates an enormous mechanical shear stress at the delicate junction (petiole) connecting the leaf stem to the branch. Once this stress exceeds the mechanical strength of the stem, the stem breaks, and the leaves detach and fall under gravity.

Step 4: Final Answer

Leaves detach due to inertia of rest, as the branch accelerates suddenly while the leaves attempt to stay at rest, shearing the fragile stalks.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

This exact mechanism explains why ripe mangoes, apples, and dry twigs fall when trees are buffeted by heavy thunderstorm winds.

In-Text Set 1, Q4: Why do you fall in the forward direction when a moving bus brakes to a stop and fall backwards when it accelerates from rest?
Step 1: Physical Scenario

Passengers standing inside a bus experiencing (a) sudden emergency braking, and (b) sudden forward acceleration from a standing halt.

Step 2: Underlying Principle

Inertia of Motion (case a) and Inertia of Rest (case b), mediated by contact friction between the passenger's feet and the bus floor.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Dynamical Explanation

Case (a) Falling Forward during Braking:
When the bus is moving at uniform velocity, the passenger's entire body shares that same forward velocity. When brakes are applied, the bus and the passenger's feet (which are in direct frictional contact with the bus floor) come to a rapid halt. However, the upper torso of the passenger has no direct contact with the floor and continues to move forward due to inertia of motion. As a result, the passenger pitches forward.

Case (b) Falling Backward during Acceleration:
When the bus is stationary, the entire body of the passenger is at rest. When the bus suddenly accelerates forward, the passenger's feet are dragged forward by the bus floor. However, the upper body resists this change due to inertia of rest and tends to remain where it was. Relative to the advancing floor, the upper body lags behind, causing the passenger to fall backward.

Step 4: Final Summary

• Sudden Braking ⇒ Forward pitch due to Inertia of Motion.
• Sudden Starting ⇒ Backward jerk due to Inertia of Rest.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

CBSE Topper Formula: Always divide the human body into two halves in your answer: (1) feet in contact with floor, and (2) upper torso governed by inertia!

In-Text Set 2, Q1: If action is always equal to the reaction, explain how a horse can pull a cart.
Step 1: The Apparent Paradox

If the horse pulls the cart forward with force Fcart, the cart pulls the horse backward with equal force -Fcart. How does forward motion occur?

Step 2: Underlying Principle

Newton's Third Law (Action-Reaction act on different bodies) and Newton's Second Law (Acceleration depends on the net unbalanced external force on that specific body).

Step 3: Complete Free-Body Analysis

1. To pull the cart forward, the horse pushes the ground backward with its hooves at a slant angle (Action).
2. According to Newton's Third Law, the ground exerts an equal and opposite forward reaction force on the horse's hooves (Reaction, R).
3. The horizontal component of this forward ground reaction force is Fforward.
4. The backward forces acting on the horse-cart system are the backward pull of the cart and the opposing rolling friction between the cart wheels and the ground (ffriction).
5. As long as the horse pushes hard enough against the Earth so that Fforward > ffriction, there is a net unbalanced forward force acting on the system:

Fnet = Fforward - ffriction > 0
6. By Newton's Second Law, this net unbalanced force accelerates the horse and cart forward!

Step 4: Final Resolution

The cart moves because the action and reaction forces between horse and cart act on different bodies and do not cancel. The external forward force supplied by the Earth's reaction on the horse's hooves exceeds the wheel friction, accelerating the system forward.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

If you placed the horse and cart on frictionless ice, the horse's hooves would slip backwards, no forward reaction could be generated, and the horse could NEVER pull the cart!

In-Text Set 2, Q2: Explain, why is it difficult for a fireman to hold a hose, which ejects large amounts of water at a high velocity.
Step 1: Physical Scenario

A firefighter holding a heavy fire hose nozzle emitting hundreds of liters of water per minute at supersonic or high hydrodynamic exit velocity.

Step 2: Underlying Principle

Newton's Third Law of Motion & Rate of Change of Linear Momentum: Ejecting mass at high velocity carries high forward momentum, creating a colossal backward reaction force.

Step 3: Dynamical Derivation

Let mass Δm of water be ejected in time interval Δt with forward velocity v.
• The forward momentum carried away per second by water is the rate of change of momentum:

Fwater = Δp / Δt = (Δm / Δt) × v
• Because both Δm/Δt (mass flow rate) and v (velocity) are extremely large, Fwater is hundreds or thousands of Newtons.
• By Newton's Third Law, the exiting water exerts an equal and opposite backward reaction force on the hose nozzle:
Frecoil = - Fwater
• This immense backward recoil force pushes the hose violently backwards and tends to wrench it out of the firefighter's grasp.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

It is difficult to hold the hose because the high-speed ejection of massive water volume exerts an equal and opposite backward reaction force (recoil) on the hose.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

This is why two or three muscular firefighters often hold a single high-pressure hose together, bracing their feet firmly against the ground!

In-Text Set 2, Q3: From a rifle of mass 4 kg, a bullet of mass 50 g is fired with an initial velocity of 35 m/s. Calculate the initial recoil velocity of the rifle.
Step 1: Given Data & Unit Conversions

• Mass of rifle (M) = 4 kg
• Mass of bullet (m) = 50 g = 50 / 1000 kg = 0.05 kg
• Velocity of bullet after firing (v) = + 35 m/s
• Initial velocity of rifle and bullet before firing: U = 0, u = 0
• Initial recoil velocity of rifle (V) = ?

Step 2: Underlying Principle & Formula

By the Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum (isolated system with zero external force):

Total Momentum Before Firing = Total Momentum After Firing
(M + m) × 0 = M × V + m × v
V = - (m × v) / M

Step 3: Mathematical Computation

Substitute the given numerical values:
V = - (0.05 kg × 35 m/s) / 4 kg
V = - (1.75) / 4
V = - 0.4375 m/s ≈ - 0.44 m/s

Step 4: Final Result with Physical Direction

The initial recoil velocity of the rifle is 0.44 m/s (or - 0.4375 m/s). The negative sign indicates that the rifle moves in the direction opposite to that of the bullet (i.e., backwards into the shooter's shoulder).

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Trap alert: Did you remember to convert 50 g into 0.05 kg? Leaving 50 g unconverted gives V = -437.5 m/s — a fatal error that will score 0 marks!

In-Text Set 2, Q4: Two objects of masses 100 g and 200 g moving at 2 m/s and 1 m/s collide. After collision, first object moves at 1.67 m/s. Find velocity of second object.
Step 1: Given Data & SI Conversions

• Mass of object 1 (m₁) = 100 g = 0.1 kg
• Mass of object 2 (m₂) = 200 g = 0.2 kg
• Initial velocity of object 1 (u₁) = + 2 m/s
• Initial velocity of object 2 (u₂) = + 1 m/s (same line and direction)
• Final velocity of object 1 after collision (v₁) = + 1.67 m/s
• Final velocity of object 2 after collision (v₂) = ?

Step 2: Underlying Principle & Formula

By the Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum:

m₁ u₁ + m₂ u₂ = m₁ v₁ + m₂ v₂

Step 3: Mathematical Computation

1. Compute Total Initial Momentum:
pinitial = (0.1 kg × 2 m/s) + (0.2 kg × 1 m/s)
pinitial = 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4 kg·m/s
2. Set up Final Momentum:
pfinal = (0.1 kg × 1.67 m/s) + (0.2 kg × v₂)
pfinal = 0.167 + 0.2 v₂
3. Equate Initial and Final Momentum:
0.4 = 0.167 + 0.2 v₂
0.2 v₂ = 0.4 - 0.167 = 0.233
v₂ = 0.233 / 0.2 = 1.165 m/s

Step 4: Final Result

The velocity of the second object after the collision is 1.165 m/s (or 1.17 m/s) in the same original direction of motion.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Sanity check: Object 1 slowed down from 2 m/s to 1.67 m/s, so it transferred momentum to Object 2, which accelerated from 1.0 m/s to 1.165 m/s. The physical result makes complete sense!

Example 6.1: A constant force acts on an object of mass 5 kg for a duration of 2 s, increasing velocity from 3 m/s to 7 m/s. Find the force. If applied for 5 s, find final velocity.
Step 1: Given Data

• Mass (m) = 5 kg
• Initial velocity (u) = 3 m/s
• Final velocity (v) = 7 m/s
• Time duration (t) = 2 s
• Force (F) = ?
• For t' = 5 s with same force: v' = ?

Step 2: Governing Formulas

1. Newton's Second Law: F = m(v - u) / t
2. First Equation of Motion: v' = u + (F / m) × t'

Step 3: Step-by-Step Computation

Part A: Finding the applied force F:
F = 5 × (7 - 3) / 2 = 5 × 4 / 2 = 5 × 2 = 10 N

Part B: Finding final velocity after 5 seconds:
Acceleration a = F / m = 10 N / 5 kg = 2 m/s²
v' = u + a × t' = 3 + (2 × 5) = 3 + 10 = 13 m/s

Step 4: Final Answer

• Applied Force F = 10 N
• Final velocity after 5 seconds v' = 13 m/s

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Remember that a constant force produces a constant acceleration! Once acceleration is known (2 m/s²), you can find velocity at any time using v = u + at.

Example 6.2: Which would require a greater force — accelerating a 2 kg mass at 5 m/s² or a 4 kg mass at 2 m/s²?
Step 1: Given Data

• Case 1: m₁ = 2 kg, a₁ = 5 m/s²
• Case 2: m₂ = 4 kg, a₂ = 2 m/s²

Step 2: Formula

Newton's Second Law: F = m × a

Step 3: Computation

• Force for Case 1: F₁ = m₁ × a₁ = 2 kg × 5 m/s² = 10 N
• Force for Case 2: F₂ = m₂ × a₂ = 4 kg × 2 m/s² = 8 N
Comparing the two values: 10 N > 8 N (F₁ > F₂).

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Accelerating a 2 kg mass at 5 m/s² requires a greater force (10 N vs 8 N).

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Notice that acceleration contributes just as strongly to force as mass does! A lighter mass undergoing high acceleration can demand more force than a heavier mass undergoing gentle acceleration.

Example 6.3: A motorcar moving at 108 km/h takes 4 s to stop after brakes are applied. Find braking force if total mass is 1000 kg.
Step 1: Given Data & SI Unit Conversion

• Mass of car with passengers (m) = 1000 kg
• Initial velocity (u) = 108 km/h = 108 × (5 / 18) = 6 × 5 = 30 m/s
• Final velocity (v) = 0 m/s (comes to stop)
• Time taken (t) = 4 s
• Braking force (F) = ?

Step 2: Formulas

Acceleration: a = (v - u) / t
Force: F = m × a

Step 3: Computation

a = (0 - 30) / 4 = - 30 / 4 = - 7.5 m/s²
F = m × a = 1000 kg × (- 7.5 m/s²) = - 7500 N

Step 4: Final Result

The force exerted by the brakes is - 7500 N (magnitude = 7500 N). The negative sign signifies that the braking force opposes the forward motion of the car.

Always state both the magnitude (7500 N) and explain what the negative sign means physically to gain full marks from examiners.

Example 6.4: A force of 5 N gives m₁ an acceleration of 10 m/s² and m₂ an acceleration of 20 m/s². What acceleration would it give if both masses were tied together?
Step 1: Given Data

• Force applied (F) = 5 N
• Acceleration of mass 1 (a₁) = 10 m/s²
• Acceleration of mass 2 (a₂) = 20 m/s²

Step 2: Formulas

From F = ma: m = F / a
When tied together: Total mass M = m₁ + m₂
Combined acceleration: a = F / M

Step 3: Computation

• m₁ = F / a₁ = 5 N / 10 m/s² = 0.5 kg
• m₂ = F / a₂ = 5 N / 20 m/s² = 0.25 kg
• Combined mass M = m₁ + m₂ = 0.5 + 0.25 = 0.75 kg = 3/4 kg
• Combined acceleration a = F / M = 5 / 0.75 = 5 / (3/4) = 20 / 3 = 6.67 m/s²

Step 4: Final Result

If both masses are tied together, the force of 5 N will produce an acceleration of 6.67 m/s² (or 20/3 m/s²).

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Notice: You cannot simply average the accelerations! You MUST calculate the individual masses first, sum them up, and then apply F / M.

Example 6.5: Velocity-time graph of a ball of mass 20 g moving along a straight line on a long table shows velocity drops from 20 cm/s to 0 in 10 s. Find force exerted by table.

a = (0 - 0.2) / 10 = - 0.2 / 10 = - 0.02 m/s²

The frictional force exerted by the table to bring the ball to rest is - 0.0004 N (or - 4 × 10⁻⁴ N). The negative sign indicates the force opposes motion.

Double trap alert: This problem requires converting BOTH grams to kilograms AND centimeters per second to meters per second! Double check your powers of ten.

Example 6.6: A bullet of mass 20 g is fired horizontally at 150 m/s from a pistol of mass 2 kg. What is the recoil velocity of the pistol?
Step 1: Given Data & SI Unit Conversions

• Mass of bullet (m) = 20 g = 20 / 1000 kg = 0.02 kg
• Mass of pistol (M) = 2 kg
• Bullet muzzle velocity (v) = + 150 m/s
• Initial velocities: u = 0, U = 0
• Recoil velocity of pistol (V) = ?

Step 2: Formula

By Conservation of Momentum:

Total Initial Momentum = Total Final Momentum ⇒ 0 = m·v + M·V ⇒ V = - (m × v) / M

Step 3: Computation

V = - (0.02 kg × 150 m/s) / 2 kg = - (3) / 2 = - 1.5 m/s

The recoil velocity of the pistol is - 1.5 m/s (speed = 1.5 m/s in the backward direction).

Note how much lighter the bullet is compared to the gun (0.02 kg vs 2 kg — a ratio of 1:100). Consequently, the bullet gets 150 m/s while the gun recoils at only 1.5 m/s!

Example 6.7: A girl of mass 40 kg jumps with a horizontal velocity of 5 m/s onto a stationary cart with frictionless wheels (mass 3 kg). What is her velocity when cart starts moving?
Step 1: Given Data

• Mass of girl (m₁) = 40 kg
• Initial velocity of girl (u₁) = + 5 m/s
• Mass of stationary cart (m₂) = 3 kg
• Initial velocity of cart (u₂) = 0 m/s
• Combined final velocity (v) = ?

Step 2: Formula

Since no external unbalanced horizontal force acts, total momentum is conserved:

m₁ u₁ + m₂ u₂ = (m₁ + m₂) × v ⇒ v = (m₁ u₁) / (m₁ + m₂)

Step 3: Computation

Total Initial Momentum = (40 × 5) + (3 × 0) = 200 kg·m/s
Total Combined Mass = 40 + 3 = 43 kg
v = 200 / 43 ≈ + 4.65 m/s

Step 4: Final Result

The common final velocity of the girl on the cart is + 4.65 m/s in the direction of the girl's initial leap.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

This is a classic completely inelastic collision where the two interacting bodies stick together after impact and share a common final velocity.

Example 6.8: Two hockey players collide and become entangled. Player 1 (60 kg at 5.0 m/s) and Player 2 (55 kg at 6.0 m/s towards Player 1). In which direction and with what velocity will they move?
Step 1: Given Data & Direction Convention

Let motion from left to right be the positive (+) direction.
• Player 1: m₁ = 60 kg, u₁ = + 5.0 m/s (moving left to right)
• Player 2: m₂ = 55 kg, u₂ = - 6.0 m/s (moving right to left towards player 1)
• Entangled combined mass: M = m₁ + m₂ = 60 + 55 = 115 kg
• Combined velocity after entanglement (v) = ?

Step 2: Formula

By Conservation of Linear Momentum:

m₁ u₁ + m₂ u₂ = (m₁ + m₂) × v

Step 3: Computation

1. Momentum of Player 1: p₁ = 60 × (+ 5.0) = + 300 kg·m/s
2. Momentum of Player 2: p₂ = 55 × (- 6.0) = - 330 kg·m/s
3. Total Initial Momentum: pinitial = (+ 300) + (- 330) = - 30 kg·m/s
4. Equating to final momentum:
115 × v = - 30 ⇒ v = - 30 / 115 = - 0.26 m/s

Step 4: Final Result

The two entangled players will move together with a velocity of 0.26 m/s in the direction of the second player's initial motion (from right to left, as indicated by the negative sign).

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Crucial exam insight: Even though Player 1 has a greater mass (60 kg vs 55 kg), Player 2 had a larger initial momentum magnitude (330 kg·m/s vs 300 kg·m/s). The system always moves in the direction of the larger initial momentum!

Exercise Q1: An object experiences a net zero external unbalanced force. Is it possible for the object to be travelling with a non-zero velocity? State conditions.
Step 1: Question Analysis

We are asked whether a non-zero velocity can exist when net external force ΣF = 0.

Step 2: Scientific Principle

Newton's First and Second Laws: F = m × a. If F = 0, then acceleration a = 0. Zero acceleration means velocity is CONSTANT (it does not mean velocity must be zero!).

Step 3: Necessary Conditions

Yes, it is entirely possible! For an object to travel with a non-zero velocity under net zero external force, the following conditions must be satisfied:
1. Magnitude of Velocity must remain strictly constant: The speed cannot increase or decrease.
2. Direction of Velocity must remain strictly unchanged: The object must move strictly in a straight line (no turning).
3. Opposing forces like friction and air resistance must be zero (or perfectly balanced by a driving force): Any uncompensated resistance would make ΣF ≠ 0.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Yes. The object will continue in uniform straight-line motion at constant velocity.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Voyager 1 cruising through interstellar space is a real-life example: with its thrusters turned off and zero gravity/drag, it moves forever at ~17 km/s with zero force!

Exercise Q2: When a carpet is beaten with a stick, dust comes out of it. Explain.
Step 1: Physical System

A hanging rug or carpet embedded with fine dust particles, struck vigorously with a stick.

Step 2: Principle

Newton's First Law: Inertia of Rest.

Step 3: Mechanism

Initially, both the carpet fibers and the dust particles trapped within them are at rest. When struck by a stick, the stick exerts a sudden external force on the carpet fabric, causing it to accelerate rapidly forward. However, the dust particles possess inertia of rest and resist this sudden motion, remaining in their original spatial position. Consequently, the carpet moves away from underneath the dust particles. Once freed from the fabric, the dust particles fall vertically down under the influence of gravity.

Step 4: Final Answer

Dust emerges because the carpet accelerates away when struck while dust particles stay at rest due to inertia of rest and drop under gravity.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Always highlight the keywords: "Inertia of rest" and "Carpet accelerates forward while dust remains at rest".

Exercise Q3: Why is it advised to tie any luggage kept on the roof of a bus with a rope?
Step 1: Scenario

Luggage placed unsecured on the roof of a passenger bus traversing roads with stops, acceleration, and sharp turns.

Step 2: Principle

Inertia of Rest, Motion, and Direction.

Step 3: Analysis of Dynamic Scenarios

1. When the bus starts suddenly: The bus roof accelerates forward, but the luggage tends to remain at rest due to inertia of rest, causing it to slide backwards and potentially fall off the rear of the bus.
2. When the bus applies sudden emergency brakes: The bus stops rapidly, but the luggage continues moving forward due to inertia of motion, sliding forward and crashing down over the front windshield.
3. When the bus takes a sharp turn: The bus changes direction, but the luggage tends to continue in its original straight line due to inertia of direction, sliding sideways and toppling off the roof.

Step 4: Final Answer

Tying luggage with a rope provides the necessary external tension force to counteract inertia in all three dimensions, preventing luggage from falling off during starting, braking, or turning.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Full-mark answers must mention all THREE scenarios: sudden start (falls back), sudden brake (falls forward), and sharp turn (falls sideways).

Exercise Q4: A batsman hits a cricket ball which rolls on level ground and comes to rest after a short distance. Why does it stop?
Step 1: NCERT Options

(a) The batsman did not hit the ball hard enough.
(b) Velocity is proportional to the force exerted on the ball.
(c) There is a force on the ball opposing the motion.
(d) There is no unbalanced force on the ball, so the ball would want to come to rest.

Step 2: Correct Choice

Correct Option: (c)

Step 3: Detailed Justification

By Newton's First Law, an object in motion will continue in uniform motion indefinitely unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force. As the cricket ball rolls on the ground, two opposing forces act upon it: (1) rolling friction between the leather surface of the ball and the grass/soil, and (2) air resistance. These opposing forces provide a continuous negative acceleration (deceleration) that gradually drains the ball's momentum until its velocity becomes zero.

Step 4: Fallacy Refutation

Option (b) is Aristotle's ancient fallacy! Velocity is NOT proportional to applied force; rather, acceleration is proportional to net unbalanced force.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

If the same ball rolled on a perfectly smooth, frictionless surface in a vacuum, it would roll FOREVER without stopping!

Exercise Q5: A truck starts from rest, rolls down a hill with constant acceleration, travels 400 m in 20 s. Find acceleration and force if mass is 7 tonnes.
Step 1: Given Data & SI Unit Conversions

• Initial velocity (u) = 0 m/s (starts from rest)

• Time taken (t) = 20 s
• Mass of truck (m) = 7 tonnes = 7 × 1000 kg = 7000 kg
• Acceleration (a) = ?
• Force (F) = ?

2. Dynamics (Newton's Second Law): F = m × a

Part A: Finding acceleration a:
400 = (0 × 20) + ½ × a × (20)²
400 = ½ × a × 400 = 200 a
a = 400 / 200 = 2 m/s²

Part B: Finding force F:

Step 4: Final Results

• Acceleration of the truck = 2 m/s²
• Force acting on the truck = 14,000 N

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Always remember: 1 metric tonne = 1000 kg. Forgetting this conversion results in 14 N instead of 14,000 N!

Exercise Q6: A stone of 1 kg is thrown with velocity 20 m/s across frozen lake and comes to rest after 50 m. What is the friction force?
Step 1: Given Data

• Final velocity (v) = 0 m/s (comes to rest)
• Distance traveled (s) = 50 m
• Friction force (F) = ?

Step 2: Formulas

2. Force equation: F = m × a

Whenever an object comes to rest over a known distance, always use v² = u² + 2as to find acceleration before computing force.

Exercise Q7: 8000 kg engine pulls train of 5 wagons (2000 kg each), engine force 40000 N, friction 5000 N. Find net force, acceleration, and force of wagon 1 on wagon 2.
Step 1: Given Data

• Mass of engine (me) = 8000 kg
• Number of wagons = 5; Mass of each wagon (mw) = 2000 kg
• Total mass of 5 wagons = 5 × 2000 = 10,000 kg
• Total mass of the whole train (M) = 8000 + 10,000 = 18,000 kg
• Forward force exerted by engine (Fengine) = 40,000 N
• Opposing track friction (f) = 5,000 N

Step 2: Formulas

(a) Net accelerating force: Fnet = Fengine - f
(b) Acceleration of train: a = Fnet / Mtotal
(c) Force of wagon 1 on wagon 2: Wagon 1 pulls the remaining 4 wagons behind it (wagons 2, 3, 4, 5). Mass of 4 wagons = 4 × 2000 = 8000 kg. Force = m4 wagons × a.

Step 3: Computation

(a) Net Accelerating Force:
Fnet = 40,000 N - 5,000 N = 35,000 N

(b) Acceleration of the train:
a = Fnet / Mtotal = 35,000 N / 18,000 kg = 35 / 18 ≈ 1.944 m/s²

(c) Force of wagon 1 on wagon 2:
Behind the coupling of wagon 1 and wagon 2, there are 4 wagons (wagons 2, 3, 4, 5).
Mass behind coupling = 4 × 2000 kg = 8,000 kg.
Force = mass × acceleration = 8,000 × (35 / 18) = 140,000 / 9 ≈ 15,555.56 N

Step 4: Final Results

(a) Net accelerating force = 35,000 N
(b) Acceleration of train = 1.94 m/s² (or 35/18 m/s²)
(c) Force of wagon 1 on wagon 2 = 15,555.56 N

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

Critical distinction: To find the total acceleration, you must include the engine's mass (18,000 kg). But for the force of wagon 1 on wagon 2, only the mass of the trailing 4 wagons (8,000 kg) is pulled!

Exercise Q12: Student justifies that parked truck does not move because action and reaction cancel each other. Comment on this logic.
Step 1: Student's Argument

A student argues: "When we push a massive truck, the truck pushes back on us with an equal and opposite force. These two equal and opposite forces cancel each other out, which is why the truck does not move."

Step 2: Flaw in Student's Logic

The student's reasoning is completely flawed and unscientific!
Forces can cancel each other into equilibrium only if they act on the same object. By Newton's Third Law, the pushing force acts on the truck, while the reaction force acts on the person's hands. Because action and reaction act on two distinctly different bodies, they CANNOT cancel each other out!

Step 3: The True Scientific Reason

The real reason the truck does not move is the force of static friction between the heavy rubber tires of the truck and the rough road surface:
1. Because the truck is extremely massive (several tonnes), the normal reaction force N is enormous.
2. Maximum static friction is proportional to normal reaction: fmax = μs × N = μs × mg.
3. This limiting static friction is several thousand Newtons.
4. A human pushing with 300 N to 400 N is completely unable to overcome this huge static friction.
5. The static friction between road and tires automatically adjusts to 400 N in the opposite direction, keeping the net horizontal force on the truck equal to zero (Fnet = Fpush - ffriction = 0).

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Action and reaction do not cancel because they act on different bodies. The truck remains stationary because the human push is too small to overcome the large static friction between the truck's tires and the road.

Step 5: Teacher's Takeaway

This is one of the most frequently asked 3-mark conceptual questions in CBSE Class 9 examinations!

Exercise Q17: Akhtar, Kiran and Rahul debate insect hitting windshield of fast motorcar. Evaluate each person's suggestion.

• Rahul: "Both the motorcar and the insect experienced the same force and the same change in their momentum."

Step 2: Underpinning Laws

Newton's Third Law (Fcar = - Finsect) and the Law of Conservation of Momentum (Δpcar = - Δpinsect).

Step 3: Pedagogical Evaluation of Each Student

1. Kiran is WRONG: While it is true that the insect undergoes a massive change in velocity (Δv ≈ 30 m/s) compared to the car's imperceptible Δv, momentum change is Δp = m × Δv. Because the car's mass is gigantic and the insect's mass is tiny, their changes in momentum are strictly equal and opposite (Δpcar = - Δpinsect).

2. Akhtar is WRONG: By Newton's Third Law, forces always act in equal and opposite pairs. The force exerted by the car on the insect is exactly equal to the force exerted by the insect on the car's windshield. The car cannot exert a "larger force"!

3. Rahul is CORRECT: By Newton's Third Law, both the car and insect experience equal and opposite contact forces (Fcar = - Finsect). Because the interaction time Δt is identical, the impulse J = F × Δt is equal for both. Hence, both experience the exact same magnitude of change in momentum (Δp).

If forces are equal, why did the insect splatter while the car suffered no damage?
Acceleration is inversely proportional to mass: a = F / m.
• For the insect (mass m ≈ 0.0001 kg), the deceleration is catastrophic (a ≈ 10⁵ m/s²), obliterating its fragile exoskeleton.
• For the car (mass M ≈ 1200 kg), the deceleration is practically zero (a ≈ 10⁻⁴ m/s²).

Rahul gave the accurate scientific analysis! This question tests your ability to distinguish between Force, Acceleration, and Change in Momentum.

5.3 Short Answer Questions (2-Mark & 3-Mark SAQs)

SAQ 1 (2M): Define 1 Newton using Newton's Second Law. Derive its relation with the CGS unit 'dyne'.

Definition of 1 Newton [1 Mark]:
One Newton (1 N) is defined as that magnitude of external unbalanced force which produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² when applied to an object having a mass of 1 kg (1 N = 1 kg × 1 m/s²).

Conversion into CGS Dynes [1 Mark]:
• 1 kg = 1000 g = 10³ g
• 1 m/s² = 100 cm/s² = 10² cm/s²
• 1 N = (10³ g) × (10² cm/s²) = 10⁵ g·cm/s²
• Since 1 dyne = 1 g·cm/s², we have:
1 Newton = 10⁵ Dynes (or 1 Dyne = 10⁻⁵ N).

SAQ 2 (2M): Why are seatbelts and automatic airbags mandatory in passenger cars? Explain using impulse and momentum.

Step-by-Step Physics Breakdown [2 Marks]:
1. In a high-speed vehicular collision, the car stops almost instantly (Δt is extremely small), but the passenger's upper torso continues to move forward at high velocity due to inertia of motion.
2. By the Impulse-Momentum Theorem: F = Δp / Δt. The change in momentum Δp (from cruising speed to 0) is constant.
3. Seatbelts and airbags stretch and inflate, significantly increasing the time duration Δt over which the passenger's momentum is brought to zero.
4. Because Δt is increased by a factor of 10 to 20, the peak decelerating impact force F is dramatically reduced, preventing lethal head injuries against the steering wheel or windshield.

SAQ 3 (3M): Differentiate between Balanced and Unbalanced Forces on the basis of 3 physical criteria.
Criteria [1 Mark each] Balanced Forces Unbalanced Forces
1. Resultant Net Force (ΣF) The vector sum of all forces is strictly zero (ΣF = 0). The vector sum of all forces is non-zero (ΣF ≠ 0).
2. Effect on State of Motion Cannot change state of rest or uniform motion (acceleration = 0). Always changes speed and/or direction (acceleration ≠ 0).
3. Effect on Shape & Size Can change the shape and size (deformation) of an object (e.g., squeezing a rubber ball). Mainly causes acceleration; can also cause deformation during accelerating contact.
SAQ 4 (3M): State Newton's Second Law. Prove mathematically that the First Law is a special case of the Second Law.

Statement of Second Law [1 Mark]:
The rate of change of momentum of an object is directly proportional to the applied unbalanced force, and takes place in the direction of the force: F = m(v - u) / t = ma.

Mathematical Deduction of First Law [2 Marks]:
From Second Law: F × t = m(v - u)
If no external force acts on the object, then F = 0.
Substituting F = 0 into the equation:
0 = m(v - u)
Since mass m ≠ 0 for any real physical body, we must have:
v - u = 0 ⇒ v = u
• If the body was initially at rest (u = 0), then v = 0 (it remains at rest).
• If the body was moving with speed u, then v = u (it continues moving with constant velocity in a straight line).
This is the exact statement of Newton's First Law of Motion! Hence proved.

SAQ 6 (3M): A high jumper prefers to land on a foam mattress rather than a hard cement floor. Explain using Newton's Second Law.

Physics Analysis [3 Marks]:
1. When the athlete clears the bar and falls to the ground, they possess a large downward momentum p = mv just before landing.
2. When landing on a hard cement floor:
• The rigid concrete does not yield, stopping the athlete in an extremely tiny time interval (Δt ≈ 0.01 s).
• By Newton's Second Law: F = Δp / Δt. Because Δt is minuscule, the impact force F exerted on the bones and joints is huge, resulting in severe fractures.
3. When landing on a soft foam mattress or sand pit:
• The cushion depresses progressively, prolonging the stopping time (Δt ≈ 0.3 to 0.5 s).
• Because Δt is 30 to 50 times longer, the rate of change of momentum is greatly reduced, and the impact force F becomes gentle and harmless.

5.4 Long Answer Questions (5-Mark LAQs with Explicit Marking Schemes)

LAQ 1 (5M): State the Universal Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum. Derive it mathematically for two colliding bodies.
Marking Scheme Breakdown

• Statement of Law: [1 Mark]
• Diagram & Physical Assumptions: [1 Mark]
• Newton's Second Law Momentum Rate Derivation: [1.5 Marks]
• Newton's Third Law Substitution & Final Equation: [1.5 Marks]

1. Statement [1 Mark]:
In an isolated system (where no net external unbalanced force acts), the total linear momentum of the interacting bodies remains constant in magnitude and direction. Total momentum before collision equals total momentum after collision.

2. Assumptions & Setup [1 Mark]:
Consider two bodies A and B of masses mA and mB moving in the same straight line with initial velocities uA and uB (with uA > uB). They collide for a time duration t, and separate with final velocities vA and vB.

3. Application of Newton's Second Law [1.5 Marks]:
• Force exerted by Body A on Body B (FAB):
FAB = Rate of change of momentum of B = mB (vB - uB) / t … (Equation 1)
• Force exerted by Body B on Body A (FBA):
FBA = Rate of change of momentum of A = mA (vA - uA) / t … (Equation 2)

4. Application of Newton's Third Law [1.5 Marks]:
Action and reaction are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction:
FAB = - FBA
Substitute Equations 1 and 2:
mB (vB - uB) / t = - [mA (vA - uA) / t]
Multiplying both sides by t:
mB (vB - uB) = - mA (vA - uA)
mB vB - mB uB = - mA vA + mA uA
Rearranging the negative terms onto opposite sides:
mA uA + mB uB = mA vA + mB vB
Hence, Total Initial Momentum = Total Final Momentum. Q.E.D.

LAQ 2 (5M): (a) Define linear momentum. (b) A car of 1200 kg at 72 km/h is stopped in 50 m. Calculate a, t, F, and Δp.
Marking Scheme Breakdown

• Part (a) Definition & SI Unit: [1 Mark]
• Part (b)(i) Acceleration calculation: [1 Mark]
• Part (b)(ii) Stopping time calculation: [1 Mark]
• Part (b)(iii) Braking force calculation: [1 Mark]
• Part (b)(iv) Change in momentum: [1 Mark]

Part (a) Definition & SI Unit [1 Mark]:
Linear Momentum (p) is the total quantity of motion contained in a body, defined mathematically as the product of its mass (m) and its velocity (v): p = m × v. Its SI unit is kg·m/s.

Part (b) Numerical Solutions:
Given Data:
• Mass (m) = 1200 kg
• Initial velocity (u) = 72 km/h = 72 × (5 / 18) = 20 m/s
• Final velocity (v) = 0 m/s
• Stopping distance (s) = 50 m

(i) Braking Acceleration (a) [1 Mark]:
v² - u² = 2as ⇒ 0² - (20)² = 2 × a × 50
- 400 = 100 a ⇒ a = - 4 m/s² (Deceleration = 4 m/s²).

(ii) Stopping Time (t) [1 Mark]:
v = u + at ⇒ 0 = 20 + (- 4) × t ⇒ 4t = 20 ⇒ t = 5 seconds.

(iii) Braking Force (F) [1 Mark]:
F = m × a = 1200 kg × (- 4 m/s²) = - 4800 N (Magnitude = 4800 N opposing motion).

(iv) Change in Momentum (Δp) [1 Mark]:
Δp = m(v - u) = 1200 × (0 - 20) = - 24,000 kg·m/s (or - 24,000 N·s).

LAQ 3 (5M): (a) State Newton's Third Law & 3 key rules. (b) Derive gun recoil formula. Why does gun kick less than bullet?
Marking Scheme Breakdown

• Part (a) Third Law & 3 Rules: [2.5 Marks]
• Part (b) Derivation of Recoil Velocity: [1.5 Marks]
• Part (b) Acceleration disparity explanation: [1 Mark]

Part (a) Newton's Third Law & Core Tenets [2.5 Marks]:
Statement: To every action, there is always an equal and opposite reaction (FAB = - FBA).
Three Fundamental Tenets:
1. Action and reaction forces act on two distinctly different bodies simultaneously.
2. Action and reaction forces never cancel each other out.
3. Forces in nature always occur in matched pairs; an isolated single force cannot exist.

Part (b) Mathematical Derivation of Gun Recoil Velocity [1.5 Marks]:
Let mass of rifle = M, initial velocity = U = 0
Let mass of bullet = m, initial velocity = u = 0
Total initial momentum of system = (M + m) × 0 = 0.
After firing, bullet moves with velocity v, and rifle recoils with velocity V.
Total final momentum = M·V + m·v.
By Conservation of Linear Momentum:
0 = M·V + m·v ⇒ M·V = - m·v
V = - (m × v) / M

Why does the gun recoil with a much smaller velocity than the bullet? [1 Mark]:
By Newton's Second Law: a = F / m.
The force F exerted by bullet on gun equals the force exerted by gun on bullet.
However, Mgun >> mbullet (gun is 100 to 500 times more massive).
Since acceleration is inversely proportional to mass (a ∝ 1/m), the bullet experiences a massive acceleration and exits at supersonic speed, while the heavy rifle recoils with a gentle, manageable velocity.

LAQ 4 (5M): Galileo's double incline plane thought experiments and the overthrow of Aristotelian mechanics.
Marking Scheme Breakdown

• Aristotle's Fallacy & Historical Context: [1 Mark]
• Galileo's Symmetrical Incline Observations: [1.5 Marks]
• Decreased Slope & Horizontal Deduction: [1.5 Marks]
• Formal Connection to Newton's First Law: [1 Mark]

1. Aristotle's Fallacy [1 Mark]:
For 2000 years, Aristotle's doctrine held that "An external force is required to keep a body in uniform motion; when force is removed, motion ceases." Aristotle failed to recognize that a sliding body stops only because an opposing contact force — friction — is actively dragging it to rest.

2. Galileo's Symmetrical Incline Plane [1.5 Marks]:
Galileo studied a marble rolling down a smooth inclined plane:
• Rolling down: Gravity component accelerates the marble (speed increases).
• Rolling up an opposite plane: Gravity decelerates the marble (speed decreases).
• On a double inclined plane of equal slopes: The marble climbs up the second plane to reach nearly the exact same vertical height (h) from which it was released.

3. Decreased Slope and Horizontal Deduction [1.5 Marks]:
• When the inclination angle of the second plane is reduced, the marble still climbs to the same height h, but it must travel a longer distance (d) to reach it.
• The Grand Deduction: If the second plane is made completely horizontal (angle θ = 0°), the marble can NEVER reach its original height h!
• Therefore, in the ideal absence of friction, the marble will continue to roll indefinitely at a constant speed along the horizontal plane!

4. Connection to Newton's First Law [1 Mark]:
Galileo concluded that uniform motion in a straight line is as natural a state of matter as rest. Force is NOT required to maintain uniform motion; force is required ONLY to overcome friction or change velocity. This historic insight formed the exact foundation of Newton's First Law of Motion.

5.5 Competency-Based Case Studies (Real-World Applied Physics)

Case Study 1: Modern Automotive Crash Safety Dynamics Read the technical passage below and answer the four competency questions:

Modern automotive engineering relies heavily on classical dynamics to protect passengers during catastrophic collisions. In the 1950s, cars were manufactured with rigid steel chassis. In a 60 km/h collision with a solid bridge abutment, a rigid car stopped within 0.05 seconds, subjecting human occupants to lethal decelerations exceeding 35g (350 m/s²). Internal organs ruptured, and passengers broke ribs against steering columns.

Today, automobile crash cages feature engineered crumple zones at the front and rear, paired with three-point inertia-reel seatbelts and pyrotechnic airbags. When a crash occurs, the front crumple zone buckles progressively like an accordion, absorbing kinetic energy and extending the deceleration time from 0.05 seconds to 0.25 seconds. Simultaneously, airbags inflate within 30 milliseconds, preventing direct impact between the occupant's skull and the dashboard.
Case Study 1 — Sub-Questions (1 to 4) Solved

Sub-Question 1.1 (1M): Which physical law directly explains why a passenger's body slams forward when a car collides with a barrier?
Answer: Newton's First Law of Motion (Inertia of Motion). When the car halts violently, the passenger's upper torso maintains its cruising speed and continues moving forward.

Sub-Question 1.2 (1M): By what factor is the average impact force reduced when crumple zones extend the crash stopping time from 0.05 s to 0.25 s?
Answer: From F = Δp / Δt, force is inversely proportional to time.
F₂ / F₁ = Δt₁ / Δt₂ = 0.05 / 0.25 = 1 / 5.
The impact force is reduced by a factor of 5 (cut by 80%)!

Sub-Question 1.3 (1M): A 60 kg driver traveling at 20 m/s is brought to rest in 0.25 s by an airbag. Calculate the average force exerted on the driver.
Answer:
Δp = m(v - u) = 60 kg × (0 - 20 m/s) = - 1200 kg·m/s
F = Δp / Δt = - 1200 / 0.25 = - 4800 N (Magnitude = 4800 N).

Sub-Question 1.4 (1M): Why are rigid steel bumpers no longer used in modern consumer automobiles?
Answer: Rigid steel bumpers do not deform, resulting in an extremely tiny stopping time Δt and delivering catastrophic peak impact forces to the occupants.

Case Study 2: ISRO's Launch Vehicle Technology & Orbital Dynamics Read the technical passage below and answer the four competency questions:

India's Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV) and Launch Vehicle Mark 3 (LVM3) are multi-stage rockets developed by ISRO to deploy satellites into Sun-Synchronous and Geostationary Transfer Orbits. A rocket is essentially a propulsion device that operates on the Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum. Unlike aeroplanes, which intake atmospheric oxygen and push against air molecules, a rocket carries both its own fuel and liquid oxidizer internally.

During launch, combustion inside the rocket engine generates gas at temperatures exceeding 3000 K and colossal pressure. The convergent-divergent de Laval nozzle expands these gases, ejecting them downwards at supersonic exhaust velocities exceeding 3000 m/s. As billions of gas molecules carry downward momentum away, the rocket body is driven upwards. As propellant is consumed at rates of hundreds of kilograms per second, the rocket's overall mass decreases continuously.
Case Study 2 — Sub-Questions (1 to 4) Solved

Sub-Question 2.1 (1M): A student states: "Rockets cannot accelerate in outer space because there is no air to push against." Explain why this statement is completely false.
Answer: A rocket does NOT push against ambient air. It accelerates by ejecting its own combustion exhaust gases backwards at high speed. By Newton's Third Law and momentum conservation, the backward momentum of exhaust gases creates an equal forward momentum on the rocket body, operating flawlessly in the vacuum of space.

Sub-Question 2.2 (1M): If an ISRO rocket engine burns propellant at a rate of 120 kg/s and ejects exhaust gases at 2500 m/s, calculate the upward thrust generated.
Answer:
Thrust F = vexhaust × (Δm / Δt) = 2500 m/s × 120 kg/s = 300,000 N = 300 kN (or 3 × 10⁵ N).

Sub-Question 2.3 (1M): Why does the acceleration of a rocket increase continuously during its upward flight even if engine thrust remains constant?
Answer: By Newton's Second Law: a = Fthrust / Mrocket. Because propellant is continuously consumed and expelled, the mass Mrocket drops steadily. As denominator M decreases with constant thrust F, the acceleration 'a' progressively increases!

Sub-Question 2.4 (1M): Why do modern space launch vehicles utilize multiple stages (e.g., PSLV has 4 stages) instead of a single giant fuel tank?
Answer: Once fuel in a stage is exhausted, the dead structural weight (empty metal tanks) is jettisoned. This drastic reduction in rocket mass allows the subsequent stages to achieve the enormous orbital speeds (~8 km/s) required for spaceflight.

6.1 Five Fatal Student Blunders in Board Exams

Blunder 1: Believing Action and Reaction Cancel Out to Zero Why Students Fall Into This Trap: Students memorize that "Action equals minus Reaction" (FAB = - FBA) and falsely conclude that the net force on every interacting system is zero, making acceleration impossible.

The Scientific Truth: Forces can cancel each other into equilibrium only if they act on the same body! By Newton's Third Law, action and reaction act on two distinctly different bodies. A horse pushes the ground (Action); the ground pushes the horse (Reaction). The cart pulls the horse; the horse pulls the cart. Each individual body experiences its own unbalanced net force and accelerates!

How to Secure Full Marks: In any exam answer about Newton's Third Law, always state: "Action and reaction forces act on two different bodies and therefore never cancel each other."
Blunder 2: Confusing Normal Reaction with the 3rd Law Pair of Gravity (Weight) Why Students Fall Into This Trap: When a book rests on a table, the downward weight is W = mg and the upward normal contact force is N. Because N = mg, students mistakenly declare: "Normal reaction is the Newton's Third Law reaction to gravity!"

The Scientific Truth: This is completely false!
• Weight (W = mg) is the Earth pulling the book downwards. Its true Newton's Third Law reaction pair is the book pulling the Earth upwards!
• Normal contact force (N) is the table pushing the book upwards. Its true Newton's Third Law reaction pair is the book pushing the table downwards!
• N and W act on the same body (the book); hence they are balanced forces, NOT a Third Law pair!

How to Secure Full Marks: A Third Law pair must involve the exact same two interacting bodies and the exact same type of force (gravitational with gravitational, contact with contact).
Blunder 3: Forgetting Vector Direction Signs in Momentum Changes (Δp) Why Students Fall Into This Trap: When a ball moving at 10 m/s hits a wall and rebounds back at 8 m/s, students calculate: Δp = m(10 - 8) = 2m. This loses 100% of the numerical marks!

The Scientific Truth: Velocity is a vector quantity. If the initial velocity is positive (u = + 10 m/s), the rebounded velocity moving in the opposite direction MUST carry a negative sign (v = - 8 m/s).
Therefore: Δp = m(v - u) = m[(- 8) - (+ 10)] = - 18 m (Magnitude = 18m, NOT 2m!).

How to Secure Full Marks: Whenever a problem states "rebounds", "returns along original path", or "bounces back", immediately write a negative sign next to the final velocity!
Blunder 4: Neglecting SI Unit Conversions Before Substituting into Formulas Why Students Fall Into This Trap: Questions frequently give masses in grams (e.g., 50 g bullet), velocities in km/h (e.g., 72 km/h car), and distances in centimeters (e.g., 80 cm height). Students directly multiply these raw numbers, generating answers that are off by factors of 1000 or 100,000!

The Scientific Truth: Formulas like F = ma, p = mv, and J = F·Δt require strictly coherent SI units:
• Mass MUST be in kilograms (kg): 50 g = 0.05 kg; 7 tonnes = 7000 kg.
• Speed MUST be in m/s: 72 km/h × (5/18) = 20 m/s; 108 km/h × (5/18) = 30 m/s.
• Distance MUST be in metres (m): 80 cm = 0.8 m; 50 cm = 0.5 m.

How to Secure Full Marks: Dedicate Step 1 of your solution exclusively to "Given Data & SI Unit Conversions" before touching any equation.
Blunder 5: Believing a Heavier Body Naturally Stops Faster on a Frictionless Track Why Students Fall Into This Trap: Students rely on flawed daily intuition, thinking a heavy truck has more "trouble moving" and will halt sooner than a light bicycle.

The Scientific Truth: On a frictionless horizontal surface, an object requires zero force to maintain its motion! Once set into motion, both a 100-tonne railway locomotive and a 1-gram ping pong ball will coast at constant velocity FOREVER without slowing down at all (Newton's First Law).

How to Secure Full Marks: Remember Galileo's fundamental truth: Friction is the ONLY reason everyday objects stop! Without friction, motion is eternal.

6.2 Fifteen High-Yield Golden Revision Points

Memorize these 15 bulleted core takeaways for flawless last-minute exam recall:

1
Definition of Force: Force is an external push or pull resulting from interaction between two bodies that changes or tends to change state of rest, velocity, or shape.
2
Balanced vs Unbalanced: Balanced forces have ΣF = 0 (produce zero acceleration, can deform). Unbalanced forces have ΣF ≠ 0 (sole cause of acceleration).
3
Aristotle's Fallacy Overthrown: Uniform motion does NOT require an external force to sustain it. Force is required solely to overcome friction or to change motion.
4
Galileo's Incline Planes: A ball rolling on a horizontal frictionless plane would continue rolling indefinitely at constant velocity because no force opposes it.
5
Newton's First Law: A body stays at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless compelled by an external unbalanced force. Also known as the Law of Inertia.
6
Three Types of Inertia: Inertia of Rest (carpet dust), Inertia of Motion (bus braking forward lurch), and Inertia of Direction (tangential sparkler flight).
7
Mass as Measure of Inertia: Mass is the quantitative measure of inertia. Greater mass directly implies greater inertia (resistance to acceleration).
8
Linear Momentum (p = mv): The quantity of motion possessed by a body. Vector quantity, directed along velocity. SI unit: kg·m/s.
9
Newton's Second Law: Rate of change of momentum is directly proportional to applied force: F = dp/dt = ma. Quantifies force and defines 1 Newton.
10
One Newton Defined: 1 N = 1 kg × 1 m/s². The force that accelerates a 1 kg mass at 1 m/s². In CGS units: 1 N = 10⁵ dynes.
11
Impulse (J = F·Δt = Δp): Extending collision time Δt slashes impact force F. Explains cricket catching, airbags, seatbelts, and high-jump sand pits.
12
Newton's Third Law: To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction (FAB = - FBA). Forces always act in pairs on two different bodies.
13
Unequal Accelerations from Equal Forces: Because a = F/m, equal action-reaction forces cause massive acceleration in light bodies (bullet) and tiny acceleration in heavy bodies (gun).
14
Conservation of Linear Momentum: In an isolated system (ΣFext = 0), mAuA + mBuB = mAvA + mBvB.
