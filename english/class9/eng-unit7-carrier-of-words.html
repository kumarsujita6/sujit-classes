Part A: Prose Feature

“Carrier of Words” — Khetaram and the Thar Desert Lifeline

Chapter Overview & Curricular Framework “Carrier of Words” is a vivid human-interest reportage feature published in the NCERT Class 9 English textbook Kaveri. Set against the blistering, inhospitable terrain of the Thar Desert in western Rajasthan, near the Indo-Pakistan international border, it documents the daily life of Khetaram, a dedicated Gramin Dak Sewak (GDS) serving at the Somarad Branch Post Office. The prose highlights the indispensable role of physical letters, money orders, and postal workers in maintaining human survival and dignity where modern digital technology ceases to exist.

1. The Geographic Setting: The Arid Sands of the Thar Desert

The narrative begins by establishing an immediate and striking contrast between the modern urban lifestyle and the harsh reality of India’s arid borderlands. In metropolitan cities, human communication is instantaneous: a tap on a glass smartphone screen transmits text, voice notes, and high-definition videos across oceans in milliseconds. However, as one travels westward into Rajasthan, passing the district of Barmer and moving towards the remote borderlands near Pakistan, the paved highways dissolve into gravel, and the gravel ultimately crumbles into soft, shifting sand.

This is the realm of the Thar Desert — an expansive wilderness of towering sand dunes known locally as tibbas. Here, natural elements dominate human existence:

  • Extreme Temperatures: Summer daytime temperatures frequently surge past 49.9°C to 50°C, turning the sand into a bed of scorching embers and generating blinding hot winds known as loo.
  • Chronic Drought and Famine (Akaal): Rainfall is scarce and erratic, averaging barely 100 to 200 mm annually. In most years, the land suffers from akaal (famine), where agricultural fields yield almost nothing.
  • The Digital Void: Towering sand dunes and extreme isolation prevent mobile cellular towers from functioning reliably. High-speed 4G/5G internet signals are virtually non-existent; modern smartphones become mere paperweights devoid of network coverage.
  • Scattered Human Settlements (Dhaanis): Human habitation does not exist in compact towns, but in tiny, isolated hamlets called dhaanis — clusters of three to ten mud-and-thatch huts separated by miles of barren sand tracks.

2. Profile of Khetaram: The Lone Postman of Somarad

At the center of this harsh ecosystem stands Khetaram. For over fifteen years, he has served as the sole postman of the Somarad Branch Post Office. His physical appearance carries the indelible signature of his trade: his left shoulder is visibly slumped and drooped. This permanent postural deformity is the result of carrying, day after day, year after year, a massive leather mailbag laden with registered parcels, letters, government circulars, and money orders.

Official Dossier: Khetaram (GDS)
  • Designation: Gramin Dak Sewak (GDS) • Branch Postman
  • Station: Somarad Branch Post Office, Barmer District, Rajasthan
  • Length of Service: Over 15 years of uninterrupted desert delivery
  • Family Background: Sole breadwinner supporting a family of five dependents
  • Economic Reality: His agricultural land yields barely one crop of bajra (pearl millet) during favorable monsoons; postal remuneration is the sole buffer against starvation.
  • Footwear & Travel: Traditional leather mojris or bare feet across shifting dunes; covering 10 to 15 kilometers daily on foot.

3. More Than a Courier: The Postman as Scribe, Reader, and Confidant

Why does the community revere Khetaram as more than a simple delivery agent? The answer lies in the demographic reality of rural Rajasthan. A substantial proportion of the elderly villagers and women residing in these border dhaanis are non-literate. When an envelope arrives from a distant city — sent by a young son working as a daily-wage laborer in Surat, an army jawan guarding the borders of Kashmir, or a daughter married into a distant village — the recipient cannot read the ink on the paper.

In this delicate moment, Khetaram steps into a profound sociological role:

  1. The Patient Reader: Khetaram sits under the shade of a thatched veranda or a khejri tree, carefully unseals the letter, and reads every word aloud in the local Rajasthani Marwari dialect. He modulates his voice to match the emotional weight of the sender’s words.
  2. The Trusted Scribe: Illiterate mothers and fathers dictate their heartfelt responses to him. Khetaram dips his pen and translates their spoken joys, maternal prayers, and anxieties into written sentences, sealing the envelope and affixing the postage stamp.
  3. The Custodian of Family Secrets: Through his hands pass intimate confessions, domestic disputes, financial distress, and private pledges. Yet, Khetaram upholds an unwritten code of absolute confidentiality; the village trusts him implicitly.
  4. The Financial Conduit (Money Orders): In a region plagued by akaal, survival depends entirely on money remittances sent home by migrant sons. When Khetaram counts out crisp currency notes from a postal money order, he literally delivers food, medicine, and kerosene to impoverished households.

4. The Cultural Semiotics: Jaggery (Gur) vs. The Torn Corner

In traditional rural Indian postal culture, the envelope is not an inanimate piece of stationery; it is an active cultural artifact encoded with visible symbols:

  • The Offering of Jaggery (Gur): When a letter brings auspicious news — such as the birth of a newborn grandson, the finalization of a wedding match, or a military promotion — the ecstatic family immediately celebrates by offering Khetaram a piece of freshly broken gur (unrefined jaggery). In desert folklore, sweetening the bearer’s mouth honors the divine delivery of happiness.
  • The Tragic Torn Corner: Conversely, before modern telephones existed, how did a sender warn an illiterate recipient that a letter contained news of death without causing an abrupt shock? By ancient custom, the sender would deliberately tear off a small diagonal corner of the envelope. When Khetaram saw a torn-corner envelope in his mailbag, his heart grew heavy. He knew he had to approach that dhaani with quiet gravity, gather the family elders, and break the news with gentle, comforting empathy.

5. Macro-Perspective: The Historical Evolution of India Post

The chapter broadens its lens from Khetaram’s individual footprints to the monumental institutional history of India Post. Today, India boasts the largest postal network on Earth, encompassing over 155,000 post offices, more than 90% of which are located in rural villages. However, this democratic public utility did not emerge overnight; it evolved across three dramatic historical epochs:

Figure 1.4: Evolution of India Post — From Colonial Exploitation Machine to Universal Democratic Lifeline.

Originally founded under the British East India Company by Warren Hastings in 1774, postal routes were engineered exclusively for imperial administrative decrees, military troop movements, and revenue extraction. The common Indian peasant had neither access nor the financial means to use it. The revolutionary Post Office Act of 1854 under Lord Dalhousie introduced uniform low postage rates (half-anna letters) and the country’s first adhesive stamps, democratizing mail for the public. Following independence in 1947, the national government transformed India Post into a sacred democratic instrument: ensuring that every citizen, no matter how impoverished or isolated in Siachen, the Andaman Islands, or the Thar Desert, possesses an equal constitutional right to communicate.

CBSE Exam Focus: The Modern Transformation Board examiners frequently ask: “How does the evolution of Gramin Dak Sewaks into Gramin Sanchar Sewaks redefine rural public service?”
Topper Answer Point: With the integration of India Post Payments Bank (IPPB) and handheld biometric micro-ATMs, GDS workers like Khetaram now deliver DBT pensions, subsidized insurance, and cash withdrawals directly at the villager’s doorstep, transforming from traditional letter carriers into financial inclusion warriors.
Part B: Companion Poem

“Words” — Charles Swain (1801–1874)

Poet Profile & Victorian Context Charles Swain (1801–1874) was an esteemed English poet, engraver, and lyricist from Manchester, often hailed as the “Manchester Poet”. Living through the upheaval of the Industrial Revolution, Swain’s poetry stood out for its emotional warmth, moral clarity, and defense of human sincerity over material artificiality. In “Words”, Swain crafts a philosophical critique of hollow rhetoric, demonstrating that while insincere language evaporates into empty air, a few heartfelt words possess eternal spiritual power.

Complete Stanza-by-Stanza Literary Deconstruction

The poem consists of six quatrains (24 lines) written in Common Metre / Ballad Metre (alternating iambic tetrameter and iambic trimeter) with an impeccable alternating ABAB rhyme scheme. Let us examine each stanza line-by-line:

Stanza 1: The Transience of Empty Speech
If words could satisfy the heart,
  The heart might find less care;
But words, like summer birds, depart,
  And leave but empty air.

Line-by-Line Meaning & Analysis:

  • Lines 1–2: The speaker opens with a hypothetical conditional clause: “If words could satisfy the heart...”. If mere spoken phrases had the inherent power to soothe human sorrow, the human heart would be free from anxiety and care. But human grief is too deep for superficial verbiage.
  • Lines 3–4: The poet introduces a luminous simile: “words, like summer birds, depart”. Just as migratory birds flock during warm summer days but instantly abandon the land at the first frost of autumn, empty promises and flattering words vanish the instant hardship strikes, leaving behind nothing but “empty air” — a void of disappointment.
Stanza 2: The Pilgrim Heart & Worthless Weeds
The heart, a pilgrim upon earth,
  Finds often, when it needs,
That words are of as little worth
  As just so many weeds.

Line-by-Line Meaning & Analysis:

  • Lines 5–6: Swain employs an evocative metaphor: “The heart, a pilgrim upon earth”. A pilgrim is a solitary, weary traveler on a sacred quest, enduring dust, thirst, and fatigue. When this vulnerable pilgrim heart faces a genuine emotional or physical crisis (“when it needs”), it searches for sustenance.
  • Lines 7–8: Instead of bread or water, it is handed empty words, which are compared via simile to “just so many weeds”. Weeds are invasive, wild, and utterly devoid of nutritional value; they choke life instead of nourishing it. Empty declarations are useless clutter in moments of genuine suffering.
Stanza 3: The Potency of Sincere Brevity (Head vs. Heart)
A little said,—and truly said,—
  Can deeper joy impart,
Than hosts of words, which reach the head,
  But never touch the heart.

Line-by-Line Meaning & Analysis:

  • Lines 9–10: The core philosophical turning point: “A little said,—and truly said...”. Swain emphasizes that the value of communication lies in quality and sincerity, not volume. A brief sentence of genuine empathy imparts boundless, profound joy.
  • Lines 11–12: Swain builds a masterclass in antithesis between the head (intellect, logic, superficial flattery) and the heart (the seat of authentic human feeling). A speaker can unleash “hosts of words” (a massive army of rhetoric) that impress the intellectual mind, but they remain hollow because they fail to touch the core of human emotion.
Stanza 4: The Sunny Voice in a Lonely Home
The voice that wins its sunny way,
  A lonely home to cheer,
Hath oft the fewest words to say;
  But, oh! those few,—how dear!

Line-by-Line Meaning & Analysis:

  • Lines 13–14: The poet introduces radiant metaphor and personification: “The voice that wins its sunny way / A lonely home to cheer”. Just as a beam of golden sunlight breaks through the cold shadows of a dark, abandoned room, an empathetic voice illuminates a grieving, desolate household.
  • Lines 15–16: Paradoxically, the person who brings true comfort speaks the fewest words. They do not deliver long speeches; they offer a quiet word, a gentle reassurance, a tender touch. Swain ends with an emotional exclamation: “But, oh! those few,—how dear!”, underscoring that authentic kindness is beyond monetary price.
Stanza 5: The Test of Reality
If words could satisfy the breast,
  The world might hold a feast;
But words,—when summoned to the test,—
  Oft satisfy the least!

Line-by-Line Meaning & Analysis:

  • Lines 17–18: Here, “breast” is used poetically as a synecdoche for the soul or inner consciousness. If empty promises were edible nourishment, the entire world could celebrate an unending, lavish feast without ever working.
  • Lines 19–20: But reality is relentless. When crisis arrives and words are “summoned to the test” — when someone requires actual shelter, medical care, sacrifice, or loyalty — fair-weather words collapse and satisfy the least. True love requires deeds, not mere vocabulary.
Stanza 6: The Horticultural Climax (Blossom vs. Fruit)
Like plants that make a gaudy show,
  All blossom to the root;
But whose poor nature cannot grow
  One particle of fruit!

Line-by-Line Meaning & Analysis:

  • Lines 21–22: Swain closes with an unforgettable extended botanical simile. Insincere, loquacious people are compared to ostentatious ornamental plants that put on a “gaudy show” — smothering themselves in dazzling, decorative blossoms from branch down to root.
  • Lines 23–24: Despite this extravagant exterior display, their fundamental nature is impoverished: they cannot produce “one particle of fruit”. Flowers represent decorative rhetoric, whereas fruit represents tangible, nourishing, life-saving action. A person who speaks loudly but never helps in need is biologically sterile and morally bankrupt.

Part D: Grammar Masterclass

Active and Passive Voice in Postal & Official Contexts

Grammar Focus: Voice Transformation Matrix In NCERT Unit 7 Kaveri, grammar instruction centers on Active and Passive Voice. Understanding voice transformation is not just a board exam requirement; it is the structural cornerstone of official reports, administrative notices, and technical documentation. In postal work, the focus shifts naturally from who is doing the action to what has been delivered, registered, or dispatched.

1. Core Principles: Active vs. Passive Voice

  • Active Voice: The grammatical subject performs the action denoted by the verb.
    Formula: Subject (Doer) + Verb + Object (Receiver)
    Example: The Gramin Dak Sewak (Subject) delivers (Verb) the money orders (Object).
  • Passive Voice: The grammatical subject receives the action of the verb. The original object is promoted to the subject position.
    Formula: Object becomes Subject + Appropriate form of ‘to be’ + Past Participle (V3) + [by + Agent]
    Example: The money orders (New Subject) are delivered (be + V3) by the Gramin Dak Sewak (Agent).

  • 4. Supreme Emotional Intelligence:
    Khetaram adapts his spirit to the needs of his people: he smiles and accepts jaggery when delivering joyful birth notices, yet when carrying an envelope with a torn corner, he summons village elders and breaks tragic news of death with gentle, tearful consolation.

Conclusion: Khetaram is the unsung backbone of rural India, proving that true heroism lies not in battlefield glory, but in steadfast, selfless daily service to humanity.

Q19. (5 Marks) Imagine you are Khetaram’s apprentice accompanying him on his postal route for the first time. Write a detailed account of your observations and emotional awakening.
CBSE 5-Mark Evaluation Rubric First-Person Persona & Sensory Atmosphere: 2 Marks • Reflective Realization & Thematic Depth: 2 Marks • Format & Fluency: 1 Mark
Exemplar Topper Answer (5 Marks)

A Day in the Desert Sands: My Journey with Uncle Khetaram

Until today, I naively believed that working as a postman was a simple, mechanical task of dropping envelopes into metal letterboxes. Walking twelve kilometers beside my uncle Khetaram across the blinding, furnace-like sand dunes of Somarad shattered every illusion I held.

By nine in the morning, the desert sun was blazing like molten brass. The loose sand gave way under our leather mojris; for every two steps forward, our feet slipped one step backward. I watched Uncle Khetaram’s left shoulder — noticeably curved and slumped under the weight of the thick leather bag. When I offered to carry it, he smiled gently and warned that the strap requires years of calloused habit to balance across burning sand. Our mobile phones had lost all network signals within twenty minutes of leaving the branch office; we were walking into an ancient, silent world.

My true emotional awakening, however, occurred not on the dunes, but inside the mud courtyards of the isolated dhaanis. At our first stop, Uncle Khetaram sat cross-legged under an acacia tree, unfolding a registered letter for an elderly woman whose cataract-veiled eyes filled with tears. As he read her soldier son’s words aloud in warm Marwari, I realized Uncle Khetaram was not delivering paper — he was delivering life itself. When the mother pressed a sticky piece of gur into his weathered hands, I understood that this sweetness was sacred.

Hours later, when he quietly separated a letter with a torn corner and gathered the village elders to break news of bereavement, I saw him weep alongside the family. That afternoon, I learned what textbook lessons could never teach: true public service is not measured by algorithms or speed, but by the weight of human empathy. I walked home through the cooling twilight feeling immense pride in my uncle’s noble footprints.

Q20. (5 Marks) In an age dominated by artificial intelligence, automated messaging, and instant digital feeds, evaluate why the human element in communication remains indispensable.
CBSE 5-Mark Evaluation Rubric Critical Evaluation of Digital Culture: 2 Marks • Integration of Swain’s Philosophy & Khetaram’s Service: 2 Marks • Stylistic Coherence & Conclusion: 1 Mark
Exemplar Topper Answer (5 Marks)

The 21st century has achieved the pinnacle of technical velocity. Artificial intelligence can generate thousands of eloquent paragraphs in seconds, and messaging applications transmit billions of instant notifications worldwide. Yet, contemporary society is simultaneously plagued by unprecedented emotional alienation, loneliness, and psychological distress. Evaluating this modern paradox through the twin lenses of Charles Swain’s poetry and Khetaram’s desert odyssey reveals why the human element in communication can never be replaced by machines.

1. The Illusion of Algorithmic Abundance:

Swain’s poem “Words” anticipates our digital dilemma with uncanny precision. Algorithms generate “hosts of words, which reach the head, / But never touch the heart”. Automated chatbot responses, predictive texting, and generic social media comments are modern iterations of the plant that makes a “gaudy show, / All blossom to the root”. They look impressive on digital screens, yet when human souls are “summoned to the test” of genuine tragedy, clinical depression, or bereavement, automated text satisfies the least. It is like feeding desert weeds to an earthly pilgrim starving for love.

2. The Irreplaceable Value of Physical Presence and Sincerity:

No digital code or automated drone can replace Khetaram sitting on a rope cot beside an unlettered, grieving mother in a desert dhaani. Communication requires sensory presence: the tone of empathy, the solemnity of silence, the gentle modulation of a voice that “wins its sunny way / A lonely home to cheer”, and the physical integrity of counting out money order notes to save a family from akaal.

Conclusion:

Technology provides the pipes, but humanity provides the water. Communication is an ethical act of communion. As NEP 2020 emphasizes, genuine education must foster compassion, active listening, and the moral courage to speak “a little said,—and truly said”, ensuring that technological speed never eclipses the human soul.

Case Study 1 • Prose Extract

Extract from “Carrier of Words” (NCERT Kaveri)

“Beyond the last railhead where the tarmac dissolves into wind-blown sand, modern telecommunication signals disappear entirely. Here, in the shadow of shifting dunes near the border perimeter, stands Khetaram. His left shoulder bears the permanent slump of fifteen years carrying the heavy leather mailbag. In years of akaal, his arrival at a scattered dhaani is nothing short of miraculous. When he counts out the crisp currency of a money order sent by a son working in a distant textile mill, he delivers more than cash — he delivers flour, medicine, and survival. As the grateful mother presses a small lump of gur into his weathered palm, the desert heat seems to soften.”
Question 1.1: Why does the author describe Khetaram’s arrival during ‘akaal’ as ‘nothing short of miraculous’? (1 Mark)

Answer: During years of akaal (drought and famine), crops fail completely and desert families face acute starvation. The money order Khetaram delivers provides the essential purchasing power to buy food grains and medicine, literally preserving their physical survival.

Question 1.2: What is the physical consequence of fifteen years of service visible on Khetaram’s body? (1 Mark)

Answer: His left shoulder is visibly and permanently slumped and drooped due to the daily physical strain of carrying a heavy leather mailbag loaded with mail, parcels, and delivery registers across miles of soft sand.

Question 1.3: Explain the cultural significance of the mother pressing ‘a small lump of gur’ into his palm. (1 Mark)

Answer: Offering gur (jaggery) is a traditional Indian custom of ‘sweetening the mouth’ to celebrate auspicious tidings (shubh samachar) and express deep communal gratitude to the bearer of good news.

Question 1.4: Transform this extract sentence into Passive Voice: “Khetaram counts out the crisp currency of a money order.” (1 Mark)

Answer: “The crisp currency of a money order is counted out by Khetaram.”

Case Study 2 • Poetry Extract

Extract from “Words” by Charles Swain

“A little said,—and truly said,—
Can deeper joy impart,
Than hosts of words, which reach the head,
But never touch the heart.

Like plants that make a gaudy show,
All blossom to the root;
But whose poor nature cannot grow
One particle of fruit!”
Question 2.1: Identify the rhyme scheme and poetic meter of the extract stanzas. (1 Mark)

Answer: The rhyme scheme is ABAB (said/impart/head/heart and show/root/grow/fruit), written in Common Metre / Ballad Metre alternating iambic tetrameter (8) and iambic trimeter (6).

Question 2.2: What figure of speech is employed in ‘hosts of words, which reach the head’? Explain its effect. (1 Mark)

Answer: It employs Antithesis (contrasting the intellectual ‘head’ with the emotional ‘heart’) and Metaphor (comparing words to a massive army or ‘host’), illustrating that eloquent rhetoric often fails to stimulate authentic empathy.

Question 2.3: In the botanical simile of the second stanza, what do ‘blossom’ and ‘fruit’ respectively represent? (1 Mark)

Answer: ‘Blossom’ represents attractive, decorative, and boastful verbal speech, whereas ‘Fruit’ represents tangible, practical, and life-saving sacrificial action.

Question 2.4: Provide one synonym from the extract for ‘multitude or army’. (1 Mark)

Answer: Hosts (from “hosts of words”).

When this pilgrim heart experiences deep distress, it needs real spiritual bread and active help; offering it hollow flattery is as worthless as handing it desert weeds.

5 Common Student Blunders & Examiner Traps

Trap 1: The Romanticizing Delusion (Why Khetaram Took the Job)

The Blunder: Students often write that Khetaram became a postman because “he loved desert adventure, loved walking in nature, or wanted to explore sand dunes”.

The Topper Fix: State clearly that his motivation was stark economic survival. Arid farming in the Thar Desert yields barely one crop of bajra and frequently fails due to akaal (drought); his postal stipend feeds his family of five.

Trap 2: The Damaged Stationery Myth (The Torn Corner)

The Blunder: Claiming that the envelope corner was “torn accidentally by rough handling, sandstorms, or postal sorting machines”.

The Topper Fix: The corner was intentionally torn by the sender before dispatching as an established cultural non-verbal code indicating death or tragedy (bereavement).

Trap 3: The Passive Voice Auxiliary Mismatch

The Blunder: Changing tenses during voice transformation (e.g., converting “Khetaram delivers letters” into *“Letters were delivered by Khetaram”*).

The Topper Fix: Tense never changes in passive voice! Simple Present Active (delivers) converts to Simple Present Passive (is/are + V3 → are delivered).

Trap 4: The Literal Botanical Fallacy in Swain’s Poem

The Blunder: Writing that Swain is a botanist warning farmers about bad weeds and poor fruit crops.

The Topper Fix: Weeds, blossoms, and fruits are metaphorical symbols. ‘Weeds’ signify empty, useless words; ‘blossom’ signifies superficial flattery; ‘fruit’ signifies practical, compassionate deeds.

Trap 5: The ‘Courier Only’ Oversimplification

The Blunder: Describing Khetaram’s job as simply “dropping envelopes into mailboxes”.

The Topper Fix: Emphasize that Khetaram is a reader, scribe, confidant, and financial conduit. He unseals letters for illiterate villagers, reads them aloud, scribes replies, and disburses critical money order cash.
