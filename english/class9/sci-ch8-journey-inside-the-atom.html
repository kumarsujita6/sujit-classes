The Core Scientific Narrative of Chapter 8 For over two millennia, humans believed that matter was built of hard, indivisible billiard balls called atoms (from Greek atomos, meaning uncuttable). However, late 19th-century electrical experiments shocked the scientific world: matter could become electrically charged! If rubbing a plastic comb through dry hair can attract bits of paper, matter must contain smaller, charged pieces. This chapter is a grand scientific detective story showing how J.J. Thomson, Eugen Goldstein, Ernest Rutherford, Niels Bohr, and James Chadwick unlocked the mysterious inner chambers of the atom.

Comprehensive Mind Map: Structure of the Chapter

This master syllabus blueprint breaks Chapter 8 into five interconnected intellectual pillars:

1. Charged Particles & Subatomic Discovery
  • Static Electricity: Friction transfers electrons (amber, glass, plastic comb).
  • Canal Rays (E. Goldstein, 1886): Positively charged anode rays led to the discovery of the Proton (p⁺, +1 charge, mass ≈ 1 u).
  • Cathode Rays (J.J. Thomson, 1897): High-voltage gas discharge revealed subatomic negatively charged streams: the Electron (e⁻, -1 charge, mass ≈ 1/2000 u).
  • Neutron (James Chadwick, 1932): Bombardment of Beryllium by α-particles revealed neutral particle (n⁰, 0 charge, mass ≈ 1 u).
2. Rutherford's Scattering & The Nucleus
  • Gold Foil Experiment: Fast α-particles (He²⁺, mass 4 u) fired at ~100 nm (1000 atoms thick) gold foil.
  • 3 Startling Observations: Most pass straight (99.9%); small fraction deflected by small angles; 1 in 12,000 bounced back (180°).
  • Rutherford's Conclusions: Most of the atom is empty space; positive charge & nearly all mass is concentrated in a tiny central nucleus (r ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m).
  • Critical Flaw: Accelerated revolving electrons must radiate electromagnetic energy, spiral inward, and crash into the nucleus within 10⁻⁸ s.
3. Bohr's Quantized Planetary Model
  • Postulate 1: Electrons revolve only in certain special non-radiating orbits called discrete orbits or stationary states.
  • Postulate 2: While revolving in these discrete orbits, electrons do NOT lose or radiate energy.
  • Energy Shells: Designated by letters K, L, M, N... or quantum numbers n = 1, 2, 3, 4...
  • Energy Quantization: Electrons only jump when absorbing or emitting exact quantum energy packets (ΔE = hν).
4. Bohr-Bury Scheme & Valency
  • Rule 1 (2n²): Maximum capacity per shell: K(n=1)=2, L(n=2)=8, M(n=3)=18, N(n=4)=32.
  • Rule 2 (Octet Limit): Outermost valence shell cannot hold more than 8 electrons (except K which holds max 2).
  • Rule 3 (Stepwise Filling): Inner shells must be completely filled before outer shells begin filling.
  • Valence Electrons & Valency: Combining capacity. If valence e⁻ ≤ 4, Valency = valence e⁻. If valence e⁻ > 4, Valency = 8 - valence e⁻.
5. Nuclear Species: Isotopes & Isobars
  • Atomic Number (Z): Total number of protons in the nucleus (defines the element's chemical identity).
  • Mass Number (A): Total nucleons (Protons + Neutrons). A = Z + N.
  • Isotopes: Same atomic number Z, different mass number A (e.g., ¹H, ²H, ³H; ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl). Same chemical properties, different physical weights.
  • Isobars: Same mass number A, different atomic number Z (e.g., ⁴⁰₁₈Ar and ⁴⁰₂₀Ca). Different chemical properties!
  • Real-World Applications: U-235 (nuclear fuel), Co-60 (cancer therapy), I-131 (goitre treatment), C-14 (carbon dating).

2.1 The Dawn of Atomic Thought: From Philosophy to Science

Human beings have always wondered: If you take a piece of gold or a block of wood and keep cutting it into smaller and smaller slices, will you eventually reach a tiny piece that cannot be cut any further?

1
Acharya Kanada (c. 600 BCE, India): In his philosophical system known as Vaisheshika Darshana, Maharishi Kanada proposed that if we divide matter (Padartha or Dravya) continuously, we reach ultimate, indivisible, eternal particles beyond which further division is impossible. He named these smallest particles Parmanu (ପରମାଣୁ - from Parama = ultimate, and Anu = small particle).
2
Pakudha Katyayana (Ancient India): Elaborated that these ultimate particles do not normally exist in isolation; instead, they combine in various proportions and groupings to yield the vast diversity of substances found in nature.
3
Democritus & Leucippus (c. 400 BCE, Greece): Suggested that if we divide matter into finer and finer pieces, a stage is reached where particles cannot be divided further. Democritus called these indivisible units atomos (Greek for "uncuttable" or "indivisible").
Science vs. Philosophy: What Was Missing? Ancient Indian and Greek ideas were brilliant philosophical intuitions, but they lacked experimental verification. For nearly 2,200 years, no scientific experiments were conducted to test whether atoms truly existed or what they were made of.

John Dalton’s Atomic Theory (1808)

In 1808, British schoolteacher and chemist John Dalton put forward the first modern scientific Atomic Theory based on quantitative experimental laws of chemical combination (the Law of Conservation of Mass and the Law of Definite Proportions):

Dalton's Postulate (1808) Scientific Meaning Modern Status (Post-1900 Discovery)
1. Ultimate Matter All matter consists of indivisible, indestructible particles called atoms. Disproved Atoms are divisible into subatomic electrons, protons, and neutrons.
2. Indestructibility Atoms cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction (Conservation of Mass). Valid in Chemistry Still holds true in ordinary chemical reactions (modified in nuclear reactions: E = mc²).
3. Identity of Same Element All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties. Modified Disproved by Isotopes (e.g., Carbon-12 and Carbon-14 have different masses).
4. Difference of Elements Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties. Modified Disproved by Isobars (e.g., Argon-40 and Calcium-40 have the exact same mass number).
5. Whole Number Ratios Atoms combine in simple, whole-number ratios to form chemical compounds. Valid Forms the bedrock of stoichiometry (e.g., H₂O is strictly 2:1 ratio).
6. Relative Number The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound. Valid Basis of the Law of Constant Proportions.

2.2 Charged Particles in Matter: The Fall of the Indivisible Atom

Dalton believed the atom was a solid, impenetrable billiard ball. But a simple tabletop observation completely shattered this assumption:

Everyday Electrostatic Evidence (In-Text Activity 8.1)
  • Action A: Comb your clean, dry hair briskly with a plastic comb, then bring it near tiny pieces of paper. Observation: The paper bits jump up and cling to the comb!
  • Action B: Rub an inflated balloon with a woolen cloth or silk, and touch it against a dry wall. Observation: The balloon sticks to the wall as if glued!
  • Scientific Deduction: Neutral objects become electrically charged upon friction. Where do these electric charges come from? They cannot come from empty space—they must originate from inside the atoms of the materials! Therefore, atoms are not solid indivisible spheres; they contain internal electrical constituents!

The Discovery of the Subatomic Particles

Between 1886 and 1932, three foundational subatomic particles were isolated through brilliant experimental physics:

Figure 8.2: The Gas Discharge Tube Experiment showing Cathode Rays (J.J. Thomson, 1897) streaming toward the anode, and Canal / Anode Rays (E. Goldstein, 1886) passing through cathode perforations.

The Electron (e⁻)

Discoverer: J.J. Thomson (1897)

Discovery Method: Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) using high voltage under low gas pressure.

Charge: Relative charge of -1 (Absolute: -1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ Coulombs).

Mass: Extremely negligible! Approximately 1/1840 (or ~1/2000) of the mass of a hydrogen atom (Absolute: 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg).

Key Fact: The charge-to-mass ratio (e/m) of cathode rays is universal—it is completely independent of the gas inside the tube or the metal of the cathode.

The Proton (p⁺)

Discoverer: Eugen Goldstein (1886)

Discovery Method: Canal Rays (streams of positive ions travelling in a direction opposite to cathode rays in a perforated cathode tube).

Charge: Relative charge of +1 (Absolute: +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ Coulombs).

Mass: Taken as 1 unified mass unit (1 u) (Absolute: 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg). It is nearly 1840 times heavier than an electron!

Key Fact: For hydrogen gas, the canal ray particles are bare hydrogen nuclei (protons).

The Neutron (n⁰)

Discoverer: James Chadwick (1932)

Discovery Method: Bombarding a thin sheet of Beryllium with high-energy alpha particles.

Charge: Zero (Neutral). Carries no electric charge whatsoever.

Mass: Approximately 1 unified mass unit (1 u) (Absolute: 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg), almost identical to the proton mass.

Key Fact: Present inside the nucleus of all atoms except ordinary hydrogen (Protium, ¹₁H), which has 0 neutrons!

2.3 J.J. Thomson’s Plum Pudding Model (1904)

Once electrons were discovered, a major puzzle arose: Since matter is normally neutral, how are negative electrons and positive charges arranged inside an atom?

J.J. Thomson proposed the first formal atomic architecture, often called the Plum Pudding Model or Watermelon Model:

The Two Core Postulates of Thomson’s Model
  1. Positively Charged Sphere: An atom consists of a uniformly distributed sphere of positive electrical charge.
  2. Embedded Electrons: The negatively charged electrons are embedded randomly within this positive sphere, exactly like raisins (plums) in a British Christmas pudding, or black seeds embedded in the red pulp of a sweet watermelon!
  3. Electrical Neutrality: The total magnitude of negative charge (electrons) is equal to the total positive charge of the sphere. Therefore, the atom as a whole is electrically neutral.
Why Thomson's Model Had to Be Discarded Although Thomson brilliantly explained why atoms are electrically neutral, his model had a fatal flaw: it treated positive charge as a diffuse, jelly-like cloud spread across the entire atom. It could not explain why fast-moving alpha particles in Rutherford's experiment experienced sudden, powerful deflections. If Thomson's model were correct, heavy alpha particles would pass right through the diffuse positive jelly with virtually zero deflection!

2.4 Rutherford's Alpha Particle Scattering Experiment (1911)

Ernest Rutherford, a former student of J.J. Thomson, designed one of the most famous experiments in the history of science to probe the interior of the atom.

Experimental Setup & Components:

  • Alpha (α) Particles: Fast-moving, energetic helium nuclei carrying a charge of +2 (doubly-charged helium ions, He²⁺) with a mass of 4 u. Because they are heavy and fast, they carry significant kinetic energy.
  • Ultra-Thin Gold Foil: Rutherford chose gold because it is the most malleable of all metals. The foil was hammered down to a thickness of only about 1000 atoms (approximately 100 nm thick).
  • Circular Fluorescent Screen: A zinc sulfide (ZnS) screen surrounded the foil. Whenever an α-particle struck the screen, a microscopic flash of light (scintillation) was produced, allowing researchers to measure deflection angles.

Figure 8.3: Rutherford's Alpha Particle Scattering Experiment. Most α-particles travel completely undeflected through empty atomic space; only the rare particle executing a head-on trajectory rebounds from the dense, positively charged nucleus.

The 3 Crucial Observations & Their Direct Scientific Deductions

Experimental Observation Scientific Deduction / Conclusion Real-World Analogous Thinking
1. Most α-particles passed straight through the gold foil without any deflection (over 99.9%). Most of the space inside the atom is completely empty! There are no positive charges or heavy obstacles filling the body of the atom. Walking through an empty soccer stadium with zero obstacles in your path.
2. Very few particles were deflected by small and moderate angles from their straight path. The positive charge of the atom is not spread throughout the atom (as Thomson claimed), but occupies a very small volume. The deflection is caused by intense Coulomb electrostatic repulsion between the positive α-particle (He²⁺) and the positive center. A ball brushing lightly against the post of a goal, glancing away slightly.
3. A surprisingly tiny fraction (about 1 in 12,000) rebounded almost completely (deflected by ~180°). All the positive charge and nearly all the mass of the atom is concentrated in an unimaginably tiny, dense central region called the nucleus. Rutherford remarked: "It was as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch artillery shell at a sheet of tissue paper and it came back and hit you!"
Scale Comparison: The Stadium vs. The Cricket Ball The radius of an atom is approximately 10⁻¹⁰ meters (0.1 nanometer), while the radius of the atomic nucleus is approximately 10⁻¹⁵ meters (1 femtometer). The atom is 100,000 times larger than its nucleus! If an atom were expanded to the size of a giant international cricket stadium (radius ~100 meters), the nucleus at the pitch would be the size of a tiny green pea or a small glass marble at the very center!

Postulates of Rutherford’s Nuclear Model of the Atom

  1. Central Positive Nucleus: There is a positively charged, extremely dense center in an atom called the nucleus. Nearly all the mass of an atom resides inside this nucleus.
  2. Planetary Electrons: The negatively charged electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular paths called orbits, much like planets orbiting around the Sun.
  3. Nuclear Dimensions: The size of the nucleus is extremely small compared to the overall size of the atom.

The Fatal Drawback of Rutherford’s Model: The Collapse Dilemma

While Rutherford revolutionized physics by discovering the nucleus, his planetary model faced an insurmountable theoretical contradiction under Classical Electrodynamics (Maxwell's Theory of Electromagnetism):

Why Rutherford's Atom Should Self-Destruct in 10⁻⁸ Seconds:
  • Circular Motion is Accelerated: Any object moving in a circular path constantly changes direction. Therefore, even at constant speed, it undergoes continuous acceleration.
  • Accelerating Charges Must Radiate Energy: According to Maxwell's laws, an accelerating charged particle must continuously radiate electromagnetic energy into space.
  • The Death Spiral: As the revolving electron radiates energy, its orbital kinetic energy decreases. It must spiral inward closer and closer to the positive nucleus, finally crashing into the nucleus in less than 10⁻⁸ seconds (10 nanoseconds)!
  • The Paradox: If Rutherford were right, matter could not exist for more than a fraction of a second! Yet our chairs, rocks, planets, and bodies have remained perfectly stable for billions of years. Hence, Rutherford's model could not explain atomic stability.

2.5 Niels Bohr’s Model of the Atom (1913)

In 1913, Danish physicist Niels Bohr saved the atom from destruction by introducing revolutionary quantum concepts that defied classical Newtonian physics.

Niels Bohr’s Two Master Postulates
  1. Discrete Orbits (Stationary States): Only certain special circular orbits, known as discrete orbits (or non-radiating orbits), are permissible for electrons inside an atom. Electrons are forbidden from existing in between these quantized tracks.
  2. Non-Radiating Orbits: While revolving in these discrete orbits, electrons do NOT radiate energy! The electron's energy remains constant and strictly conserved as long as it stays in its assigned shell. Therefore, it never spirals inward and never crashes into the nucleus!

2.6 How Are Electrons Distributed in Shells? (The Bohr-Bury Rules)

The systematic arrangement of electrons into various energy shells of an atom is called its electronic configuration. This distribution is strictly governed by three fundamental rules formulated by Niels Bohr and Charles Bury:

R1
The 2n² Capacity Formula: The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in a given energy shell is given by the formula 2n², where n is the orbit number (energy level index):
  • K Shell (n = 1): 2(1)² = 2 × 1 = 2 electrons max
  • L Shell (n = 2): 2(2)² = 2 × 4 = 8 electrons max
  • M Shell (n = 3): 2(3)² = 2 × 9 = 18 electrons max
  • N Shell (n = 4): 2(4)² = 2 × 16 = 32 electrons max
R2
The Outermost Octet Limit: The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in the outermost (valence) shell is strictly 8, even if that shell has a theoretical capacity to hold more electrons (by the 2n² rule)! (Exception: If K is the only shell, its maximum is 2).

Example: In Potassium (Z = 19), the configuration is 2, 8, 8, 1, NOT 2, 8, 9!

R3
Stepwise Shell Filling: Electrons are not accommodated in a given outer shell unless the inner shells are completely filled. Shells are filled in a progressive, step-wise sequence based on increasing energy levels.

2.7 Valence Electrons and Valency (The Combining Power)

Why do chemical elements react with one another? Why does sodium combine with chlorine to form salt, while argon never reacts with anything?

Valence Electrons vs. Valency: The Fundamental Distinction
  • Valence Electrons: The electrons present in the outermost shell of an atom are called valence electrons. They dictate the chemical reactivity and bonding behaviour of the element.
  • Valency: The combining capacity of an atom of an element. It represents the number of electrons an atom must lose, gain, or share with other atoms in order to achieve a completely filled, stable outermost shell (an octet of 8 electrons, or a duplet of 2 electrons for the K shell).

The Exact Rules for Calculating Valency:

Case 1
When Valence Electrons are 1, 2, 3, or 4: It is energetically much easier for the atom to lose these few outermost electrons to expose the fully filled underlying shell.
Formula: Valency = Number of Valence Electrons
Examples:
• Sodium (Na: 2, 8, 1) → Loses 1 e⁻ → Valency = 1
• Magnesium (Mg: 2, 8, 2) → Loses 2 e⁻ → Valency = 2
• Aluminium (Al: 2, 8, 3) → Loses 3 e⁻ → Valency = 3
• Carbon / Silicon (Si: 2, 8, 4) → Shares 4 e⁻ → Valency = 4
Case 2
When Valence Electrons are 5, 6, 7, or 8: It is energetically impossible to lose 5 to 7 electrons due to enormous nuclear pull. Instead, the atom gains or shares electrons to complete its octet (8).
Formula: Valency = 8 - Number of Valence Electrons
Examples:
• Nitrogen / Phosphorus (P: 2, 8, 5) → Gains/shares 3 e⁻ → Valency = 8 - 5 = 3
• Oxygen / Sulphur (S: 2, 8, 6) → Gains/shares 2 e⁻ → Valency = 8 - 6 = 2
• Chlorine / Fluorine (Cl: 2, 8, 7) → Gains 1 e⁻ → Valency = 8 - 7 = 1
• Neon / Argon (Ne: 2, 8) → Outermost shell full! → Valency = 8 - 8 = 0

2.8 Atomic Number (Z) and Mass Number (A)

Every element in the universe is uniquely tagged by its internal subatomic count:

1. Atomic Number (Symbol: Z) The Atomic Number of an element is defined as the total number of protons present in the nucleus of its atom.
• Z = Number of Protons (p⁺)
• In a normal, electrically neutral atom: Number of Protons = Number of Electrons = Z.
• Crucial Note: The atomic number is the true chemical identity of an element. All atoms of hydrogen have Z = 1; all atoms of carbon have Z = 6; all atoms of gold have Z = 79.
2. Mass Number (Symbol: A) Electrons have virtually negligible mass (~1/1840 u). Therefore, the entire mass of an atom is concentrated in its nucleus, contributed by protons and neutrons. Together, protons and neutrons are called nucleons.
The Mass Number of an atom is defined as the total sum of protons and neutrons present in its nucleus.
• A = Number of Protons (Z) + Number of Neutrons (N)
• To find the number of neutrons in any atom: N = A - Z

Standard International Atomic Notation:

Scientists represent an atom with its mass number as a superscript (top left) and its atomic number as a subscript (bottom left):

AZX

Where X = Chemical Symbol of Element, A = Mass Number (Top), Z = Atomic Number (Bottom).

2.9 Isotopes and Isobars

Nature has several fascinating variations in nuclear composition:

1. Isotopes (Same Element, Different Masses)

Isotopes are defined as atoms of the same element having the same atomic number (Z), but different mass numbers (A). Because their atomic numbers are identical, isotopes contain the exact same number of protons and electrons, but differ in their number of neutrons.

Figure 8.6: The nuclear architecture of Hydrogen isotopes: Protium (0 neutrons), Deuterium (1 neutron), and Tritium (2 neutrons).

Why Do Isotopes Have Identical Chemical Properties? Chemical properties depend strictly on the number of valence electrons and their arrangement in electron shells. Since all isotopes of an element have the exact same atomic number (Z), they have the exact same number of electrons and identical electronic configurations! Therefore, Carbon-12 and Carbon-14 react identically with oxygen to form CO₂. However, because their masses differ, their physical properties (such as density, melting point, and boiling point) are slightly different.

Calculation of Fractional / Average Atomic Mass

In nature, most elements exist as a mixture of isotopes with fixed natural abundance percentages. Therefore, the atomic mass listed in the periodic table is a weighted average of the masses of its natural isotopes.

The Master Weighted Average Formula:
Average Atomic Mass = (Mass₁ × % Abundance₁) + (Mass₂ × % Abundance₂) / 100

Classic NCERT Case Study: Chlorine (Cl)
In nature, chlorine occurs in two isotopic forms:
• ³⁵₁₇Cl with mass 35 u (Natural abundance = 75% or 3:1 ratio)
• ³⁷₁₇Cl with mass 37 u (Natural abundance = 25% or 1:4 ratio)

Mathematical Calculation:
Average Atomic Mass of Cl = [35 × (75/100)] + [37 × (25/100)]
= [35 × (3/4)] + [37 × (1/4)]
= (105 / 4) + (37 / 4)
= 142 / 4 = 35.5 u
Exam Alert: This does not mean any single chlorine atom weighs 35.5 u! Every individual chlorine atom weighs either 35 u or 37 u. 35.5 u is the mathematical average weight of a random macroscopic sample.

High-Value Real-World Applications of Isotopes:

  • Nuclear Fuel: An isotope of Uranium (Uranium-235) is used as fuel in nuclear fission reactors to generate clean electricity.
  • Cancer Radiotherapy: An isotope of Cobalt (Cobalt-60) emits high-energy gamma rays and is widely used in the medical treatment of cancer (radiation oncology).
  • Thyroid / Goitre Treatment: An isotope of Iodine (Iodine-131) is used in the diagnostic imaging and therapeutic treatment of thyroid disease and goitre.
  • Radiocarbon Dating: The radioactive isotope Carbon-14 (C-14) is used by archaeologists and geologists to determine the exact age of ancient wood, fossils, and historical artifacts.
  • Leak Detection in Pipelines: An isotope of Sodium (Sodium-24) is used as an industrial tracer to locate subterranean leaks in oil and water distribution pipelines.

2. Isobars (Different Elements, Same Mass)

Isobars are defined as atoms of different chemical elements having the same mass number (A), but different atomic numbers (Z).

Classic NCERT Isobar Example: Argon and Calcium
• Argon (⁴⁰₁₈Ar): Atomic Number Z = 18 (18 protons, 22 neutrons). Mass Number A = 18 + 22 = 40.
• Calcium (⁴⁰₂₀Ca): Atomic Number Z = 20 (20 protons, 20 neutrons). Mass Number A = 20 + 20 = 40.
Key Scientific Fact: Isobars belong to completely different chemical elements. They have completely different electronic configurations (Ar: 2, 8, 8 vs. Ca: 2, 8, 8, 2). Therefore, their chemical properties are completely different!

Activity 8.1: Rubbing comb on dry hair & balloon on silk cloth
Step 1: Core Concept

Triboelectric Charging (Electrostatic Induction by Friction) proves that neutral matter contains internal subatomic electric charges that can be transferred between materials upon contact and friction.

Step 2: Experimental Observation

(a) When a clean plastic comb is vigorously run through dry hair, it attracts small bits of dry paper.
(b) When an inflated rubber balloon is rubbed with a silk or woolen cloth and placed against a wall, it adheres firmly to the wall.

Step 3: Scientific Deduction

During friction, loosely bound outermost electrons are transferred from hair to the plastic comb. The comb acquires a net negative electrostatic charge. When brought near paper bits, it polarizes the neutral molecules of paper by repelling their electrons and attracting the positive nuclei, causing the paper bits to fly upward. Similarly, rubbing transfers electrons to the balloon, allowing it to induce opposite charges on the wall surface.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Atoms are not indivisible solid spheres as Dalton claimed. They contain mobile, charged subatomic constituents (electrons) that can be moved from one atom to another.

Step 5: Topper's Exam Tip

If hair is wet or oily, friction does not transfer electrons effectively because water forms a conductive dissipation film. Dry hair is mandatory for electrostatics experiments.

Activity 8.2: The Barbed Wire Fence vs. Solid Wall Analogy
Step 1: Core Concept

Analogy modeling used by Rutherford to deduce empty atomic volume versus dense central nuclear mass.

Step 2: Experimental Setup

A blindfolded child throws stones at (a) a massive solid brick wall, and (b) a barbed-wire fence with large gaps between thin strands.

Step 3: Comparative Analysis

(a) At the solid wall, every thrown stone hits the wall and produces a sound because the obstacle is continuous across the entire area.
(b) At the barbed-wire fence, most stones pass right through the wide open gaps without striking any wire; only rarely does a stone hit a thin wire and bounce off.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Because over 99.9% of alpha particles passed undeflected through the gold foil, the atom resembles a barbed-wire fence with vast empty space, rather than a solid brick wall (Thomson's diffuse sphere).

Step 5: Topper's Exam Tip

Use this analogy in 3-mark conceptual questions to explain why Rutherford deduced that the atom is mostly empty space.

Activity 8.3: Calculating & Sketching Electron Distribution of First 20 Elements
Step 1: Core Concept

The Bohr-Bury Scheme: Maximum capacity 2n², outermost octet rule, and progressive inward-outward shell filling.

Step 2: Given Data

Atomic numbers Z = 1 to 20 (Hydrogen to Calcium).

Step 3: Mathematical Distribution

• K shell (n=1) fills first up to 2 e⁻ (H: 1; He: 2).
• L shell (n=2) fills next up to 8 e⁻ (Li: 2,1 to Ne: 2,8).
• M shell (n=3) begins filling (Na: 2,8,1 to Ar: 2,8,8).
• Even though M shell can theoretically hold 18 (2×3²), rule 2 dictates that the outermost shell cannot exceed 8. Therefore, for Potassium (Z=19) and Calcium (Z=20), electrons begin populating the N shell: K: 2,8,8,1 and Ca: 2,8,8,2.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

The electronic distribution follows: H(1), He(2), Li(2,1), Be(2,2), B(2,3), C(2,4), N(2,5), O(2,6), F(2,7), Ne(2,8), Na(2,8,1), Mg(2,8,2), Al(2,8,3), Si(2,8,4), P(2,8,5), S(2,8,6), Cl(2,8,7), Ar(2,8,8), K(2,8,8,1), Ca(2,8,8,2).

Step 5: Topper's Exam Tip

Never write Potassium as 2, 8, 9. That violates the outermost octet rule and results in an automatic zero in exams.

Activity 8.4: Analyzing Valency Trends Across Elements
Step 1: Core Concept

Valency is the combining power determined by the number of valence electrons needed to reach an octet (or duplet).

Step 2: Given Data / Investigation

Comparing period 2 (Li to Ne) and period 3 (Na to Ar).

Step 3: Pattern Analysis

• Period 2: Li(1), Be(2), B(3), C(4), N(3), O(2), F(1), Ne(0).
• Period 3: Na(1), Mg(2), Al(3), Si(4), P(3,5), S(2), Cl(1), Ar(0).
Across a period from left to right, valency first increases from 1 to 4, and then decreases steadily from 4 to 0.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Valency shows a strict periodic cycle: metals lose electrons (valency = valence e⁻), metalloids share (valency = 4), non-metals gain electrons (valency = 8 - valence e⁻), and noble gases have valency = 0.

Step 5: Topper's Exam Tip

Elements in the same vertical group (like F and Cl, or Li and Na) have the same number of valence electrons and therefore identical valency.

Activity 8.5: Calculation of Chlorine's Average Atomic Mass from Isotopic Abundances
Step 1: Core Concept

Weighted average atomic mass formula: Average Mass = [(Mass₁ × %₁) + (Mass₂ × %₂)] / 100.

Step 2: Given Data

Isotope 1: ³⁵₁₇Cl with mass = 35 u, abundance = 75%.
Isotope 2: ³⁷₁₇Cl with mass = 37 u, abundance = 25%.

Step 3: Mathematical Deduction

Average Mass = [35 × (75/100)] + [37 × (25/100)]
= [35 × (3/4)] + [37 × (1/4)]
= (105 / 4) + (37 / 4) = 142 / 4 = 35.5 u.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

The average atomic mass of natural chlorine is 35.5 u.

Step 5: Topper's Exam Tip

Always state in your answer that no single atom of chlorine has a mass of 35.5 u; it represents a statistical average of the two isotopes.

In-Text Page 47, Q1: What are canal rays?
Step 1: Definition

Canal rays (also known as anode rays) are streams of positively charged gaseous ions that travel in a direction opposite to cathode rays in a specially designed discharge tube.

Step 2: Discovery Details

Discovered by German physicist Eugen Goldstein in 1886 using a discharge tube fitted with a perforated (pierced with holes) cathode.

Step 3: Physical Nature

They consist of positive ions formed when high-speed cathode rays knock electrons out of residual gas atoms inside the tube. When hydrogen gas is used, these rays consist of bare protons.

Step 4: Scientific Significance

The discovery of canal rays provided the first experimental proof of the existence of the proton inside the atom.

Step 5: Topper's Tip

Unlike cathode rays (whose e/m ratio is universal), the charge-to-mass ratio of canal rays depends on the gas filled in the tube.

In-Text Page 47, Q2: If an atom contains one electron and one proton, will it carry any charge or not?
Step 1: Core Concept

Principle of Electrostatic Superposition and Net Elementary Charge summation.

Step 2: Given Data

• Number of electrons = 1 (Charge = -1 elementary unit = -1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)

Net Charge = Charge of proton + Charge of electron
= (+1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) + (-1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) = 0 C.

No, the atom will not carry any charge. It will be completely electrically neutral. (This represents a neutral Protium hydrogen atom, ¹₁H).

Always mention that the magnitude of charge on a proton and electron is exactly identical, which ensures perfect electrical neutrality.

In-Text Page 49, Q1: On the basis of Thomson’s model of an atom, explain how the atom is neutral as a whole.
Step 1: Core Concept

Thomson's Plum Pudding Model of Atomic Neutrality.

Step 2: Structural Architecture

According to J.J. Thomson (1904), an atom consists of a positively charged continuous sphere in which negatively charged electrons are embedded like seeds in a watermelon.

Step 3: Balance of Charges

The total positive electric charge uniformly spread throughout the sphere is equal in magnitude to the total negative electric charge of all embedded electrons.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Since the opposite electrical charges cancel each other completely, the atom as a whole behaves as an electrically neutral entity.

Step 5: Topper's Tip

Mention that while Thomson correctly predicted overall neutrality, his spatial distribution of charges was proved wrong by Rutherford.

The proton is the positively charged subatomic particle present inside the nucleus.

In-Text Page 49, Q4: What do you think would be the observation if the α-particle scattering experiment is carried out using a foil of a metal other than gold?
Step 1: Core Concept

Step 2: Case 1 - If another Heavy Metal (e.g., Platinum) is used

If another heavy, highly malleable metal with a large atomic number (like Platinum, Z=78) were hammered into an ultra-thin foil, the observations would be essentially identical to gold: most α-particles would pass through, a small fraction would deflect, and ~1 in 12,000 would rebound.

Without a heavy nucleus containing a large positive charge, the discovery of the dense nucleus would not have been clearly observed.

Exercise Q2: What are the limitations of J.J. Thomson’s model of the atom?
Step 1: Core Flaw

Inability to account for the spatial localization of positive charge and massive scattering angles.

Step 2: Thomson's Premise

Thomson assumed that the entire atom is a uniformly distributed sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded throughout.

Step 3: Experimental Contradiction

1. Failure to Explain Alpha Scattering: If positive charge were spread thinly across the whole atom, high-energy α-particles (mass 4 u) would pass right through with virtually negligible deflection. Thomson's model completely failed to explain why some α-particles deflected at large angles or bounced straight back (180°).
2. Lack of Experimental Foundation: The model was purely speculative and lacked any supporting quantitative experimental evidence.
3. Failure to Predict Nuclear Structure: It could not explain how the positive sphere held onto electrons without collapsing due to mutual attraction.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Thomson's plum pudding model could not explain the results of Rutherford's alpha scattering experiment and was therefore discarded.

Step 5: Topper's Tip

Acknowledge that Thomson's model was successful in explaining electrical neutrality, but failed on dynamics and scattering.

Exercise Q3: What are the limitations of Rutherford’s model of the atom?
Step 1: Theoretical Conflict

Direct contradiction with Maxwell's Classical Electromagnetic Theory regarding accelerating charges.

Step 2: The Acceleration Factor

In Rutherford's model, electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular orbits. Any particle moving in a circular path undergoes continuous centripetal acceleration due to changing direction of velocity.

Step 3: The Energy Loss & Inward Spiral

According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle must continuously radiate electromagnetic energy. Losing energy, the electron's speed must decrease and its orbit must shrink. The electron would spiral inward and crash into the positive nucleus in approximately 10⁻⁸ seconds.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Rutherford's model predicts that atoms should be violently unstable and collapse almost instantaneously. Since matter is exceptionally stable, Rutherford's model could not explain the stability of an atom. Furthermore, it did not specify the distribution or energy of electrons in orbits.

Step 5: Topper's Tip

State the exact term: "Continuous electromagnetic radiation by an accelerated charged particle leading to orbital collapse."

Exercise Q4: Describe Bohr’s model of the atom.
Step 1: Core Hypothesis

Niels Bohr (1913) resolved the stability dilemma by introducing the concept of quantized, non-radiating energy orbits.

Step 2: Postulate 1 - Discrete Orbits

Only certain special orbits known as discrete orbits (or stationary states) of electrons are allowed inside the atom. Electrons can only revolve in these specific orbits and cannot exist between them.

Step 3: Postulate 2 - Non-Radiating Energy

While revolving in these discrete orbits, electrons do not radiate energy. Their energy remains constant. An electron only absorbs energy to jump to a higher shell, or emits energy when falling to a lower shell.

Step 4: Energy Levels / Shells

These orbits or energy levels are designated by letters K, L, M, N... or quantum numbers n = 1, 2, 3, 4... starting from the one closest to the nucleus.

Step 5: Topper's Tip

Always accompany your answer with a neat sketch showing the central nucleus and the concentric K, L, M shells.

Step 2: Rule 1 - Maximum Capacity (2n²)

Exercise Q7: Define valency by taking examples of silicon and oxygen.
Step 1: Formal Definition

Valency is defined as the combining capacity of an atom of an element. It is the number of electrons an atom must lose, gain, or share with other atoms to achieve a stable octet (or duplet) in its outermost shell.

Step 2: Example 1 - Silicon (Si)

• Atomic Number Z = 14.
• Electronic Configuration = 2, 8, 4.
• Number of valence electrons = 4.
• To complete its octet, silicon shares 4 electrons with other atoms.
• Valency of Silicon = 4.

Step 3: Example 2 - Oxygen (O)

• Atomic Number Z = 8.
• Electronic Configuration = 2, 6.
• Number of valence electrons = 6.
• Instead of losing 6 electrons, oxygen gains or shares 2 electrons to complete its octet (8).
• Valency = 8 - 6 = 2.
• Valency of Oxygen = 2.

Step 4: Final Conclusion

Silicon exhibits a tetravalency of 4 by sharing, while oxygen exhibits a divalency of 2 by gaining/sharing 2 electrons.

Exercise Q8: Explain with examples (i) Atomic number, (ii) Mass number, (iii) Isotopes and (iv) Isobars. Give any two uses of isotopes.
Step 1: (i) Atomic Number (Z)

The total number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom. Defines chemical identity.
Example: Carbon has 6 protons in its nucleus, so its Atomic Number Z = 6.

Step 2: (ii) Mass Number (A)

The total sum of protons and neutrons (nucleons) present in the nucleus of an atom (A = Z + N).
Example: An aluminium atom has 13 protons and 14 neutrons; Mass Number A = 13 + 14 = 27.

Step 3: (iii) Isotopes & (iv) Isobars

• Isotopes: Atoms of the same element having same atomic number (Z) but different mass numbers (A). Example: Hydrogen isotopes: Protium (¹₁H), Deuterium (²₁H), Tritium (³₁H); or Carbon: ¹²₆C and ¹⁴₆C.
• Isobars: Atoms of different elements having same mass number (A) but different atomic numbers (Z). Example: Argon (⁴⁰₁₈Ar) and Calcium (⁴⁰₂₀Ca), both having mass number 40.

Step 4: Two High-Value Uses of Isotopes

1. Uranium-235: Used as fuel in nuclear reactors to generate commercial electrical power via nuclear fission.
2. Cobalt-60: Used in radiation oncology for the medical treatment of cancer tumors.

Step 5: Topper's Tip

Never confuse Isotopes (same element) with Isobars (different elements). Remember: Isotopes have same Protons; Isobars have same A (mass number).

Exercise Q9: Na⁺ has completely filled K and L shells. Explain.
Step 1: Neutral Sodium Configuration

To attain a stable noble gas configuration, the sodium atom loses its single valence electron from the outermost M shell:

• The K shell contains 2 electrons, which is its maximum capacity (2n² = 2(1)² = 2).

Q11 (3M): Explain why the mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus. Calculate electron mass percentage in Carbon-12.

Model Answer (Point-Wise):

  1. Subatomic Mass Disparity: The mass of a proton is 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (~1 u) and a neutron is 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (~1 u). In contrast, the mass of an electron is only 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg (~1/1840 u).
  2. Nuclear Residence: Because protons and neutrons reside exclusively inside the nucleus, virtually 99.95% of the total atomic mass is confined to the nucleus.
  3. Calculation for Carbon-12 (¹²₆C):
    • Carbon-12 contains 6 protons, 6 neutrons, and 6 electrons.
    • Mass of nucleus = 6 × 1 u + 6 × 1 u = 12 u.
    • Mass of 6 electrons = 6 × (1/1840 u) = 6 / 1840 u ≈ 0.00326 u.
    • Total mass of atom ≈ 12.00326 u.
    • Percentage of mass contributed by electrons = (0.00326 / 12.00326) × 100 ≈ 0.027%!
    Conclusion: Electrons contribute less than 0.03% of the atomic weight, proving that atomic mass is essentially equal to nuclear mass.
Q12 (2M): Differentiate between Valence Electrons and Valency. Find the valency of Phosphorus (Z = 15).

Model Answer:

  • Valence Electrons: The actual total number of electrons present in the outermost shell of an atom.
  • Valency: The combining capacity of an atom, representing the number of electrons it needs to lose, gain, or share to reach an octet (8).
  • For Phosphorus (Z = 15):
    • Electronic configuration = 2, 8, 5.
    • Number of valence electrons = 5.
    • Valency = 8 - 5 = 3 (Phosphorus also exhibits variable valency of 5 in PCl₅ by sharing all 5 valence electrons).
Q13 (3M): Why is the electronic configuration of Calcium (Z = 20) written as 2, 8, 8, 2 and not 2, 8, 10?

Model Answer:

  1. The 2n² Capacity vs. Outermost Octet Limit: By the Bohr-Bury formula 2n², the theoretical maximum capacity of the M-shell (n = 3) is 2(3)² = 18 electrons.
  2. Rule 2 Violation in 2, 8, 10: If the configuration were written as 2, 8, 10, the M-shell would become the outermost shell. However, Rule 2 of the Bohr-Bury scheme strictly states that the outermost shell cannot contain more than 8 electrons.
  3. Opening the N-Shell: Once the M-shell acquires 8 electrons (forming the stable octet of Argon, 2, 8, 8), the next electrons must enter the next higher energy shell, the N-shell.
  4. Conclusion: For Potassium (Z=19) it is 2, 8, 8, 1, and for Calcium (Z=20) it is strictly 2, 8, 8, 2.

Q16 (2M): Why do noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) exhibit zero chemical reactivity?

Model Answer:

Chemical reactivity is driven by an atom's desire to achieve a stable electronic configuration (a completely filled valence shell).
• Helium (He: 2): Has a completely filled K-shell with 2 electrons (stable duplet).
• Neon (Ne: 2, 8): Has a completely filled L-shell with 8 electrons (stable octet).
• Argon (Ar: 2, 8, 8): Has a completely filled M-valence shell with 8 electrons (stable octet).

Part 1: Experimental Setup (1 Mark)

Ernest Rutherford (1911) directed a narrow beam of high-energy, fast-moving alpha particles (doubly-charged helium ions He²⁺, mass = 4 u) emitted from a radioactive source inside a lead box onto an ultra-thin gold foil (~1000 atoms thick, ~100 nm). Gold was chosen because of its unmatched malleability. A circular zinc sulfide (ZnS) screen surrounded the foil to detect deflected particles via light scintillations.

Part 2: Three Observations (1 Mark)

1. Over 99.9% of the alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil without experiencing any deflection.
2. A very small fraction of alpha particles were deflected by small and moderate angles.
3. An extraordinarily tiny fraction—about 1 in 12,000 particles—rebounded almost straight back along its incoming path (deflection angle ~180°).

Part 3: Three Deductions (1.5 Marks)

1. Empty Space: Since most α-particles passed straight through, most of the space inside the atom is completely empty.
2. Concentrated Positive Charge: The positive charge is not spread out thinly across the atom (as Thomson hypothesized), but occupies a very tiny central volume, exerting strong repulsive force on α-particles that pass close to it.
3. Dense Nucleus: All the positive charge and practically the entire mass of the atom is concentrated in an unimaginably small, dense central core called the nucleus (nuclear radius ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m, atom radius ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m).

Part 4: The Fatal Flaw of Rutherford's Model (1.5 Marks)

Rutherford proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus like planets orbiting the sun. However, according to Maxwell's classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle (such as an electron in circular motion) must continuously radiate energy. Losing energy, the electron must spiral inward toward the positive nucleus and collapse within 10⁻⁸ seconds. Rutherford's model could not explain why atoms are stable.

Q18 (5M): Bohr's Model of the Atom, Postulates, and Stability Resolution
Official CBSE 5-Mark Marking Scheme Breakdown:
• Two Master Postulates of Bohr's Model: 2 Marks
• Resolution of the Rutherford Stability Dilemma: 1.5 Marks
• Energy Shell Diagram & Labeling (K, L, M, N): 1.5 Marks

(a) Two Master Postulates of Niels Bohr (1913):

  1. Discrete Non-Radiating Orbits: Only certain specific, discrete circular orbits (known as stationary states) are permissible for electrons inside an atom. Electrons cannot revolve at random, continuous distances from the nucleus.
  2. Energy Conservation in Orbits: While revolving in these permissible discrete orbits, electrons do not radiate energy. An electron's energy remains constant and quantized as long as it remains in that orbit. Energy is absorbed or emitted only when an electron jumps from one discrete orbit to another.

(b) How Bohr Resolved Rutherford's Stability Paradox:

Rutherford's electrons collapsed because classical physics demanded continuous energy radiation from accelerating charges. Bohr broke away from classical physics by stating that energy radiation in an atom is quantized. Because electrons in discrete orbits cannot radiate energy continuously, they cannot lose speed or spiral inward. The innermost orbit (K-shell, n=1) represents the absolute minimum ground energy state below which no electron can drop. Thus, atomic stability was conclusively explained.

(c) Energy Shell Designations:

Energy shells are labeled starting from the center outward: K (n=1), L (n=2), M (n=3), N (n=4). Energy increases with increasing n (E_K < E_L < E_M < E_N).

(c) Numerical Problem: Percentage Abundance of Boron Isotopes

Given: Mass of ¹⁰₅B = 10.01 u; Mass of ¹¹₅B = 11.01 u; Average atomic mass = 10.81 u.

Q20 (5M): Comprehensive Valency Determination Framework Across Elements
Official CBSE 5-Mark Marking Scheme Breakdown:
• Scientific definition of Valency & Octet rule: 1 Mark
• Step-by-step determination for Na, N, Ne, and Al: 3 Marks
• Group valency constancy deduction: 1 Mark

1. Fundamental Concept: Valency is the combining capacity of an atom. According to the Octet Rule, atoms combine to attain an outermost shell containing 8 electrons (or 2 for K-shell), matching the noble gases.

2. Step-by-Step Determinations:

  • Sodium (Na, Z = 11): Electronic configuration = 2, 8, 1. It has 1 valence electron. To reach an octet, it easily loses 1 electron: Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8) + e⁻. Valency = 1.
  • Aluminium (Al, Z = 13): Electronic configuration = 2, 8, 3. It has 3 valence electrons. It loses 3 electrons to expose the full L-shell: Al (2,8,3) → Al³⁺ (2,8) + 3e⁻. Valency = 3.
  • Nitrogen (N, Z = 7): Electronic configuration = 2, 5. It has 5 valence electrons. Losing 5 electrons requires prohibitive ionization energy; therefore, it gains or shares 3 electrons: Valency = 8 - 5 = 3.
  • Neon (Ne, Z = 10): Electronic configuration = 2, 8. It has 8 valence electrons. Its outermost shell is already saturated with an octet. It neither loses, gains, nor shares electrons. Valency = 8 - 8 = 0.

3. Group Constancy Deduction: Elements in the same vertical column (group) of the periodic table have the same number of valence electrons (e.g., all alkali metals Li, Na, K have 1 valence e⁻; all halogens F, Cl, Br have 7 valence e⁻). Consequently, all elements in a given group share the exact same valency.

5.5 Competency-Based Case Studies (NEP 2026-27 Pattern)

Case Study 1: The Detective Work of Isotopes & Radiocarbon Dating

In an archaeological excavation near the ancient city of Sisupalgarh in Odisha, archaeologists discovered ancient wooden pillars and burnt charcoal fragments. To determine whether these artifacts dated back to the Mauryan or post-Mauryan era, scientific samples were sent to a radiocarbon dating laboratory. The method relies on the radioactive isotope Carbon-14 (¹⁴₆C), which is continuously produced in the upper atmosphere by cosmic rays colliding with Nitrogen-14. While living plants continuously absorb Carbon-14 and Carbon-12 in a constant atmospheric ratio through photosynthesis, upon death, carbon assimilation ceases, and ¹⁴₆C decays steadily into ¹⁴₇N with a half-life of 5,730 years.

Question 1.1: Compare the nuclear compositions of Carbon-12 and Carbon-14.

• Carbon-12 (¹²₆C): Protons = 6, Neutrons = 12 - 6 = 6, Electrons = 6.
• Carbon-14 (¹⁴₆C): Protons = 6, Neutrons = 14 - 6 = 8, Electrons = 6.
Both have identical atomic number (Z=6) and identical electron count (6), but Carbon-14 has 2 extra neutrons in its nucleus.

Question 1.2: Why do Carbon-12 and Carbon-14 both react identically with oxygen to form CO₂?

Chemical reactions depend strictly on electronic configuration and the number of valence electrons. Since both isotopes have 6 electrons with the identical configuration (2, 4), their chemical bonding behaviour with oxygen is completely indistinguishable.

Question 1.3: If a fossil bone sample is found to have only 25% of the original Carbon-14 remaining, estimate its approximate age.

• After 1 half-life (5,730 years): 50% of ¹⁴C remains.
• After 2 half-lives: 50% of 50% = 25% of ¹⁴C remains.
• Estimated age = 2 × 5,730 years = 11,460 years.

Question 1.4: Name another radioactive isotope used in medical oncology and state its specific application.

Cobalt-60 (⁶⁰₂₇Co) is used in radiation oncology. It emits penetrating high-energy gamma rays (γ) that destroy localized cancerous tumors without surgical incision.

Case Study 2: Atomic Architecture & The Magic of Valency in Salt Formation

A student observed that metallic sodium (Na) is an extremely soft, silver-white metal that reacts explosively with cold water, releasing hydrogen gas. Chlorine (Cl) is a suffocating, greenish-yellow poisonous gas used as a chemical weapon in World War I. However, when a heated piece of sodium is dropped into a gas jar of chlorine, a vigorous exothermic reaction occurs with a bright yellow flash, producing harmless white crystals of common table salt (Sodium Chloride, NaCl), which humans consume daily without injury.

Question 2.1: Write the electronic configurations of sodium and chlorine atoms before the reaction.

• Sodium (Na, Z = 11): 2, 8, 1 (Valence electrons = 1).
• Chlorine (Cl, Z = 17): 2, 8, 7 (Valence electrons = 7).

Question 2.2: Explain in terms of electrons why the violent properties of Na and Cl disappear when NaCl is formed.

Reactivity is caused by unstable, incomplete valence shells. Sodium has an unstable single electron in its M-shell, while chlorine desperately seeks 1 electron to complete its octet. During reaction, sodium transfers its 1 valence electron to chlorine. Both species achieve completely filled, stable noble gas octets: Na⁺ (2,8 - Neon configuration) and Cl⁻ (2,8,8 - Argon configuration). Because their octets are fully satisfied, the explosive and toxic properties vanish completely!

Question 2.3: What is the valency of sodium and chlorine in this compound?

Both sodium and chlorine have a valency of 1 (monovalent). Sodium loses 1 electron, and chlorine gains 1 electron.

Question 2.4: State the number of protons and electrons present in the Na⁺ cation and Cl⁻ anion.

• Na⁺ Cation: Protons = 11, Electrons = 10 (Net charge: +1).
• Cl⁻ Anion: Protons = 17, Electrons = 18 (Net charge: -1).

5.6 Previous Years Questions (PYQs) & Most Probable Questions

PYQ 1 [CBSE 2024 / 2023]: An element has 2 electrons in its M-shell. Find Z, valency, and formula of its chloride.
Step 1: Deducing Electronic Configuration

If an atom has electrons in the M-shell, its inner K and L shells must be completely filled by Bohr-Bury Rule 3.
• K-shell = 2 electrons
• L-shell = 8 electrons
• M-shell = 2 electrons (given)
• Total electrons = 2 + 8 + 2 = 12.

Step 2: Atomic Number & Identity

Atomic Number Z = Number of electrons = 12. The element is Magnesium (Mg).

Step 3: Valency

Because it has 2 valence electrons (≤ 4), it loses 2 electrons to achieve an octet. Valency = 2.

Step 4: Formula of its Chloride

Valency of Mg = 2, Valency of Chlorine (Cl) = 1. Applying criss-cross rule: MgCl₂ (Magnesium Chloride).

PYQ 2 [CBSE 2022]: What would happen if a neutron beam were used in Rutherford's experiment instead of alpha particles?
Core Concept

Rutherford's deflection was caused by electrostatic Coulomb repulsion between the positively charged alpha particle (+2) and the positively charged gold nucleus (+79).

Scientific Deduction

Neutrons carry zero electric charge. If a beam of neutrons were directed at the gold foil, they would experience zero electrostatic repulsion from the positive nucleus or electrons. Almost all neutrons would pass completely undeflected, or undergo direct nuclear capture/collision. The wide-angle electrostatic deflections that revealed the positive charge of the nucleus would never have been observed!

PYQ 3 [CBSE 2020]: Why is chlorine written as 2, 8, 7 and not 2, 9, 6? What changes when it becomes Cl⁻?

1. Why 2, 8, 7 and NOT 2, 9, 6: By the Bohr-Bury scheme, the maximum capacity of the L-shell (n = 2) is strictly 2n² = 2(2)² = 8 electrons. Accommodating 9 electrons in the L-shell violates the fundamental quantum capacity rule. Therefore, once L reaches 8, the next electron must enter the M-shell, making it 2, 8, 7.

2. Changes when forming Chloride Ion (Cl⁻):
• Protons: Unchanged! Remains strictly 17.
• Neutrons: Unchanged! Remains strictly 18 (for Cl-35).
• Electrons: Increases from 17 to 18 (Configuration becomes 2, 8, 8).

PYQ 4 [CBSE 2019 / Exemplar]: Nucleus X has 11p, 12n; Nucleus Y has 11p, 13n. State relation and chemical behaviour.

• Atomic Number: Z_X = 11, Z_Y = 11. (Both are Sodium, Na).
• Mass Numbers: A_X = 11 + 12 = 23 (²³₁₁Na); A_Y = 11 + 13 = 24 (²⁴₁₁Na).
• Relation: Because they have the same atomic number (11 protons) but different mass numbers (23 and 24), X and Y are Isotopes of sodium.
• Chemical Behaviour: Identical! Both have 11 electrons with configuration 2, 8, 1 and react with water and halogens in the exact same manner.

The 5 Most Fatal Student Blunders in Chapter 8

Blunder 1: Equating "Valence Electrons" Directly with "Valency"

The Mistake: Writing that the valency of Nitrogen (2, 5) is 5, or the valency of Oxygen (2, 6) is 6, or the valency of Chlorine (2, 8, 7) is 7.
The Correction: Valence electrons are the actual electrons in the outermost shell. Valency is the combining power. When valence electrons > 4, you MUST subtract from 8:
• Nitrogen: 8 - 5 = 3 | Oxygen: 8 - 6 = 2 | Chlorine: 8 - 7 = 1.
• The only elements where valence electrons equal valency are elements with 1, 2, 3, or 4 valence electrons (and for Helium, valence electrons = 2, but valency = 0!).

Blunder 2: Changing the Number of Protons During Ion Formation

The Mistake: Thinking that when Sodium forms Na⁺, its protons decrease from 11 to 10, or when Chlorine forms Cl⁻, its protons increase to 18.
The Correction: Protons are locked deep inside the nucleus by colossal nuclear forces! Chemical reactions and ion formations ONLY involve the loss, gain, or sharing of outermost electrons.
• In Na⁺: Protons = 11 (unchanged), Electrons = 10.
• In Cl⁻: Protons = 17 (unchanged), Electrons = 18.

Blunder 3: Blindly Applying 2n² and Writing Configurations Like 2, 8, 9

The Mistake: Because 2n² for M-shell (n=3) is 2(3)² = 18, students write Potassium (Z = 19) as 2, 8, 9 and Calcium (Z = 20) as 2, 8, 10.
The Correction: Rule 2 of Bohr-Bury explicitly states that the outermost valence shell can NEVER hold more than 8 electrons! Once the M-shell reaches 8 electrons, the N-shell must open.
• Potassium = 2, 8, 8, 1 | Calcium = 2, 8, 8, 2.

Blunder 4: Believing Fractional Atomic Mass Means Fractional Nucleons Exist

The Mistake: Believing that a chlorine atom has 17.5 neutrons, or that individual atoms exist weighing 35.5 u.
The Correction: Protons and neutrons are indivisible whole particles in chemistry. You can never have half a proton or half a neutron! Every individual chlorine atom is either ³⁵Cl (18 neutrons) or ³⁷Cl (20 neutrons). The value 35.5 u is purely a statistical weighted average of macroscopic collections of atoms in nature.

Blunder 5: Misattributing Discoveries & Confusing Particle Inventions

The Mistake: Writing that Rutherford discovered the electron, or that Thomson discovered the nucleus, or that Rutherford discovered the neutron.
The Golden Memory Matrix:
• Electron: J.J. Thomson (1897 via Cathode Rays).
• Proton: Eugen Goldstein (1886 via Canal Rays) & named by Rutherford.
• Nucleus: Ernest Rutherford (1911 via Gold Foil Scattering).
• Discrete Energy Orbits: Niels Bohr (1913).
• Neutron: James Chadwick (1932 via Beryllium α-bombardment).

Subatomic Reality: Dalton's postulate of indivisibility fell when electrical friction experiments proved atoms contain internal electric charges.

Rutherford's Flaw: Accelerated circular motion radiates energy. An electron should spiral inward and collapse into the nucleus in 10⁻⁸ s.
