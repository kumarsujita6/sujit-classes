Section 2 Exhaustive Topic-by-Topic Notes

1. The Concept and Law of Demand

In common everyday conversation, ‘desire’, ‘need’, and ‘demand’ are often used interchangeably. In economics, however, Demand has a very precise scientific definition:

📖 Definition of Demand

Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at a specific price during a given period of time, assuming all other factors remain constant.
Demand = Desire + Ability to Pay (Purchasing Power) + Willingness to Spend.

The Law of Demand:

Formulated by classical economists, the Law of Demand states that, other things remaining equal (ceteris paribus), there exists an inverse relationship between the price of a commodity and its quantity demanded:

  • When the price of a good rises, its quantity demanded falls.
  • When the price of a good falls, its quantity demanded rises.
Figure 9.2: The Downward Sloping Demand Curve (DD’) Demonstrating the Inverse Relationship Between Price and Quantity Demanded.

Individual Demand vs Market Demand:

  • Individual Demand: The quantity demanded by a single individual consumer (such as Srivalli buying mangoes).
  • Market Demand: The total aggregate quantity demanded by all consumers in the market across different prices. Graphically, the market demand curve (DmDm’) is obtained by horizontally summing all individual demand curves. It is significantly flatter because a price drop triggers purchases across thousands of households simultaneously.

2. Determinants of Demand (Factors Influencing Demand)

While price changes cause movement along the demand curve, other factors cause the entire demand curve to shift (Rightward = Increase in Demand; Leftward = Decrease in Demand):

  1. Income of the Consumer:
    • Normal Goods: For most goods (fresh fruits, branded clothing, electronics), an increase in household income leads to higher demand.
    • Inferior Goods: For low-grade coarse grains or worn clothing, an increase in income leads to a fall in demand, as consumers can now afford superior substitutes.
  2. Prices of Related Goods:
    • Substitute Goods (Goods in Competition): Commodities that can be used in place of one another (e.g., Tea and Coffee; Air Conditioner and Air Cooler; Petrol cars and Electric cars). If the price of petrol surges, consumers switch to electric vehicles — hence, the demand for electric cars shifts rightward.
    • Complementary Goods (Goods Used Together): Commodities consumed jointly to satisfy a single want (e.g., Car and Fuel; Smartphone and Mobile Charger; Pen and Notebook; Movie Ticket and Cinema Popcorn). If the price of petrol doubles, the cost of running petrol cars rises, causing the demand for petrol cars to decrease.
  3. Tastes, Preferences, and Social Trends: Shifting lifestyle habits (e.g., growing fitness awareness boosting demand for millets, oats, and gym shoes; festive seasons boosting demand for sweets and ethnic wear).
  4. Future Price Expectations: If consumers anticipate that gold or smartphone prices will drop during an upcoming Diwali festival sale, they postpone purchases today, causing current demand to drop. Conversely, rumors of impending fuel shortages cause panic-buying today.
  5. Demographic Size and Composition: Rapid urbanization and population growth expand the overall market demand.

3. The Concept and Law of Supply

📖 Definition of Supply

Supply refers to the specific quantity of a commodity that producers, manufacturers, or farmers are willing and able to offer for sale at a given price during a specific period of time.

The Law of Supply:

The Law of Supply states that, other things remaining constant (ceteris paribus), there exists a direct, positive relationship between the price of a good and the quantity supplied:

  • When market price rises, sellers are motivated by higher profit margins to supply more.
  • When market price falls, profits shrink, prompting sellers to supply less or divert resources elsewhere.
Figure 9.3: The Upward Sloping Supply Curve (SS’) Demonstrating the Positive Direct Relationship Between Price and Quantity Supplied.

Key Determinants of Supply:

  • Cost of Factors of Production: An increase in fertilizer prices, industrial power tariffs, or wages raises production costs, reducing profit margins and causing the supply curve to shift leftward.
  • Technological Improvements: Innovations such as precision drip irrigation, high-yielding hybrid seeds, or automated factory robotics reduce cost per unit and boost yields, expanding market supply (shifting the curve rightward).
  • Price of Alternative Goods: If chickpea prices surge while wheat prices remain stagnant, farmers shift land to chickpeas, reducing wheat supply.
  • Number of Sellers: Entry of new enterprises into an industry increases total market supply.
  • Future Price Expectations: If onion traders expect prices to surge next month, they may withhold current stocks in cold storage, temporarily lowering today’s market supply.
  • Government Taxes and Subsidies: Taxes raise production costs, whereas subsidies (e.g., solar equipment grants) lower costs, expanding supply.

4. Market Equilibrium: The Self-Correcting Price Mechanism

Neither demand alone nor supply alone sets the price. The market price is forged at the exact intersection of both opposing forces — a point known as Market Equilibrium.

Figure 9.4: Market Equilibrium and Self-Correcting Forces • Resolving Market Surplus and Market Shortage.

How the Invisible Hand Restores Balance:

  1. Case 1: When Price is Higher than Equilibrium (Surplus / Excess Supply):

    At ₹150, sellers supply 450 units, but buyers only demand 150 units. Unsold stock accumulates on warehouse shelves. To prevent spoilage and clear inventory, competing sellers slash prices. As prices drop, buyers purchase more until equilibrium Point E is restored.

  2. Case 2: When Price is Lower than Equilibrium (Shortage / Excess Demand):

    At ₹50, buyers demand 450 units, but sellers only supply 150 units. Long queues form outside shops. Eager buyers offer to pay higher prices rather than leave empty-handed. Noticing intense demand, sellers raise prices until equilibrium Point E is restored.

5. Dynamic Pricing in the Real World

In textbook models, equilibrium appears static; in reality, modern markets feature dynamic pricing where prices adjust in real time based on fluctuating algorithms, time of day, and weather:

  • Airline Ticket Pricing: An airline seat on a Delhi-Mumbai flight may cost ₹3,500 when booked two months in advance, but surge to ₹11,000 on the day of departure as business travelers urgently bid for the final remaining seats.
  • Evening Vegetable Market Discounts: Fresh tomatoes cost ₹60/kg in the morning when quality is crisp. By 8:00 PM, vegetable vendors reduce prices to ₹25/kg because tomatoes are highly perishable, and vendors would rather sell them at a discount than incur total spoilage overnight.
  • Hotel Room Tariffs: Beachfront resort tariffs double during December holiday weeks due to massive demand, but drop during monsoon off-seasons.

6. Government Intervention: Price Ceilings, Price Floors, and Taxes

While free markets are efficient, unconstrained markets can produce cruel social outcomes — making life-saving medicines unaffordable for the poor or driving impoverished farmers into debt when crop prices crash. Therefore, governments intervene through targeted mechanisms:

Position: Fixed legally BELOW equilibrium price. • Purpose: Protect poor consumers from price-gouging. • Market Consequence: Creates SHORTAGE (QD > QS). • Unintended Risks: Long queues, rationing, black market. Real-Life Case: COVID-19 Hand Sanitizers Govt invoked Essential Commodities Act (1955), capping price at ₹100 for 200 ml to stop hoarding. Also used in: Life-saving cancer drugs, cardiac stents. PRICE FLOOR / MSP (Minimum Price) • Position: Fixed legally ABOVE equilibrium price. • Purpose: Protect farmers & workers from exploitation. • Market Consequence: Creates SURPLUS (QS > QD). • Solution: Government must procure unsold grain. Real-Life Case: Minimum Support Price (MSP) FCI buys wheat and paddy at guaranteed MSP, storing buffer stocks to ensure farmer viability. Also used in: Statutory Minimum Wages for laborers.
Figure 9.5: Economic Mechanics of Price Ceilings (creating Shortages) vs Price Floors (creating Surpluses).

Taxes and Subsidies: Market-Friendly Corrections:

  • Sin Taxes on Demerit Goods: Governments levy steep GST and excise taxes (often above 50%) on cigarettes, tobacco, alcohol, and carbonated sugary drinks to increase production costs, shift the supply curve leftward, and reduce consumption.
  • Subsidies on Merit Goods: Governments provide direct financial grants for chemical fertilizers, solar rooftop panels, and electric vehicles (FAME scheme), shifting supply rightward and expanding eco-friendly adoption.

Question 1: ‘An increase in income always leads to a rise in demand for goods.’ Defend or refute with reasons.

Answer: Refuted.

The statement is economically inaccurate because whether demand rises or falls depends on the nature of the commodity:

  1. Normal Goods (Statement Holds True): For standard goods like fresh fruits, branded garments, consumer electronics, and comfortable housing, an increase in household income enhances purchasing power, leading to higher demand (positive income effect).
  2. Inferior Goods (Statement Fails): For low-quality commodities (e.g., coarse grains like jowar/bajra bought purely out of poverty, broken rice, or second-hand clothes), a rise in income causes consumers to abandon these inferior items and upgrade to superior goods (e.g., wheat, basmati rice, branded clothes). Therefore, demand for inferior goods decreases when income rises.
  3. Inexpensive Necessities of Life: For essentials like common iodized salt, demand remains virtually constant regardless of whether a family’s income doubles or triples.
Question 2: If petrol prices double, what happens to the demand for specified commodities?

Answer:

  • a. Demand for Diesel Cars: Rises.
    Diesel cars serve as a direct substitute for petrol vehicles. If petrol becomes twice as expensive, motorists switch to diesel cars to reduce running costs, shifting the demand curve for diesel cars to the right.
  • b. Demand for Electric Cars (EVs): Rises Substantially.
    Electric cars represent an eco-friendly substitute technology. Soaring petrol expenses provide a powerful financial incentive for buyers to purchase electric vehicles, causing a major rightward surge in EV demand.
  • c. Demand for Car Accessories: Falls.
    Car accessories (seat covers, music systems, alloy wheels) are complementary goods to automobiles. Doubling petrol prices dampens the overall purchase of new private petrol cars, which directly causes demand for aftermarket accessories to contract.
  • d. Demand for Public Transport: Rises Sharply.
    Metros, commuter trains, and city buses are cost-effective substitutes for private vehicular travel. Commuters leave private vehicles at home to save money, shifting travel demand toward public transit systems.
Question 3: A farmer adopts drip irrigation (reduces water use by 40%, increases yield by 30%). Analyze the economic effects.

Answer:

  • a. Effect on his Cost of Production:
    Cost of production per unit falls significantly. Water consumption drops by 40%, lowering pumping electricity and diesel costs, while overall harvest yield expands by 30%. Because total operating expenses are spread across a much larger crop harvest, the per-kilogram cost of production decreases.
  • b. Effect on his Willingness to Supply at Different Prices:
    His willingness to supply increases at every price level. Lower production costs widen his profit margins. He is now eager and profitable to supply more produce at existing market prices, shifting his individual supply curve to the right.
  • c. Effect on Overall Market Supply if Many Farmers Adopt Drip Irrigation:
    Total market supply expands substantially. The aggregate market supply curve shifts outward to the right. With abundant produce entering wholesale mandis, the market price of the crop will soften, benefiting consumers with affordable nutrition.
Question 4: Festival Discount Sales — Demand, Supply, and Mutual Benefits

Answer:

  1. Why Sellers Sell at Low Prices (Volume Over Margin):

    During festive seasons, demand is exceptionally high. Retailers exploit economies of scale — by sacrificing a small profit margin per unit, they stimulate a massive surge in sales volume. Lower prices clear aged inventory, free up blocked working capital, and maximize total net revenue.

  2. What Happens to Market Equilibrium:

    When sellers proactively slash prices below original equilibrium, a temporary market shortage is induced. However, because suppliers expand warehouse stocks ahead of time, both the demand curve and supply curve shift rightward, establishing a new equilibrium at a vastly higher quantity traded.

  3. Mutual Benefit for Both Consumers and Sellers:
    • Consumers Benefit: Acquire expensive appliances, electronics, and festive gifts at affordable, discounted rates.
    • Sellers Benefit: Achieve record inventory turnover, eliminate warehouse holding costs, attract millions of new customers, and earn higher aggregate profits.
Question 5: Government Price Ceiling on an Essential Vaccine

Question: Suppose the government sets a maximum sale price for an essential vaccine below the market-driven price. What is likely to happen?

a. Surplus
b. Shortage
c. No effect
d. Fall in demand

Correct Option: b. Shortage

Detailed Elucidation:
Fixing a legal maximum price below the free-market equilibrium is called a Price Ceiling. At this low artificial price:

  1. Quantity Demanded surges: The vaccine becomes highly affordable, so far more patients seek to purchase it.
  2. Quantity Supplied contracts: Pharmaceutical manufacturers face squeezed profit margins and reduce production or prioritize other profitable medicines.
  3. Result: Quantity Demanded exceeds Quantity Supplied (QD > QS), creating a severe market Shortage with long hospital waiting lines unless the government subsidizes production.

Question 6: Sin Taxes on Tobacco/Alcohol vs Other Price-Controlled Goods

Answer:

  1. Sin Taxes on Tobacco & Alcohol: Heavy indirect taxes raise the retail price, using the Law of Demand to discourage unhealthy consumption and reduce public healthcare expenditure.
  2. Other Goods Under Price Controls and Rationales:
    • Coronary Stents and Orthopedic Implants: The National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) caps prices to prevent private corporate hospitals from exploiting vulnerable heart patients.
    • Subsidized Domestic LPG Cylinders & PDS Kerosene: Subsidized by the Ministry of Petroleum to ensure poor rural households do not burn firewood, reducing indoor air pollution and protecting women’s lung health.
    • Chemical Fertilizers (Urea): Price-controlled to ensure smallholder farmers can nourish crops without facing bankruptcy.
Question 7: Can Excessive Government Regulation Hurt Markets? Explain with Examples.

Answer:

Yes, well-intentioned but excessive government regulation can severely distort markets and hurt economic welfare:

  1. Rent Control Acts Causing Housing Decay:

    In cities where governments freeze residential rents at artificially low rates, landlords lose all financial incentive to repair buildings or build new rental housing. As a result, urban buildings dilapidate, and a severe shortage of modern rental flats emerges.

  2. Agricultural Price Capping Causing Black Markets:

    If the government strictly caps the price of onions or pulses below market cost during bad harvest years, farmers and traders withhold stock, causing genuine supplies to vanish into the underground black market where desperate consumers pay double.

  3. The Pre-1991 ‘License Raj’ in India:

    Requiring bureaucratic licenses to produce basic goods stifled competition, created artificial shortages of scooters and telephones, and promoted corruption.

Question 9: Vegetable Market Field Investigation — Tomato Price Dynamics

Answer:

  • a. Who decides vegetable prices?
    Prices are not determined by a single king, bureaucrat, or vendor. They are established by the impersonal interaction of daily market supply and consumer demand in wholesale mandis.
  • b. Why do vegetable prices fluctuate sharply?
    Vegetables are agricultural commodities subject to weather shocks. Unseasonal heavy rains destroy standing crops (reducing supply and sending prices skyrocketing), while a bumper harvest floods mandis (expanding supply and causing prices to crash).
  • c. Why are tomatoes expensive in the morning and cheaper in the evening?
    1. Morning (Peak Quality & High Demand): Tomatoes are fresh and unbruised. Hotels and early homemakers compete to buy the best produce, keeping prices high.
    2. Evening (Perishability & Clearance Urgency): Tomatoes are perishable and cannot be stored without refrigeration. Vendors face zero demand late at night; to avoid total financial loss from rot, they slash prices to clear remaining stocks.

SA 2: Why Is the Market Demand Curve Flatter Than an Individual Demand Curve? 2 Marks

Answer:

  • Aggregation of Multiple Consumers: Market demand is the horizontal sum of all individual consumers in the economy.
  • Larger Quantity Response: When price drops by ₹50, an individual might buy 1 extra kg, but across a city of 1,00,000 households, total quantity demanded expands by thousands of kilograms, resulting in a flatter graphical slope.
SA 3: Explain How a Market Surplus Corrects Itself Automatically 3 Marks

Answer:

  1. Occurrence: Happens when market price is above equilibrium price, causing Quantity Supplied to exceed Quantity Demanded (QS > QD).
  2. Seller Competition: Facing unsold stock in godowns, competing sellers cut prices to attract buyers.
  3. Restoration: Falling prices encourage buyers to purchase more while discouraging excess production until equilibrium is restored.
SA 4: What Is a Price Floor and Why Is It Enacted for Agricultural Crops? 3 Marks

Answer:

  1. Definition: A legally mandated minimum price set above the equilibrium price below which a commodity cannot be sold.
  2. Protection for Farmers: In bumper harvest years, open-market crop prices can crash below production costs. The government announces a Minimum Support Price (MSP) to guarantee that farmers earn a fair living.
SA 5: What Are the Unintended Consequences of a Price Ceiling? 2 Marks

Answer:

  • Persistent Shortages: Low prices induce excess demand while discouraging suppliers from manufacturing the product.
  • Emergence of Black Markets: Unscrupulous traders hoard goods and sell them under the table at inflated prices to desperate buyers.

D. Long Answer Questions (3 Questions • 5M with Marking Breakdown)

LA 1: Detail the Laws of Demand and Supply and the Determination of Market Equilibrium 5 Marks

Marking Breakdown:

  • Law of Demand & curve behavior [1.5 Marks]
  • Law of Supply & curve behavior [1.5 Marks]
  • Intersection at Point E (Market Equilibrium) [1 Mark]
  • Self-correcting adjustment process [1 Mark]

Comprehensive Answer:

  1. Law of Demand: Inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded (downward-sloping curve DD’). As price falls, consumers purchase more.
  2. Law of Supply: Direct positive relationship between price and quantity supplied (upward-sloping curve SS’). As price rises, sellers supply more to maximize profits.
  3. Market Equilibrium: The market price is determined where DD’ and SS’ intersect at Point E. At this point, Quantity Demanded equals Quantity Supplied (QD = QS). The corresponding price is the Equilibrium Price, and quantity is the Equilibrium Quantity.
  4. Self-Correction Mechanism:
    • If Price > Pe: Market Surplus emerges; sellers slash prices until Pe is restored.
    • If Price < Pe: Market Shortage emerges; buyers bid up prices until Pe is restored.

  • Real-world Indian policy examples [1 Mark]

Comprehensive Answer:

LA 3: Evaluate Dynamic Pricing in Real-World Contemporary Markets 5 Marks

Marking Breakdown:

  • Concept of dynamic pricing vs static equilibrium [1 Mark]
  • Airline and railway ticket surge algorithms [1.5 Marks]
  • Vegetable mandi intraday price adjustments [1.5 Marks]
  • Consumer implications & fairness debate [1 Mark]

Comprehensive Answer:

  1. The Concept: Real-world markets are dynamic organisms where prices fluctuate continuously in response to shifting consumer demand, time constraints, seasons, and perishability.
  2. Airlines and Ride-Hailing Apps: Airlines utilize automated dynamic algorithms. Because flight departure times are fixed and empty seats generate zero revenue once airborne, algorithms raise prices as seats fill up, charging premium rates to last-minute corporate travelers.
  3. Intraday Produce Markets: Vegetable vendors adjust prices from morning to evening. Fresh, firm tomatoes sell at ₹60/kg in the morning, but as evening approaches, vendors discount them to ₹25/kg to avoid total losses due to overnight decay.
  4. Implications: Dynamic pricing ensures efficient capacity utilization and clears perishable goods, but can frustrate consumers during emergency crises (e.g., taxi surge pricing during heavy monsoon flooding).

E. Competency-Based Case Studies (2 Real-World Scenarios)

Case Study 1: The Essential Commodities Intervention in Corona-Times 4 Marks

Case Narrative: In March 2020, with the sudden outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, panic-buying led to nationwide stockouts of hand sanitizers and surgical masks. Private retailers began charging ₹300 for a 200 ml sanitizer bottle that previously sold for ₹70. In response, the Ministry of Consumer Affairs invoked the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, declaring hand sanitizers an essential commodity and imposing an absolute price ceiling of ₹100 per 200 ml bottle. Simultaneously, the government granted emergency alcohol distilleries permission to manufacture medical sanitizers. Within six weeks, supplies expanded across all pharmacies.

Questions & Step-by-Step Solutions:

1. Why did hand sanitizer prices skyrocket initially? (1 Mark)
Answer: An unprecedented surge in consumer panic demand clashed with fixed short-term factory supply, creating massive excess demand and allowing retailers to charge exorbitant prices.

2. What regulatory mechanism did the government impose, and what risk did it carry? (1.5 Marks)
Answer: The government imposed a Price Ceiling. The potential economic risk of capping prices below equilibrium is creating severe shortages and driving goods into black markets.

3. How did the government successfully prevent a prolonged shortage? (1.5 Marks)
Answer: The government addressed the supply side by allowing sugar mills and alcohol distilleries to produce sanitizers, dramatically expanding market supply (shifting SS’ rightward) and permanently neutralizing the shortage.

Case Study 2: The Green Energy Shift in City Mobility 4 Marks

Case Narrative: Metro City experienced severe winter smog due to vehicular exhaust. The municipal government doubled the parking cess and fuel surcharge on petrol two-wheelers while offering an immediate ₹15,000 cash subsidy on electric scooters and exempting them from road tax. Within nine months, sales of petrol scooters dropped by 35%, while electric scooter dealerships reported a 200% waiting list.

Questions & Step-by-Step Solutions:

1. How does this case illustrate the relationship between Substitute Goods? (1.5 Marks)
Answer: Petrol scooters and electric scooters are substitute goods. By raising fuel surcharges on petrol scooters (making them costlier) and subsidizing electric scooters (making them cheaper), consumer demand shifted decisively toward electric mobility.

2. Which market determinant was altered by the government subsidy? (1 Mark)
Answer: The subsidy altered the relative price of substitute goods and boosted the purchasing power of electric vehicle consumers.

3. How does this scenario demonstrate the power of economic incentives over administrative bans? (1.5 Marks)
Answer: Instead of enforcing a heavy-handed police ban, the government used taxes and subsidies to modify market price signals, naturally aligning self-interested consumer choices with the public good of cleaner air.

Section 6 Exam Hall Common Mistakes & 15 Crisp Revision Points

Top 5 Conceptual Pitfalls to Avoid

⚠ Mistake 1: Confusing Movement Along a Curve with a Shift of the Curve

The Trap: Stating that a change in consumer income causes a movement along the demand curve.
The Correction: A change in the good’s own price causes movement along the curve (expansion/contraction). Changes in other factors (income, substitutes, tastes) cause the entire curve to shift.

⚠ Mistake 2: Mixing Up Price Ceiling with Price Floor Placement

The Trap: Drawing a Price Ceiling above the equilibrium point.
The Correction: A Price Ceiling is set BELOW equilibrium (to make goods cheaper). A Price Floor is set ABOVE equilibrium (to guarantee higher incomes).

⚠ Mistake 3: Confusing Substitute Goods Cross-Price Effect

The Trap: Writing that if petrol price rises, demand for diesel cars falls.
The Correction: For substitutes, a price rise in Good A leads to an increase in demand for Good B as consumers switch away from the costly item.

⚠ Mistake 4: Believing Demand Equals Just ‘Desire’

The Trap: Defining demand as someone wishing to own a product.
The Correction: Demand requires three components: Desire + Ability to Pay (Purchasing Power) + Willingness to Spend. A pauper desiring an airplane is not economic demand.

⚠ Mistake 5: Assuming Equilibrium Price Never Changes

The Trap: Treating market equilibrium as a permanent fixed number.
The Correction: Market equilibrium is dynamic; shifts in demand or supply constantly forge new equilibrium prices.

15 Ultra-Crisp Revision Points (Read Before Entering Exam Hall)

  1. Demand Definition: Quantity consumers are willing and able to purchase at a specific price.
  2. Law of Demand: Price and quantity demanded share an inverse relationship (ceteris paribus).
  3. Demand Curve: Slopes downward from left to right (DD’).
  4. Market Demand: Horizontal aggregation of all individual demand curves; flatter in slope.
  5. Substitute Goods: Positive cross-price effect (Tea/Coffee; Petrol cars/EVs).
  6. Complementary Goods: Inverse cross-price effect (Cars/Petrol; Phones/Earphones).
  7. Supply Definition: Quantity producers are willing and able to offer for sale at a specific price.
  8. Law of Supply: Price and quantity supplied share a direct positive relationship.
  9. Supply Curve: Slopes upward from left to right (SS’).
  10. Market Equilibrium: Point E where Quantity Demanded equals Quantity Supplied.
  11. Surplus Dynamics: Occurs when Price > Pe; sellers slash prices to clear excess inventory.
  12. Shortage Dynamics: Occurs when Price < Pe; buyers bid up prices until balance is restored.
  13. Price Ceiling: Maximum legal price set below equilibrium; creates shortages (e.g., vaccines).
  14. Price Floor / MSP: Minimum legal price set above equilibrium; creates surpluses (e.g., grain MSP).
  15. Sin Taxes: High taxes on tobacco and alcohol raise costs to discourage unhealthy consumption.
