In everyday speech, we use the word "work" very loosely. Reading a heavy book for three hours, singing a song, standing at a bus stop holding a heavy bag, or pushing against a concrete wall until we sweat are all called "hard work" in ordinary English. However, in the precise language of physics, scientific work has a very strict and specific mathematical meaning.

For work to be done in physics, two non-negotiable physical conditions must be simultaneously satisfied: (1) A net external force must act on the object, and (2) The object must be displaced along or partially along the direction of the force. If there is no displacement, or if the displacement is perpendicular to the applied force, the scientific work done is strictly zero!

2.1 The Scientific Concept of Work

In everyday language, almost any mental, emotional, or physical exertion is described as "work". When a student sits motionless at a desk reading a 400-page biology textbook for six hours, her parents praise her for "working extremely hard". When a factory worker stands completely stationary for an hour holding a 30 kg iron girder on his shoulders, he feels intense muscular strain and sweats profusely, complaining of "exhausting work".

However, in the rigorous language of secondary school physics, neither of these people has performed any scientific work!

Scientific Definition of Work Scientific work is said to be done on an object when an external force acts on the object and produces a displacement of the object along or partially along the line of action of the force.

To establish whether work has taken place in a scientific sense, you must always verify that both of the following conditions are simultaneously met:

  1. A non-zero net external force must act on the object: If no force acts (F = 0), no work can possibly be done.
  2. The object must experience a displacement (s ≠ 0): The body must physically move from one position to another. If displacement is zero, no work is done, regardless of how large the applied force is.
The Concrete Wall Paradox: Why Sweating Is Not Scientific Work Suppose you push with all your muscular might against a massive concrete wall of a building with a force of 500 N for two hours. Your muscles consume biochemical energy, your body burns glucose, and your heart races. But the wall does not move even a fraction of a millimetre (Displacement s = 0).
Mathematically: W = F × s = 500 N × 0 m = 0 Joules.
Scientific work done on the wall is strictly zero! The biochemical energy expended by your body is converted into internal thermal energy inside your muscle fibres, raising your body temperature and causing sweat, but zero mechanical work is delivered to the wall.

Mathematical Formulation of Work

When a constant force F acts on a body and displaces it by a distance s in the exact direction of the force, the work done W is mathematically defined as the scalar product of the magnitude of the force and the displacement:

W = F × s

Units of Work: The Joule and The Erg

Work is a scalar quantity. It possesses magnitude and an algebraic sign (+, -, or 0), but it has no spatial direction.

  • SI Unit: The SI unit of force is the Newton (N) and the SI unit of displacement is the metre (m). Therefore, the SI unit of work is the Newton·metre (N·m), which is named the Joule (J) in honour of the English physicist James Prescott Joule (1818–1889).
  • Definition of 1 Joule: One Joule is defined as the amount of work done when an external force of 1 Newton moves an object through a displacement of 1 metre in the direction of the applied force.
    1 Joule = 1 Newton × 1 Metre = 1 N·m = 1 kg·m²·s⁻²
  • Multiples of Joule: For larger mechanical operations:
    • 1 Kilojoule (kJ) = 1,000 J = 10³ J
    • 1 Megajoule (MJ) = 1,000,000 J = 10⁶ J
    • 1 Gigajoule (GJ) = 1,000,000,000 J = 10⁹ J
  • CGS Unit (Erg): In the CGS (Centimetre-Gram-Second) system, the unit of force is the dyne and displacement is measured in centimetres (cm). The CGS unit of work is the Erg (1 erg = 1 dyne × 1 cm).
    Derivation of Conversion between Joule and Erg:
    We know: 1 Newton = 1 kg × 1 m/s² = 1000 g × 100 cm/s² = 10⁵ dynes.
    And: 1 metre = 100 cm = 10² cm.
    Therefore: 1 Joule = 1 N × 1 m = (10⁵ dynes) × (10² cm) = 10⁷ dynes·cm = 10⁷ ergs.
    1 Joule = 10⁷ Ergs (or 1 Erg = 10⁻⁷ Joules).

2.2 Work Done at an Angle & The Three Sign Conventions

In many practical situations, the applied force does not act exactly parallel to the direction in which the object moves. For instance, when a young child pulls a toy car using an inclined string, the string pulls upward and forward at an angle θ to the horizontal floor, yet the toy car glides purely horizontally along the ground.

In such cases, only the component of the force that acts along the line of displacement contributes to mechanical work. Resolving the force F into rectangular components:

  • The horizontal component parallel to displacement is F_parallel = F × cos θ.
  • The vertical component perpendicular to displacement is F_perp = F × sin θ (this component only alters the normal reaction, doing zero work along the floor).
W = F × s × cos θ
Figure 7.2: Vector Analysis & Sign Conventions of Scientific Work: Positive (θ=0°), Zero (θ=90°), and Negative (θ=180°).

Depending on the geometric angle θ between the force vector F and the displacement vector s, the physical work done falls into three distinct categories:

Case 1: Positive Work (0° ≤ θ < 90°)

When the angle θ is acute (between 0° and 90°), cos θ is positive. Specifically, when the force and displacement are in the exact same direction, θ = 0° and cos 0° = +1:

W = F × s × cos 0° = + F × s

Physical Meaning: The applied force actively aids and encourages the displacement. Energy is transferred from the agent applying the force to the object, increasing its mechanical energy.

Everyday Examples of Positive Work:

  • A horse pulling a cart forward along a straight road (Force and displacement both point forward).
  • A free-falling mango dropping from a tree toward the Earth (Gravitational force pulls vertically downwards and the mango is displaced vertically downwards; W_gravity = +mgh).
  • A weightlifter lifting a heavy barbell from the floor to chest height (Upward lifting force and upward displacement are in the same direction).

Case 2: Zero Work (θ = 90° or s = 0)

When the force vector acts at a right angle (perpendicular) to the displacement vector, θ = 90°. Since cos 90° = 0:

W = F × s × cos 90° = F × s × 0 = 0 Joules

Physical Meaning: The force has zero component along the direction of motion. It neither accelerates nor retards the object along its path. Consequently, it delivers zero energy to the object.

Classic Zero-Work Exam Scenarios:

  • The Railway Coolie (Porter) Dilemma: A porter carries a heavy 40 kg suitcase on his head and walks 100 metres along a horizontal railway platform.
    Analysis: The downward gravitational pull on the suitcase is F_g = mg (acting vertically downward). The porter provides an upward reaction force R = mg to prevent the luggage from falling. However, his displacement s is horizontal along the platform. The angle between the vertical gravitational force and the horizontal displacement is exactly θ = 90°.
    Therefore, the work done by gravity on the luggage is: W = mg × s × cos 90° = 0.
    Similarly, the work done by the porter's upward supporting force on the luggage is also zero!
    (Note for curious minds: Why does the porter get tired? The porter does positive work against the frictional force of the floor using his leg muscles, and consumes metabolic energy balancing his body, but zero work is done ON the luggage against gravity!)
  • Planetary and Satellite Orbits (Circular Motion): The Moon orbits the Earth in an approximately circular orbit. The Earth's gravitational pull acts radially inward toward the Earth's center (centripetal force). However, at every instant, the instantaneous displacement (tangential velocity) of the Moon is directed along the tangent to the circular orbit.
    The radius is always strictly perpendicular to the tangent (θ = 90°).
    Therefore: W_gravity = F_centripetal × s × cos 90° = 0.
    The Earth does zero work on satellites in circular orbit! This is why satellites can orbit for billions of years without needing fuel to counteract gravity.

Case 3: Negative Work (90° < θ ≤ 180°)

When the force acts in a direction opposing the displacement, the angle θ is obtuse (between 90° and 180°), and cos θ is negative. In the extreme case where the force directly opposes the displacement, θ = 180° and cos 180° = -1:

W = F × s × cos 180° = - F × s

Physical Meaning: The force resists, opposes, and retards the motion of the body. Energy is taken away (drained) from the object, decreasing its kinetic energy.

Everyday Examples of Negative Work:

  • Frictional Resistance: When a wooden box slides across a rough floor to the right, the force of friction between the box and the floor acts to the left (opposing relative motion). Here θ = 180°. The work done by friction is W_f = -f × s. This negative work removes kinetic energy from the box and transforms it into heat and sound.
  • Throwing an Object Vertically Upward: When you throw a cricket ball vertically up into the air, the ball travels upward (displacement vector is upward), while the Earth's gravitational pull acts vertically downward. The angle between them is 180°. Therefore, the work done by gravity while the ball is ascending is negative: W_gravity = -mgh. Gravity steadily drains the ball's kinetic energy until its velocity reaches zero at the maximum height.
  • Braking a Motor Vehicle: When the driver slams on the brakes of a speeding car, the braking pads generate frictional torque opposing wheel rotation. The braking force acts opposite to the vehicle's forward displacement. The work done by the braking force is negative, bringing the car safely to rest.

2.3 The Scientific Concept of Energy

Life is impossible without energy. The demand for energy is ever increasing. Where do we get energy from? The Sun is the biggest natural source of energy for us. Many of our energy sources are derived from the Sun. We can also get energy from the nuclei of atoms, the interior of the earth, and the tides.

Definition of Energy Energy is defined as the capacity or ability of an object to do work. An object that possesses energy can exert a force on another object and transfer energy to it.

Work and Energy are Two Sides of the Same Coin:

  • The object which does work loses energy.
  • The object on which work is done gains energy.
  • The amount of work done on an object is exactly equal to the amount of energy transferred to it.
  • Therefore, energy is measured in the exact same unit as work: the Joule (J) in the SI system.
  • Energy is a scalar quantity (it has magnitude but no directional orientation).

Energy exists in diverse physical, chemical, and nuclear forms:

K
Kinetic Energy: Energy possessed by an object in virtue of its motion (e.g., speeding bullet, blowing cyclone wind).
P
Potential Energy: Energy stored due to an object's spatial position, height, or physical deformation (e.g., water in dam, drawn bow).
T
Thermal / Heat Energy: Microscopic random kinetic energy of vibrating atoms and molecules inside a substance.
C
Chemical Energy: Energy stored within molecular chemical bonds, released during exothermic reactions (e.g., coal, petrol, food).
E
Electrical Energy: Energy associated with the movement of electric charges through a conductor.
N
Nuclear Energy: Energy bound within atomic nuclei, released during nuclear fission or nuclear fusion.

In Class 9 mechanics, our primary focus is on Mechanical Energy, which is the sum total of an object's Kinetic Energy and Potential Energy:

Mechanical Energy = Kinetic Energy (KE) + Potential Energy (PE)

2.4 Kinetic Energy: Rigorous Mathematical Derivations

A fast-moving cricket ball hits the stumps and uproots them. A bullet fired from a gun pierces through a wooden target. Fast-flowing mountain river water turns the massive blades of a water turbine. The blowing wind drives the giant sails of a windmill to generate electricity.

All these moving objects possess the ability to exert a force and displace another body—meaning they possess energy by virtue of their motion.

Definition of Kinetic Energy Kinetic energy is the mechanical energy possessed by an object due to its state of motion. The kinetic energy of an object moving with a certain velocity is equal to the work done on it to make it acquire that velocity from rest.

Step-by-Step Algebraic Derivation of Kinetic Energy Formula (KE = ½ m v²)

Let us mathematically derive the formula for the kinetic energy of an object of mass m moving with uniform velocity v:

  1. Consider a rigid object of mass m initially lying at rest on a frictionless horizontal plane.
    Therefore, its initial velocity: u = 0.
  2. Now, let a constant net external force F act on the object in the forward direction.
  3. According to Newton's Second Law of Motion, this force produces a uniform acceleration a given by:
    F = m × a     --- [Equation 1]
  4. Under this constant acceleration a, let the object travel a displacement s and attain a final velocity v.
  5. From the Third Equation of Motion for uniformly accelerated linear motion:
    v² - u² = 2as
    Substitute initial velocity u = 0 into the equation:
    v² - 0 = 2as ⇒ v² = 2as
    Solving for displacement s:
    s = v² / (2a)     --- [Equation 2]
  6. By the scientific definition of work, the work done W by the force F in moving the object through distance s is:
    W = F × s
  7. Now, substitute the value of F from Equation 1 and s from Equation 2 into the work formula:
    W = (m × a) × [v² / (2a)]
    Notice that the acceleration term a in the numerator and denominator cancel each other out completely:
    W = m × [v² / 2] = ½ m v²
  8. Since the object started from rest, the entire amount of work done on the object is stored in it as its kinetic energy. Therefore:
    Kinetic Energy (KE) = ½ m v²

The Work-Energy Theorem (Derivation for Initial Velocity u ≠ 0)

What happens if the object is already moving with an initial velocity u when the force F begins to act on it?

Let the force F accelerate the object from initial velocity u to final velocity v over displacement s:

  • From v² - u² = 2as, we have displacement: s = (v² - u²) / (2a).
  • Work done: W = F × s = (m × a) × [(v² - u²) / (2a)] = ½ m (v² - u²).
  • Expanding the brackets:
    W = ½ m v² - ½ m u² = KE_final - KE_initial = ΔKE
The Work-Energy Theorem Statement The net work done by all external forces acting on a body is exactly equal to the change in the kinetic energy of the body:
W_net = ΔKE = KE_final - KE_initial
• If W_net > 0: Kinetic energy increases (object speeds up).
• If W_net < 0: Kinetic energy decreases (object slows down).
• If W_net = 0: Kinetic energy remains constant (uniform speed).

Relationship Between Kinetic Energy and Linear Momentum

Linear momentum is defined as p = m × v, which means velocity can be expressed as v = p / m.

Substitute this into the kinetic energy formula:

KE = ½ m v² = ½ m (p / m)² = ½ m (p² / m²) = p² / (2m)
KE = p² / (2m)   or inversely:   p = √(2 m · KE)

High-Yield Exam Deductions:

  • If speed is doubled (v → 2v): Since KE is proportional to the square of velocity (KE ∝ v²), doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy (KE becomes 4 times)! A car travelling at 100 km/h has 4 times more kinetic energy than at 50 km/h, requiring 4 times longer braking distance.
  • If momentum is doubled (p → 2p) for constant mass: KE increases by 2² = 4 times (a 300% increase).
  • Equal kinetic energies for heavy and light bodies: From p = √(2m·KE), if two bodies have the same KE, the heavier body (larger mass m) possesses greater linear momentum.

2.5 Potential Energy: Gravitational & Elastic

When you wind the spring of a toy car, stretch a rubber band in a catapult, compress an accordion bellows, or lift a brick to the roof of a house, you perform work on the object. In each of these cases, the object does not gain velocity immediately. Where does your expended work go?

The work done is stored inside the object as Potential Energy.

Definition of Potential Energy Potential energy is the energy possessed by a body by virtue of its position, elevated height, physical shape, or configuration relative to a reference frame.

1. Gravitational Potential Energy (PE = mgh)

Gravitational potential energy of an object at a point above the ground is defined as the work done in raising it from the ground to that point against gravity.

Step-by-Step Derivation:

  1. Consider an object of mass m lying on the ground.
  2. The downward gravitational force acting on it (its weight) is W_g = m × g, directed vertically downwards.
  3. To raise this object to a vertical height h with minimum energy expenditure (at a constant velocity without accelerating it), an upward external force equal and opposite to its weight must be applied:
    F_applied = m × g (upward).
  4. The displacement of the object is h (upward).
  5. Since the applied force and the displacement are both vertically upward, the angle between them is θ = 0°.
  6. The work done W by the applied force against gravity is:
    W = Force × Displacement = (m × g) × h = mgh
  7. This work done is stored in the object as its gravitational potential energy (PE):
    Gravitational Potential Energy (PE) = m g h
    Where:
    m = mass of the object (kg)
    g = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s² or 10 m/s²)
    h = vertical height above the reference level (m)

Crucial Concept: Path Independence of Gravitational Potential Energy

Does the gravitational potential energy depend on how the object reached that height?

NO! The work done by or against gravity depends ONLY on the initial and final vertical levels, NOT on the path taken!

  • Path 1: Vertical Lift: Lifting an object of mass m vertically straight up by height h requires work W = mgh.
  • Path 2: Smooth Inclined Plane (Ramp): Sliding the same object up a frictionless ramp of length L inclined at angle θ to height h (where h = L · sin θ).
    The component of gravity parallel to the ramp is mg · sin θ.
    To push it up at constant speed, the applied force required is F = mg · sin θ.
    Work done: W = F × L = (mg · sin θ) × L = mg × (L · sin θ) = mgh!
  • Path 3: Zigzag Staircase: A flight of stairs consists of horizontal treads and vertical risers. During the horizontal steps, displacement is perpendicular to gravity, so work done by gravity is zero (cos 90° = 0). During the vertical risers, work is done against gravity. Summing all vertical risers: h₁ + h₂ + ... + h_n = h, giving total work W = mg(h₁ + h₂ + ...) = mgh!

Because the work done by gravity is completely independent of the path taken, gravity is classified as a conservative force.

2. Elastic Potential Energy of a Deformed Spring

When a coiled steel spring of stiffness constant k (spring constant in N/m) is compressed or stretched by a displacement x from its natural relaxed length, Hooke's Law states that the restoring force is F = k × x.

As the spring stretches from 0 to x, the force increases linearly from 0 to kx. The average force exerted is:

F_average = (0 + kx) / 2 = ½ k x
Work Done W = F_average × x = (½ k x) × x = ½ k x²

Therefore, the Elastic Potential Energy stored in a stretched or compressed spring is:

PE_elastic = ½ k x²

2.6 Law of Conservation of Energy: Complete Analytical Proof

Whenever energy changes from one form to another, the total amount of energy remains constant. This is the Law of Conservation of Energy, one of the most foundational and inviolable laws in all of natural science.

The Law of Conservation of Energy Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form into another. The total energy of an isolated physical system remains strictly constant at all times.

Mathematical Proof for a Body Falling Freely Under Gravity

Let us prove analytically that the total mechanical energy (sum of kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy) of a body of mass m falling freely under gravity from a height H is conserved at every single point during its descent.

Let us examine the total mechanical energy at three distinct positions: Point A (the initial release point at the top), Point B (an intermediate point after falling a distance x), and Point C (just before striking the ground level).

Step 1: Total Mechanical Energy at Point A (At Top, Height = H)
  • The body is held at rest at height H above the ground.
  • Initial velocity: u = 0.
  • Kinetic Energy at A: KE_A = ½ m u² = ½ m (0)² = 0.
  • Height above reference ground level: h = H.
  • Potential Energy at A: PE_A = m g H.
  • Total Mechanical Energy at A:
    E_A = KE_A + PE_A = 0 + mgH = mgH     --- [Result 1]
Step 2: Total Mechanical Energy at Point B (After Falling Distance x)
  • The body falls freely under gravity through vertical distance x to reach Point B.
  • Height of Point B above ground: h_B = H - x.
  • Potential Energy at B: PE_B = m g (H - x) = mgH - mgx.
  • Let velocity at Point B be v_B. Using the third equation of motion (v² - u² = 2as):
    Here: u = 0, acceleration a = g, and distance fallen s = x.
    v_B² - 0 = 2 g x ⇒ v_B² = 2 g x.
  • Kinetic Energy at B: KE_B = ½ m v_B² = ½ m (2 g x) = m g x.
  • Total Mechanical Energy at B:
    E_B = KE_B + PE_B = mgx + (mgH - mgx) = mgH     --- [Result 2]
Step 3: Total Mechanical Energy at Point C (At Ground Level, Height = 0)
  • The body reaches ground level, having fallen the entire vertical height H.
  • Height above ground: h_C = 0.
  • Potential Energy at C: PE_C = m g (0) = 0.
  • Let velocity just before striking the ground be v_C. Using v² - u² = 2as:
    Here: u = 0, a = g, and total distance fallen s = H.
    v_C² - 0 = 2 g H ⇒ v_C² = 2 g H.
  • Kinetic Energy at C: KE_C = ½ m v_C² = ½ m (2 g H) = m g H.
  • Total Mechanical Energy at C:
    E_C = KE_C + PE_C = mgH + 0 = mgH     --- [Result 3]
Conclusion: E_A = E_B = E_C = mgH = Constant

As the body falls, its potential energy gradually decreases while its kinetic energy increases by the exact same amount. At any intermediate point, the loss in potential energy equals the gain in kinetic energy (ΔPE = -ΔKE). The total mechanical energy remains strictly constant at all stages of free fall.

Energy Transformations in an Oscillating Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum consists of a small heavy metallic bob suspended from a rigid support by a light, inextensible thread.

Figure 7.4: Continuous Interconversion between Potential Energy and Kinetic Energy in an Oscillating Simple Pendulum.

When the bob is pulled to an extreme position A and released:

  • At Extreme Position A: The bob momentarily comes to rest (velocity v = 0). Therefore, its Kinetic Energy is zero. However, the bob has risen through a vertical height h above its lowest level. Therefore, its Potential Energy is maximum: PE = mgh. All energy is pure potential energy.
  • Moving from A towards Mean Position O: As the bob swings downward along the circular arc, its vertical height decreases, causing potential energy to decrease. Concurrently, gravitational acceleration increases the bob's speed, so its kinetic energy increases.
  • At Mean Position O (Lowest Point): The height is zero (h = 0), so Potential Energy is minimum (PE = 0). The speed of the bob reaches its maximum value: v_max = √(2gh). Therefore, its Kinetic Energy is maximum: KE = ½ m (v_max)² = ½ m (2gh) = mgh. All potential energy has been converted into kinetic energy!
  • Moving from O towards Extreme Position B: The bob continues past O due to inertia, ascending towards B. Its speed drops, converting kinetic energy back into potential energy.
  • At Extreme Position B: The bob stops momentarily (v = 0, KE = 0) at height h, storing pure potential energy PE = mgh.
Why Does a Pendulum Eventually Stop? Does it Violate Energy Conservation? In a real classroom, an oscillating pendulum gradually reduces its amplitude and eventually comes to complete rest. This does NOT violate the Law of Conservation of Energy!
As the bob swings through the air, it does work against air resistance (viscous drag) and friction at the pivot support. This mechanical energy is transformed into an equivalent amount of heat energy (warming the air molecules and the string slightly) and a faint sound energy. The total energy of the pendulum plus its surrounding atmosphere remains 100% conserved!

2.7 Power & Commercial Unit of Energy

Do all machines or humans perform work at the exact same speed?

Imagine a strong young teenager who climbs a flight of stairs carrying a 20 kg sack in 15 seconds. An elderly grandfather carrying the same 20 kg sack climbs the exact same flight of stairs in 90 seconds.

Since both lift the same mass through the same vertical height, the work done by both is identical: W = mgh. However, the teenager did the work in much less time! In engineering and daily life, the rate at which work is performed is of paramount importance.

Definition of Power Power is defined as the time rate of doing work or the rate of transfer or consumption of energy.
Mathematically: Power (P) = Work done (W) / Time taken (t) = Energy (E) / Time (t)

Units of Power: The Watt

  • SI Unit: The SI unit of work is the Joule (J) and time is measured in seconds (s). Therefore, the SI unit of power is the Joule per second (J/s), named the Watt (W) in honour of James Watt (1736–1819), the developer of the modern steam engine.
  • Definition of 1 Watt: One Watt is defined as the power of a machine or device which performs work at the rate of 1 Joule per second (or consumes energy at the rate of 1 Joule per second).
    1 Watt = 1 Joule / 1 Second = 1 J/s = 1 kg·m²·s⁻³
  • Larger Engineering Units:
    • 1 Kilowatt (kW) = 1,000 Watts = 10³ W
    • 1 Megawatt (MW) = 1,000,000 Watts = 10⁶ W
    • 1 Gigawatt (GW) = 1,000,000,000 Watts = 10⁹ W
    • 1 Horsepower (hp) = 746 Watts (Imperial/industrial unit used for automobile engines and agricultural water pumps).

The Commercial Unit of Electrical Energy: The Kilowatt-hour (kWh)

The Joule is an exceedingly small unit of energy for commercial and domestic accounting. For example, a single 100-Watt electric bulb running for 10 hours consumes:

Energy = Power × time = 100 W × (10 × 3600 s) = 3,600,000 Joules!

Writing electricity bills in millions of Joules would be completely impractical for electricity supply corporations and consumers. To make billing simple and manageable, commercial electricity meters use a much larger unit: the Kilowatt-hour (kWh), commonly referred to on electricity bills as one "Unit".

Definition of 1 Kilowatt-hour (1 kWh) One kilowatt-hour is the amount of electrical energy consumed by an electrical appliance having a power rating of 1 kilowatt (1000 Watts) when it operates continuously for a duration of 1 hour.
1 kWh = 1 Board of Trade (B.O.T.) Unit of Electricity

Step-by-Step Derivation: Converting 1 kWh to Joules

To convert 1 kilowatt-hour into standard SI Joules:

  1. Recall that: 1 Kilowatt (kW) = 1000 Watts = 1000 Joules/second.
  2. Recall that: 1 hour = 60 minutes = 60 × 60 seconds = 3600 seconds.
  3. Multiply power by time:
    1 kWh = 1 kW × 1 hour
    1 kWh = (1000 J/s) × (3600 s)
    1 kWh = 3,600,000 Joules
    In scientific exponential notation:
    1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ Joules

2.8 Simple Machines: Mechanics & The Six Classical Types

From the earliest dawn of human civilisation, humans have invented tools to multiply their muscular strength and accomplish tasks that would otherwise be impossible. A heavy boulder weighing 2000 N cannot be lifted directly by human arms, but a single person can effortlessly pry it up using a long rigid iron crowbar.

Definition of a Simple Machine A simple machine is a mechanical device that alters the magnitude, direction, or speed of an applied force, making work easier, faster, or more convenient for humans.
The Law of Conservation Rule for Machines: No Free Energy! A machine CANNOT create energy out of nothing! By the Law of Conservation of Energy, the useful work output delivered by an ideal machine can at most equal the work input supplied to it:
Work Output ≤ Work Input
If a machine allows you to apply a smaller effort to lift a heavy load (force multiplication), you MUST push that effort over a proportionally longer distance! Force is multiplied, but work is conserved.

Fundamental Parameters of Simple Machines

Three core mathematical parameters define the performance of any simple machine:

  1. Mechanical Advantage (MA): The ratio of the resistive force to be overcome (Load, L) to the force applied to the machine (Effort, E):
    Mechanical Advantage (MA) = Load / Effort = L / E
    • If MA > 1: The machine acts as a Force Multiplier (a small effort overcomes a large load, e.g., crowbar, car jack).
    • If MA < 1: The machine acts as a Speed Multiplier / Gain in Distance (the load moves faster or farther than the effort, e.g., pair of scissors cutting paper, bicycle gears).
    • If MA = 1: The machine does not amplify force; it only changes the direction of the applied force (e.g., single fixed pulley).
    • Unit: MA is a dimensionless ratio of two forces (Newtons / Newtons); it has NO units!
  2. Velocity Ratio (VR): The ratio of the distance moved by the effort (d_E) to the distance moved by the load (d_L) in the same time interval:
    Velocity Ratio (VR) = Distance moved by Effort / Distance moved by Load = d_E / d_L
    VR depends purely on the geometry and dimensions of the machine and remains constant regardless of friction. It is also dimensionless with NO units.
  3. Efficiency (η): The ratio of the useful work done by the machine (Work Output) to the total work done on the machine (Work Input), expressed as a percentage:
    Efficiency (η) = (Work Output / Work Input) × 100%
    Since: Work Output = Load × d_L and Work Input = Effort × d_E:
    η = [(Load × d_L) / (Effort × d_E)] = (Load / Effort) / (d_E / d_L) = MA / VR
    Efficiency (η) = (MA / VR) × 100%   ⇒   MA = η × VR
    • Ideal Machine: A hypothetical machine free of friction, air drag, and weight in its moving components. Here η = 100% = 1, so MA = VR.
    • Real Practical Machine: In every real-world machine, some portion of input work is dissipated as friction between sliding surfaces and energy spent lifting moving components. Therefore, η < 100% and MA is always strictly less than VR!

The Six Classical Simple Machines

1. The Lever & The Principle of Moments

A lever is a rigid straight bar capable of turning freely about a fixed point called the Fulcrum (F). When in equilibrium under the action of Load (L) and Effort (E), the Principle of Moments states:

Load × Load Arm = Effort × Effort Arm   ⇒   Load / Effort = Effort Arm / Load Arm

Therefore: MA = Effort Arm / Load Arm.

Levers are categorised into three distinct classes based on the relative position of the Fulcrum, Load, and Effort (remember the easy mnemonic FLE - 123: Fulcrum in middle = Class 1, Load in middle = Class 2, Effort in middle = Class 3):

  • Class 1 Lever (Fulcrum in the Middle: L – F – E): The fulcrum lies between the load and the effort. Depending on whether the effort arm is longer than, equal to, or shorter than the load arm, MA can be > 1, = 1, or < 1.
    Examples: See-saw (MA = 1), crowbar (MA > 1, force multiplier), scissors (MA < 1 for paper cutting speed), pliers, beam balance.
  • Class 2 Lever (Load in the Middle: F – L – E): The load lies between the fulcrum and the effort. Because the effort arm is the full distance from fulcrum to effort, the effort arm is always longer than the load arm.
    Therefore: MA is ALWAYS greater than 1 (MA > 1). Class 2 levers are always force multipliers!
    Examples: Wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener, paper guillotine cutter.
  • Class 3 Lever (Effort in the Middle: F – E – L): The effort is applied between the fulcrum and the load. The effort arm is always shorter than the load arm.
    Therefore: MA is ALWAYS less than 1 (MA < 1). While you must apply more effort than the load, you achieve a massive gain in speed and distance!
    Examples: Sugar tongs, tweezers, fishing rod, broom, human forearm lifting a dumbbell.
2. The Pulley

A pulley is a wooden or metallic disc with a grooved rim over which a flexible string or rope runs. The disc rotates on an axle supported in a frame called a block.

  • Single Fixed Pulley: The axle is anchored to a rigid overhead support. Both Load and Effort move through the same distance (d_E = d_L ⇒ VR = 1). For an ideal pulley, MA = 1. It does not multiply force. Its sole function is to change the direction of the lifting effort from awkward upward pulling to convenient downward pulling, allowing the worker to utilise their own body weight.
  • Single Movable Pulley: One end of the rope is anchored to a ceiling beam while the load is suspended from the pulley's axle. The effort pulls upward on the free end. The rope supports the load with two tension segments, so VR = 2. An ideal movable pulley has MA = 2, halving the required effort!
  • Block and Tackle System: A combination of several fixed and movable pulleys. In an ideal block-and-tackle with n total pulleys, VR = n and MA = n. A crane using a 6-pulley block can lift a 6000 N cargo with an effort of just 1000 N (ignoring friction).
3. The Inclined Plane (Ramp)

An inclined plane is a sloping rigid flat surface connecting two elevations. Instead of lifting a heavy refrigerator of weight W = mg vertically up into a truck bed of height h (which requires a massive vertical force equal to mg), the refrigerator is pushed up a smooth wooden ramp of length L.

Since Work = Force × Distance and work against gravity is path-independent (W = mgh):
Effort × Length of Ramp = Load × Height of Ramp
Effort × L = Load × h   ⇒   MA = Load / Effort = L / h = 1 / sin θ

The gentler the slope (longer L relative to h), the greater the mechanical advantage and the smaller the effort required. Examples include wheelchair hospital ramps, highway mountain switchbacks, and moving truck loading ramps.

4. The Wheel and Axle

Consists of two concentric circular cylinders of different diameters rigidly joined together so they rotate as a single unit on the same axis. The larger cylinder is the Wheel (radius R) and the smaller is the Axle (radius r).

Applying an effort to the rim of the large wheel turns the axle through one full revolution: Effort moves a distance 2πR, while the load wound around the axle moves 2πr.
VR = (2πR) / (2πr) = R / r   ⇒   MA = R / r (for ideal machine).
Examples: Screwdriver (thick handle is wheel, thin metal shaft is axle), steering wheel of an automobile, water well windlass, doorknob.

5. The Wedge

A wedge consists of two inclined planes placed back-to-back, tapering to a sharp edge. When a driving force is hammered into the flat blunt end, the wedge translates that forward driving force into enormous perpendicular lateral forces that push sideways to split an object apart.

The thinner and longer the wedge edge, the higher its mechanical advantage. Examples include axes, wood splitting wedges, knife blades, chisels, nails, needles, and animal teeth (incisors and canines).

6. The Screw

A screw is fundamentally an inclined plane wrapped spirally around a central cylindrical core. The raised helical ridge is called the thread. The vertical distance between two adjacent threads is called the pitch (p).

When the head of a screw is rotated through one full 360° turn by a lever or screwdriver handle of radius r, the effort travels a circular circumference of 2πr, while the screw penetrates linearly into the wood by just one pitch distance p.
VR = 2πr / p.
Because pitch p is tiny (often 1 or 2 mm) compared to the turning circumference 2πr, screws possess an enormous mechanical advantage. A single mechanic using a screw jack can easily elevate a 2000 kg automobile using modest hand strength.

NCERT Activity 7.1: Pushing a Heavy Stone vs. Pulling a Wheeled Trolley
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

Scientific work requires two simultaneous conditions: an external force must act (F ≠ 0), and the body must be displaced in a direction non-perpendicular to the force (s ≠ 0, θ ≠ 90°).

Step 2: Experimental Setup

Case A: A student exerts a muscular force of 200 N against a massive stationary stone boulder. The boulder does not move (s = 0 m).
Case B: The same student applies a gentle force of 30 N to a wheeled trolley, moving it smoothly through 5 m along the floor.

Step 3: Formulation

Work done: W = F × s × cos θ.

Step 4: Step-by-Step Execution

Case A (Boulder): W_boulder = 200 N × 0 m = 0 Joules. Despite heavy muscular fatigue and sweating, zero physical work is performed.
Case B (Trolley): Since force and displacement are both forward (θ = 0°):
W_trolley = 30 N × 5 m × cos 0° = 30 × 5 × 1 = +150 Joules.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: Work was done ONLY in Case B (150 J). In Case A, work done was exactly zero.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: In physics examinations, never confuse physiological exhaustion with physical work. Always identify displacement s first!

NCERT Activity 7.2: Lifting a Textbook Vertically Through 1 Metre
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

Work done against gravity involves lifting a mass vertically upward at constant speed against its downward gravitational weight mg.

Step 2: Given Data

Mass of textbook m = 0.5 kg, vertical height h = 1.0 m, acceleration due to gravity g = 9.8 m/s².

Step 3: Formulation

Upward force required: F = mg. Displacement: s = h. Angle θ = 0° (both upward).
W_lift = F × s = m × g × h.

Step 4: Step-by-Step Execution

F = 0.5 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 4.9 N.
W_lift = 4.9 N × 1.0 m = +4.9 Joules.
Concurrently, the work done by gravity during the lift is: W_gravity = mg × h × cos 180° = -4.9 Joules.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: The lifting force does +4.9 J of work, which is stored as gravitational potential energy in the book.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: Always specify WHICH force you are calculating work for: work by the lifting hand (+4.9 J) or work by gravity (-4.9 J).

NCERT Activity 7.3: Work Done by Friction on an Eraser Sliding Across a Desk
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

Frictional force opposes relative motion between contacting surfaces. When force opposes displacement, the angle between them is 180°, resulting in negative work.

Step 2: Experimental Observation

An eraser is flicked across a wooden study desk. It slides forward by a displacement of 0.4 m and comes to rest. The average kinetic frictional force exerted by the tabletop is 0.5 N pointing backward.

Step 3: Formulation

W_friction = f × s × cos 180° = - f × s.

Step 4: Step-by-Step Execution

W_friction = 0.5 N × 0.4 m × (-1) = -0.20 Joules.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: The tabletop friction does -0.20 J of work on the eraser, draining its kinetic energy and dissipating it as microscopic heat.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: Negative work always indicates energy removal from the moving object.

NCERT Activity 7.4: A Heavy Hammer Driving an Iron Nail into Wood
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

Work-Energy interconversion: A raised hammer possesses gravitational potential energy. As it falls, this converts into kinetic energy. Upon striking the nail, the hammer does work against wood resistance.

Step 2: Experimental Comparison

Trial 1: A 1 kg hammer is gently tapped on the nail from a height of 2 cm. The nail barely enters the wood.
Trial 2: The same hammer is raised to a height of 50 cm and struck forcefully downwards with speed. The nail penetrates deep into the wooden block.

Step 3: Formulation

Stored Potential Energy: PE = mgh. Work done in penetration: W = F_resistance × depth (d).

Step 4: Step-by-Step Execution

In Trial 2, the larger elevation h provides significantly greater mechanical energy (PE = 1 kg × 9.8 m/s² × 0.5 m = 4.9 J), transferring substantial kinetic energy to drive the nail against strong wood fibres.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: The capacity of a body to do work is directly proportional to its mechanical energy.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: Energy represents stored work capability. The hammer cannot do more work than the mechanical energy supplied to it.

NCERT Activity 7.5: Dropping Metallic Balls into Dry Sand (Mass vs. Velocity Effect)
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

Kinetic energy depends on both mass and speed (KE = ½mv²). The depth of the depression created in a bed of loose sand indicates the work done in displacing sand grains.

Step 2: Experimental Observation

1. Dropping the same steel ball from heights of 25 cm, 50 cm, and 100 cm creates progressively deeper crater depressions.
2. Dropping a light wooden ball vs. a dense lead ball of equal diameter from the same height shows the heavier lead ball creates a much deeper depression.

Step 3: Formulation

Speed on impact: v = √(2gh). Kinetic energy: KE = ½mv² = mgh. Work to deform sand: W = F_sand × depth.

Step 4: Step-by-Step Execution

Greater drop height increases impact velocity (v ∝ √h), increasing KE linearly with height. Greater mass increases KE linearly (KE ∝ m). Deeper penetration proves greater work capability.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: Kinetic energy is directly proportional to mass and directly proportional to the square of velocity.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: Because velocity is squared in KE = ½mv², doubling velocity has 4 times the impact on kinetic energy compared to doubling mass.

NCERT Activity 7.6: Drawn Archer's Bow and Arrow (Elastic Potential Energy Transfer)
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

Muscular work deforms an elastic body, storing elastic potential energy. Upon release, this stored potential energy converts into kinetic energy of the projectile.

Step 2: Experimental Observation

A bamboo bow with an arrow rested on its string. When simply placed on the string, the arrow falls to the ground. When the string is pulled backward bending the bow stave and released, the arrow shoots forward at high speed over great distance.

NCERT Box Question 1: When do we say that work is done?
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

A spacecraft (such as NASA's Voyager 1) travelling in deep interstellar space far away from any stars or planets. Once its rocket engines are turned off, zero net force acts on it (F = 0), yet it covers millions of kilometres of displacement every day due to its own inertia of motion.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: A force is required to change velocity (produce acceleration), but NO force is needed to maintain a constant velocity.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: Aristotle erroneously believed that force is required to sustain motion; Galileo and Newton proved that force is only needed to alter motion.

NCERT Exercise 15: Illustrate conservation of energy in an oscillating pendulum. Why does it stop? Is it a violation?
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

The Law of Conservation of Energy: Total energy of a closed system is constant (E = KE + PE + E_thermal = Constant).

Step 2: Oscillation Energy Cycle

1. At Extreme Position A: The bob is momentarily stationary (v = 0 ⇒ KE = 0). It is elevated by height h, possessing pure potential energy: E = PE = mgh.
2. Swinging Downward to Mean Position O: Height decreases and speed increases. Potential energy transforms continuously into kinetic energy.
3. At Mean Position O: Height h = 0 ⇒ PE = 0. Speed is maximum (v = √(2gh)). All energy is kinetic: E = KE = ½mv² = mgh.
4. Swinging Upward to Extreme B: Kinetic energy transforms back into potential energy until the bob halts at height h with PE = mgh.

Step 3: Why Does the Bob Stop?

In a real atmosphere, the swinging bob experiences two non-conservative resistive forces:
(i) Viscous air drag as the bob pushes air molecules aside.
(ii) Frictional resistance at the pivot point where the thread bends.
During every oscillation, the bob does mechanical work against these frictional forces. This work progressively drains mechanical energy and converts it into microscopic thermal energy (heat) in the surrounding air and thread, along with minute sound energy.

Step 4: Is Conservation of Energy Violated?

NO, it is NOT a violation! When the pendulum stops, its initial mechanical energy (mgh) has not vanished into nothingness; it has been entirely dispersed into the surrounding air and support as thermal energy. The total energy of the universe remains strictly constant.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: Energy transforms between PE and KE during oscillation, and finally into heat/sound energy. The Law of Conservation of Energy remains 100% valid.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: Clearly distinguish between mechanical energy (which decreases) and total energy (which is conserved).

NCERT Exercise 16: An object of mass m moves with constant velocity v. How much work should be done on it to bring it to rest?
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

The Work-Energy Theorem: Net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy (W = KE_final - KE_initial).

Step 2: Initial and Final States

Mass of the object = m.
Initial velocity = v ⇒ Initial Kinetic Energy = ½ m v².
Final state is at rest ⇒ Final velocity = 0 ⇒ Final Kinetic Energy = ½ m (0)² = 0.

Step 3: Formulation

Work done W = KE_final - KE_initial = 0 - ½ m v² = - ½ m v².

Step 4: Physical Interpretation

The negative sign signifies that a retarding or resistive force (such as friction or braking force) must act in the opposite direction to the object's displacement to absorb its kinetic energy.
The magnitude of work required is ½ m v².

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: The work that must be done on the object is - ½ m v² (or an opposing work of magnitude ½ m v²).
Teacher's Pro-Tip: State both: "Work done on the object = -½mv²" and "Work done by the retarding force has magnitude ½mv²".

NCERT Exercise 19: Soni says acceleration could be zero even when several forces act on it. Do you agree? Why?
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

Newton's Second Law of Motion: Acceleration is produced by the net resultant external force (F_net = m × a).

Step 2: Direct Agreement

YES, we completely agree with Soni.

Step 3: Physical Justification

Acceleration depends on the vector sum of all individual forces acting on the body:
F_net = F₁ + F₂ + F₃ + ... + F_n.
If multiple forces act on an object such that their vector sum equals zero (balanced forces), then:
F_net = 0 ⇒ m × a = 0 ⇒ a = 0.

Step 4: Real-Life Examples

1. Tug of War: Two teams pull a rope in opposite directions with equal 500 N forces. Two massive forces act on the rope, but the net force is zero and acceleration is zero.
2. Book Resting on a Table: Downward gravitational force W = mg is balanced by the upward normal reaction force R of the table. Net force is zero, so acceleration is zero.
3. Car Cruising at Constant Speed: Forward engine tractive force exactly balances opposing air resistance and tyre friction. Net force is zero; car moves at steady velocity with zero acceleration.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: Soni is completely correct because balanced forces produce zero resultant force and consequently zero acceleration.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: Emphasize the concept of "balanced forces" in your written answer.

NCERT Exercise 21: A freely falling object eventually stops on reaching the ground. What happens to its kinetic energy?
Step 1: Core Concept / Law

The Law of Conservation of Energy: Kinetic energy cannot be destroyed; upon impact, it is transformed into other non-mechanical forms.

Step 2: Impact Dynamics

Just before hitting the ground, the object possesses maximum kinetic energy: KE = ½ m v² = mgH.
Upon collision with the rigid ground, the object decelerates to rest in a fraction of a second (v → 0 ⇒ KE → 0).

Step 3: Energy Transformation on Impact

The kinetic energy of the falling object is completely transformed into the following forms: 1. Thermal Energy (Heat): The rapid compression of the object and the ground generates substantial heat, raising the temperature of both the object and the contact soil.
2. Acoustic (Sound) Energy: The sudden air pressure wave generated by the collision is heard as a loud "thud" or crash sound.
3. Deformation / Strain Work: Work is done to deform, dent, crack, or break the falling body or crater the ground surface.
4. Seismic Mechanical Waves: A small pulse of elastic kinetic energy travels through the Earth as a ground vibration.

Step 4: Total Energy Ledger

Initial Kinetic Energy = Heat Energy + Sound Energy + Work of Permanent Deformation + Ground Vibration.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Pro-Tip

Conclusion: The kinetic energy is completely converted into heat energy, sound energy, and work of physical deformation.
Teacher's Pro-Tip: Never write "energy is lost"; write "kinetic energy is transformed into thermal, acoustic, and deformation energy".

LAQ 1 (5M): State Law of Conservation of Energy. Analytically prove conservation in free fall at Points A, B, and C. Draw variation graph.
Marking Scheme Breakdown

• Statement of Law of Conservation of Energy: [1 Mark]
• Derivation of Total Energy at Point A: [1 Mark]
• Derivation of Total Energy at Point B: [1 Mark]
• Derivation of Total Energy at Point C: [1 Mark]
• Graphical representation and conclusion: [1 Mark] (Total = 5 Marks)

Part 1: Statement of the Law

The Law of Conservation of Energy states that energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form into another. The total mechanical energy of an isolated system remains constant.

Part 2: Analytical Derivations at Points A, B, and C

Consider a body of mass m released from rest at height H above ground:
1. At Point A (Top, Height H):
Initial velocity u = 0 ⇒ KE_A = 0.
Height above ground = H ⇒ PE_A = mgH.
Total Energy E_A = KE_A + PE_A = 0 + mgH = mgH   [Equation 1]

2. At Point B (After falling vertical distance x, Height h_B = H - x):
Potential Energy: PE_B = mg(H - x) = mgH - mgx.
Velocity at B from v_B² - 0 = 2gx ⇒ v_B² = 2gx.
Kinetic Energy: KE_B = ½ m v_B² = ½ m (2gx) = mgx.
Total Energy E_B = KE_B + PE_B = mgx + (mgH - mgx) = mgH   [Equation 2]

3. At Point C (Just before striking ground, Height h_C = 0):
Height = 0 ⇒ PE_C = mg(0) = 0.
Velocity at C from v_C² - 0 = 2gH ⇒ v_C² = 2gH.
Kinetic Energy: KE_C = ½ m v_C² = ½ m (2gH) = mgH.
Total Energy E_C = KE_C + PE_C = mgH + 0 = mgH   [Equation 3]

Part 3: Conclusion & Variation Graph

From Equations 1, 2, and 3: E_A = E_B = E_C = mgH = Constant.
As height decreases linearly from H to 0, PE declines linearly from mgH to 0, while KE increases linearly from 0 to mgH. The sum (E_total) is represented by a horizontal line at E = mgH (refer to Figure 7.3 graph).

Teacher's Pro-Tip: In examinations, neatly box each intermediate result: E_A = mgH, E_B = mgH, and E_C = mgH to make it effortless for the examiner to award full marks!

LAQ 2 (5M): (a) Derive KE = ½mv² algebraically. (3M) (b) A 20 g bullet at 400 m/s penetrates 10 cm of wood. Find resistive force. (2M)
Marking Scheme Breakdown

• (a) Algebraic derivation of KE = ½mv² from equations of motion: [3 Marks]
• (b) Numerical solution with unit conversions: [2 Marks] (Total = 5 Marks)

Part (a): Derivation of Kinetic Energy (3 Marks)

1. Let an object of mass m be initially at rest on a frictionless surface (u = 0).
2. A constant force F accelerates it with uniform acceleration a over displacement s, reaching final velocity v.
3. By Newton's second law: F = m × a.
4. From third equation of motion: v² - u² = 2as ⇒ v² - 0 = 2as ⇒ s = v² / (2a).
5. Work done W = F × s = (m × a) × [v² / (2a)] = ½ m v².
6. Since work done equals kinetic energy acquired from rest: KE = ½ m v².

Part (b): Bullet Resistance Numerical (2 Marks)

Given: Mass m = 20 g = 20 / 1000 kg = 0.02 kg.
Initial velocity u = 400 m/s; Final velocity v = 0 m/s.
Penetration distance s = 10 cm = 0.10 m.

Step 1: Calculate Initial Kinetic Energy of Bullet:
KE = ½ m u² = ½ × 0.02 kg × (400 m/s)² = 0.01 × 160,000 = 1600 Joules.

Step 2: Apply Work-Energy Theorem:
Work done by wood resistance = - KE = - 1600 Joules.
Since W = - F_resistive × s:
- F_resistive × 0.10 m = - 1600 J
F_resistive = 1600 / 0.10 = 16,000 Newtons (16 kN).

Conclusion

The average resistive force exerted by the wooden block on the bullet is 16,000 Newtons (or 16 kN).

Teacher's Pro-Tip

Teacher's Pro-Tip: Always remember to convert grams to kilograms (divide by 1000) and centimetres to metres (divide by 100)!

LAQ 3 (5M): Define Simple Machine, MA, VR, and Efficiency. Derive η = MA/VR. Why is practical efficiency < 100%?
Marking Scheme Breakdown

• Definitions of Simple Machine, MA, and VR: [2 Marks]
• Derivation of Efficiency relation η = MA / VR: [2 Marks]
• Physical justification why practical efficiency is strictly < 100%: [1 Mark] (Total = 5 Marks)

Part 1: Definitions

1. Simple Machine: A mechanical device that modifies the magnitude, direction, or speed of an applied force to facilitate work.
2. Mechanical Advantage (MA): The ratio of the resistive Load (L) to the applied Effort (E): MA = Load / Effort = L / E.
3. Velocity Ratio (VR): The ratio of the distance moved by the effort (d_E) to the distance moved by the load (d_L): VR = d_E / d_L.

Part 2: Derivation of η = MA / VR

1. Useful Work Output by machine = Load × Distance moved by Load = L × d_L.
2. Total Work Input supplied to machine = Effort × Distance moved by Effort = E × d_E.
3. By definition, mechanical efficiency is:
η = (Work Output / Work Input) × 100%
η = [(L × d_L) / (E × d_E)] = (L / E) / (d_E / d_L).
4. Substituting L / E = MA and d_E / d_L = VR:
η = (MA / VR) × 100%   ⇒   MA = η × VR.

Part 3: Why is Practical Efficiency Always Less Than 100%?

In any practical real-world machine, η < 100% due to three unavoidable mechanical factors: 1. Frictional Losses: Friction between rotating axles, sliding surfaces, and bearing pivots transforms a portion of input mechanical work into wasted thermal and acoustic energy.
2. Mass of Moving Parts: Additional effort must be expended simply to lift the moving parts of the machine itself (e.g., the weight of the lower movable pulley block or heavy crowbar).
3. Imperfect Rigidity: Real strings and cables are not perfectly weightless or infinitely flexible, introducing bending resistance.
Consequently: Work Output < Work Input, meaning MA is always strictly less than VR.

Teacher's Pro-Tip

Teacher's Pro-Tip: State clearly: "Velocity ratio VR is purely geometric and independent of friction, whereas MA decreases when friction is present".

5.5 Competency-Based Case Studies (Real-World Applied Science)

Case Study 1: The Physics of Hydroelectric Power Generation (Hirakud Dam, Odisha)

Contextual Narrative: The Hirakud Dam on the Mahanadi River in Sambalpur, Odisha, is one of the longest earthen dams in the world. Water stored in the massive reservoir at an effective hydraulic head (height) of H = 30 metres is released through large steel conduits called penstocks at a massive rate of 40,000 kg per second (40 tonnes/s). The high-velocity water jets blast against the curved curved buckets of giant hydro-turbines, which spin generators to produce electric power for the state grid.

Questions:
1. Describe the continuous chain of energy transformations from reservoir to transmission lines.
2. Calculate the total gravitational potential energy lost by 40,000 kg of water falling through 30 m in 1 second (Take g = 9.8 m/s²).
3. If the combined turbine-generator efficiency is 85%, calculate the actual electrical power generated in Megawatts (MW).

Comprehensive Solutions:
1. Energy Transformation Chain:
Gravitational Potential Energy (Reservoir Water) → Kinetic Energy (High-Velocity Water in Penstocks) → Rotational Mechanical Energy (Turbine Runner) → Electrical Energy (Electromagnetic Induction in Generator Stator).

2. Potential Energy Lost Per Second:
PE = m × g × H = 40,000 kg × 9.8 m/s² × 30 m = 11,760,000 Joules = 1.176 × 10⁷ Joules.
Therefore, theoretical input power available from falling water is: P_in = 11.76 Megawatts (MW).

3. Actual Electrical Power Generated:
P_out = Efficiency (η) × P_in = 0.85 × 11.76 MW = 9.996 MW ≈ 10.0 Megawatts.
The remaining 15% (1.764 MW) is dissipated as fluid turbulence, friction, and heat in the tailrace water.

Case Study 2: Biomechanics: The Human Musculoskeletal System as Simple Machines

Contextual Narrative: The human skeletal system is a masterpiece of applied mechanical engineering. Bones act as rigid lever beams, synovial joints function as low-friction fulcrums, skeletal muscles provide the input effort through contracting tendon attachments, and the body's own weight or an external object represents the load. An athlete lifting a dumbbell or a runner sprinting off starting blocks utilizes all three classical classes of levers.

Questions:
1. Identify the class of lever operating when: (a) Nodding the head forward and backward, (b) Rising onto tiptoes while running, (c) Curling a dumbbell using the biceps muscle.
2. In the human forearm (Class 3 lever), the biceps tendon attaches just 4 cm from the elbow joint, while the hand holding a 5 kg dumbbell is 32 cm from the elbow. Calculate the mechanical advantage (MA) and the upward muscular force the biceps must generate to hold the 5 kg dumbbell stationary (Take g = 10 m/s²).
3. Why did biological evolution favor Class 3 levers (where MA < 1) throughout the human limbs instead of Class 2 levers (force multipliers)?

Comprehensive Solutions:
1. Anatomical Lever Classification:
(a) Nodding the head: Class 1 Lever (Fulcrum at atlanto-occipital joint between facial load and neck muscle effort).
(b) Rising on tiptoes: Class 2 Lever (Fulcrum at ball of foot, downward body weight through tibia is load in the middle, upward calf gastrocnemius tendon is effort).
(c) Curling dumbbell: Class 3 Lever (Fulcrum at elbow joint, biceps effort attached in the middle, load in the hand at the far end).

2. Mathematical Biomechanical Calculation:
Load L = mg = 5 kg × 10 m/s² = 50 N.
Load arm d_L = 32 cm = 0.32 m; Effort arm d_E = 4 cm = 0.04 m.
MA = Effort Arm / Load Arm = 4 cm / 32 cm = 1 / 8 = 0.125.
By Principle of Moments: Effort × d_E = Load × d_L
Effort E = (50 N × 32 cm) / 4 cm = 50 × 8 = 400 Newtons!
The biceps must exert 400 N of muscular tension just to support a modest 50 N dumbbell!

3. Evolutionary Advantage:
Although Class 3 levers require 8 times greater muscular force (MA = 0.125), they act as exceptional speed and distance multipliers (Velocity Ratio = 0.125)! A tiny 1 cm contraction of the biceps muscle produces an 8 cm rapid sweep of the hand! This gave primitive humans the high limb speed needed to throw spears, swing clubs, and run with agility.

CBSE 2023 PYQ: A crane lifts a 1200 kg vehicle vertically up by 15 m in 30 s. Find Work done and Power in kW and hp (g = 10 m/s²).

Step 1: Given Data
Mass m = 1200 kg, height h = 15 m, time t = 30 s, g = 10 m/s².

Step 2: Calculate Work Done Against Gravity
W = m × g × h = 1200 kg × 10 m/s² × 15 m = 180,000 Joules (180 kJ).

Step 3: Calculate Power in Watts and kW
Power P = W / t = 180,000 J / 30 s = 6,000 Watts = 6.0 kW.

Step 4: Convert Power to Horsepower (hp)
Since 1 hp = 746 Watts:
P (hp) = 6000 / 746 ≈ 8.04 hp.

Final Conclusion: Work done = 1.8 × 10⁵ Joules; Power = 6.0 kW (or 8.04 hp).

CBSE 2022 PYQ: Two bodies m₁ and m₂ have equal linear momentum. Derive KE ratio. Which body has greater KE if m₁ > m₂?

Step 1: Formula Linking KE and Linear Momentum
We know: KE = p² / (2m).

Step 2: Formulation for Both Bodies
Let both bodies have equal momentum p₁ = p₂ = p.
KE₁ = p² / (2m₁)   and   KE₂ = p² / (2m₂).

Step 3: Calculating Ratio KE₁ / KE₂
KE₁ / KE₂ = [p² / (2m₁)] / [p² / (2m₂)] = m₂ / m₁.

Step 4: Comparison when m₁ > m₂
Since m₁ > m₂, the ratio m₂ / m₁ < 1.
Therefore: KE₁ / KE₂ < 1 ⇒ KE₁ < KE₂.

Final Conclusion: The ratio of their kinetic energies is m₂ : m₁. The lighter body (m₂) has greater kinetic energy!

CBSE 2021 PYQ: A boy of mass 50 kg runs up 45 steps in 9 seconds. Height of each step is 15 cm. Find his power (g = 10 m/s²).

Step 1: Given Data
Mass of boy m = 50 kg.
Number of steps = 45; Height of each step = 15 cm = 0.15 m.
Total vertical height h = 45 × 0.15 m = 6.75 metres.
Time taken t = 9 seconds; g = 10 m/s².

Step 2: Calculate Work Done Against Gravity
W = m × g × h = 50 kg × 10 m/s² × 6.75 m = 3375 Joules.

Step 3: Calculate Power Delivered
P = W / t = 3375 J / 9 s = 375 Watts.

Final Conclusion: The power delivered by the boy is 375 Watts.

CBSE 2020 PYQ: Pendulum bob 100 g is raised 20 cm vertically and released. Find PE at highest point and speed at lowest point (g = 9.8 m/s²).

Step 1: Given Data & Unit Conversion
Mass m = 100 g = 0.10 kg.
Vertical height h = 20 cm = 0.20 m.
g = 9.8 m/s².

Step 2: Potential Energy at Highest Point
PE_max = m × g × h = 0.10 kg × 9.8 m/s² × 0.20 m = 0.196 Joules.

Step 3: Speed at Lowest Point (Conservation of Energy)
At lowest point, all PE converts to KE: ½ m v² = PE_max = 0.196 J.
½ × 0.10 × v² = 0.196 ⇒ 0.05 × v² = 0.196 ⇒ v² = 0.196 / 0.05 = 3.92.
v = √(3.92) ≈ 1.98 m/s.
(Direct formula verification: v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 0.20) = √(3.92) ≈ 1.98 m/s).

Final Conclusion: PE at highest point = 0.196 Joules; Speed at lowest point = 1.98 m/s.

CBSE 2019 PYQ: 5 kg block slides down 10 m ramp of height 6 m, reaching bottom at 8 m/s. Work done against friction? (g = 10 m/s²).

Step 1: Given Data
Mass m = 5 kg.
Ramp length L = 10 m; Vertical height h = 6 m.
Initial velocity at top u = 0 m/s; Final velocity at bottom v = 8 m/s.
g = 10 m/s².

Step 2: Calculate Total Initial Mechanical Energy at Top
E_top = PE_top = m × g × h = 5 kg × 10 m/s² × 6 m = 300 Joules.

Step 3: Calculate Actual Kinetic Energy at Bottom
KE_bottom = ½ m v² = ½ × 5 kg × (8 m/s)² = 2.5 × 64 = 160 Joules.

Step 4: Apply Conservation of Energy with Friction
In the presence of friction:
Initial Energy = Final Mechanical Energy + Work done against Friction (W_friction)
300 J = 160 J + W_friction
W_friction = 300 J - 160 J = 140 Joules.

Final Conclusion: The work done against friction during the slide is 140 Joules (which was converted into heat along the ramp surface).

6.1 Exam Hall Trap Alerts (The 5 Most Dangerous Student Blunders)

Over 80% of marks lost by Class 9 students in Physics Chapter 7 are not due to lack of study, but due to these five recurring conceptual and calculation traps:

Blunder 1: Using W = F × s Blindly Without Verifying the Angle θ The Mistake: When asked to calculate the work done by a porter carrying a 50 kg trunk on a flat platform, students automatically multiply m × g × s = 50 × 9.8 × 20 = 9800 J.
The Correction: Always draw the force and displacement vectors! Gravity points vertically down; platform displacement is horizontal (θ = 90°). Since cos 90° = 0, work done by gravity is strictly ZERO Joules!
Blunder 2: Confusing Power with Energy in Descriptive Statements The Mistake: Writing statements like "a 1000 W electric kettle consumed 1000 W of energy in an hour".
The Correction: Watts (W) is a rate (Joules per second), NOT an amount of energy! A 1000 W kettle consumes energy at the rate of 1000 Joules every second. In 1 hour, it consumes 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ Joules or exactly 1 kWh of energy.
Blunder 3: Forgetting to Convert Velocity from km/h to m/s Before Squaring The Mistake: In KE or stopping work questions with velocity given as 72 km/h, students directly calculate KE = ½ × 1000 × (72)².
The Correction: In the SI system, velocity must ALWAYS be in metres per second (m/s). Always multiply km/h by 5/18 first: 72 × (5/18) = 20 m/s. Squaring 72 instead of 20 yields an answer that is (18/5)² = 12.96 times too high!
Blunder 4: Attaching Units to Mechanical Advantage (MA) or Velocity Ratio (VR) The Mistake: Writing "Mechanical Advantage = 4 Newtons" or "Velocity Ratio = 5 metres".
The Correction: MA is the ratio of two forces (Newtons / Newtons = 1), and VR is the ratio of two distances (metres / metres = 1). Both are pure dimensionless numbers with NO UNITS. Writing units will cost you half a mark.
Blunder 5: Assuming Mechanical Energy is Always Conserved in the Presence of Friction The Mistake: Equating initial mechanical energy directly to final mechanical energy for objects sliding down real rough ramps or oscillating in air.
The Correction: Mechanical energy (KE + PE) is conserved ONLY in the ideal absence of non-conservative forces. In the real world, friction transforms mechanical energy into heat and sound: Initial Mechanical Energy = Final Mechanical Energy + Work Done against Friction!

6.2 The 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Revise these fifteen core takeaways in the final 10 minutes before entering your exam hall:

1
Two Conditions for Work: Scientific work requires both a non-zero external force (F ≠ 0) and non-zero non-perpendicular displacement (s ≠ 0, θ ≠ 90°).
2
Work Formula: W = F × s × cos θ. Collinear forward force gives W = +F·s (positive); opposing force gives W = -F·s (negative).
3
1 Joule Definition: Work done when a force of 1 N displaces an object by 1 m along its direction. 1 J = 1 N·m = 10⁷ ergs.
4
Perpendicular Force = Zero Work: If θ = 90°, cos 90° = 0 ⇒ W = 0. Examples: Porter walking horizontally with head luggage; satellite orbiting Earth.
5
Centripetal Force Work is Zero: Centripetal force acts radially inward perpendicular to instantaneous tangential velocity, doing zero work on the revolving mass.
6
Kinetic Energy: Energy of motion: KE = ½ m v². Always positive or zero; directly proportional to mass and the square of speed.
7
Work-Energy Theorem: Net work done by all forces equals the change in kinetic energy: W_net = KE_final - KE_initial = ½m(v² - u²).
8
Momentum vs. KE: KE = p² / (2m). For bodies with equal KE, the heavier mass has greater momentum (p = √(2m·KE)).
9
Gravitational Potential Energy: PE = m g h. Stored work against gravity; independent of the path taken (conservative field).
10
Elastic Potential Energy: Stored in deformed elastic bodies (stretched spring): PE_elastic = ½ k x², where k is spring constant.
11
Conservation of Energy in Free Fall: Total mechanical energy E = KE + PE = mgH = Constant at all heights during descent.
12
Power & Units: Rate of doing work: P = W / t. SI unit is Watt (1 W = 1 J/s). 1 kW = 1000 W; 1 Horsepower (hp) = 746 W.
13
Commercial Unit (kWh): 1 kWh = 1 Unit = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ Joules (3.6 Megajoules).
14
Simple Machines Relations: MA = Load / Effort, VR = d_E / d_L, Efficiency η = (MA / VR) × 100%. Real machines always have η < 100% and MA < VR.
15
The FLE Mnemonic for Levers: F in center = Class 1 (see-saw); L in center = Class 2 (wheelbarrow, MA > 1 always); E in center = Class 3 (tongs, forearm, MA < 1 always).
