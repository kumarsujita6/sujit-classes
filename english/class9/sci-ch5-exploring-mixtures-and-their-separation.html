2.1 Purity: Everyday Perception vs Scientific Reality

When an ordinary shopper buys a packet of milk, ghee, honey, or salt marked "100% Pure" from a grocery store in Cuttack or Bhubaneswar, what does "pure" mean to them? To a consumer, "pure" simply means that the food is unadulterated—free from harmful chemicals, artificial additives, dirt, or foreign dilution (like water added to milk).

Scientific Shock: Milk is NOT Pure Matter! To a chemist or secondary science student, fresh cow milk is NOT a pure substance at all. Milk is actually a complex colloidal mixture of liquid water (~87%), liquid butterfat globules, solid proteins (casein), lactose sugars, and dissolved mineral salts. Since it contains multiple chemically distinct entities, science classifies it as a mixture!

In the strict language of chemistry, a Pure Substance (ବିଶୁଦ୍ଧ ପଦାର୍ଥ) is defined by two inviolable criteria:

  1. It consists of only one single type of constituent particle (atoms or molecules). Every single particle in the sample has an identical chemical nature.
  2. It exhibits a fixed, invariant chemical composition and distinct physical properties (sharp melting point, sharp boiling point, and constant density) throughout its entire bulk, regardless of its geographic source or method of preparation.
Core Scientific Definition: Pure Substance A pure substance is a homogeneous form of matter that consists of a single type of constituent particles (all atoms or all molecules having identical chemical composition) and cannot be separated into other kinds of matter by any physical process. Examples include pure distilled water (H₂O), pure cane sugar (sucrose, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁), common salt (sodium chloride, NaCl), pure gold (Au), and oxygen gas (O₂).

2.2 What is a Mixture? (ମିଶ୍ରଣ)

If you take a teaspoon of common salt (NaCl) and dissolve it into a glass of pure water (H₂O), or mix sand with iron nails, you have formed a mixture. Almost all materials we touch, drink, and breathe in nature—the atmospheric air, soil, river water, seawater, rocks, brass door handles, and fruit juice—are mixtures.

Core Definition: Mixture A mixture is an impure substance consisting of two or more pure substances (elements or compounds) physically blended together in any arbitrary proportion, without undergoing any chemical combination or chemical reaction.

Five Fundamental Characteristics of Mixtures

  • 1. Variable Composition: The constituent substances can be mixed together in any proportion by mass. For instance, you can make a salt solution by adding 2 g, 10 g, or 35 g of salt into 100 g of water. There is no rigid mathematical ratio required.
  • 2. Retention of Individual Properties: The constituent substances do not lose their unique chemical identities. In a mixture of iron filings and yellow sulfur powder, the iron remains magnetic, and the sulfur remains soluble in carbon disulfide (CS₂).
  • 3. Physical Separability: Because the components are held together only by weak intermolecular contacts and not by chemical bonds, they can be separated into pure substances using simple physical separation techniques (filtration, evaporation, distillation, magnetic attraction, sublimation, etc.).
  • 4. Variable Melting & Boiling Points: A mixture does not possess a sharp, distinct melting or boiling point. Instead, it melts or boils across a wide temperature range depending on the relative concentrations of its components.
  • 5. No Net Chemical Energy Exchange: When a mixture is prepared, usually no significant chemical heat, light, or electrical energy is evolved or absorbed (apart from small physical heats of solution).

2.3 Homogeneous vs Heterogeneous Mixtures

Depending on how uniformly the constituent particles disperse among each other and whether distinct physical boundaries can be detected, mixtures are classified into two broad categories:

Homogeneous Mixtures (ସମଜାତୀୟ ମିଶ୍ରଣ)

Definition: A mixture in which the constituent substances are completely mixed and have a completely uniform composition and uniform physical properties throughout its entire volume.

  • Single Phase System: It consists of only one single physical phase (solid, liquid, or gas).
  • No Phase Boundaries: There are zero visible boundaries of separation between the different components, even under an optical microscope.
  • Uniform Sampling: Every small droplet or sample taken from any corner of the mixture has the exact same taste, color, density, and chemical concentration.
  • Representative Examples:
    • Sugar dissolved completely in pure water
    • Common salt (NaCl) dissolved in water (brine)
    • Clean, filtered atmospheric air (N₂, O₂, Ar, CO₂)
    • Vinegar (dilute aqueous acetic acid)
    • Solid metallic alloys like Brass (Copper + Zinc)

Heterogeneous Mixtures (ବିଷମଜାତୀୟ ମିଶ୍ରଣ)

Definition: A mixture that does not have a uniform composition throughout and contains physically distinct parts with visible boundaries of separation.

  • Multi-Phase System: It consists of two or more distinct physical phases existing simultaneously side by side.
  • Visible Phase Boundaries: Distinct boundaries of separation can be seen either with the unaided eye or under a standard microscope.
  • Non-Uniform Sampling: Samples taken from different spots of the container have completely different proportions of the constituent components.
  • Representative Examples:
    • Mixture of sand and water
    • Mixture of common salt and iron filings
    • Oil poured over water (two immiscible liquid layers)
    • Chalk powder stirred in water
    • Muddy pond water and smoke from a chimney

2.4 Solutions (True Solutions / ଦ୍ରବଣ)

When you dissolve a spoonful of sugar in water and stir thoroughly, the white sugar crystals seem to completely vanish. Where did the sugar go? The solid sugar broken down into individual molecules (sucrose molecules) which slipped into the tiny, empty spaces between the water molecules. You have created a Solution (True Solution).

Core Definition: True Solution A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more chemically non-reacting substances whose constituent particles (atoms, ions, or molecules) have dimensions smaller than 1 nanometer (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m) in diameter.

The Two Fundamental Components of a Solution

Every binary solution consists of two designated parts:

  • 1. Solvent (ଦ୍ରାବକ): The component of a solution that dissolves the other component. It is typically the component present in the larger quantity (the continuous dispersing medium). In an aqueous salt solution, water is the solvent. Water is often called the "Universal Solvent" because it dissolves an astonishing variety of substances.
  • 2. Solute (ଦ୍ରାବ): The component of the solution that is dissolved by the solvent. It is typically present in the lesser quantity. In the salt solution, salt is the solute.

The Broad Spectrum of Solutions (Not Just Liquids!)

Most young students mistakenly assume that all solutions are liquids. In chemistry, solutions exist across all three states of matter:

Type of Solution Solute State Solvent State Real-Life Concrete Examples Key Scientific Feature
Solid in Liquid Solid Liquid Common salt in water; Sugar in water; Tincture of Iodine (I₂ solid dissolved in ethyl alcohol liquid). Tincture of iodine is a powerful antiseptic used on wounds.
Liquid in Liquid Liquid Liquid Vinegar (5–8% acetic acid in water); Alcohol in water. Both liquids are fully miscible at molecular scale.
Gas in Liquid Gas Liquid Aerated soft drinks (carbon dioxide CO₂ gas dissolved in water under high pressure); Dissolved O₂ in water. Dissolved oxygen supports aquatic fish breathing through gills.
Gas in Gas Gas Gas Atmospheric Air: ~78% Nitrogen (solvent), ~21% Oxygen (solute), along with Argon (0.93%) and Carbon dioxide (0.04%). Homogeneous gas mixture where all gases mix completely.
Solid in Solid Solid Solid Alloys (ମିଶ୍ରଧାତୁ): Brass (70% Copper + 30% Zinc); Bronze (90% Copper + 10% Tin); Stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni + C). Homogeneous metallic mixtures with modified strength and anti-corrosion properties.
High-Yield CBSE Question: Why are Alloys Classified as Mixtures? Question: Why is an alloy (like Brass) classified as a mixture, even though its constituents cannot be separated by physical filtration and it looks completely uniform?
Answer: An alloy is classified as a mixture because of two decisive chemical reasons:
  1. It shows the individual properties of its constituent metals (brass retains the electrical conductivity of copper and the mechanical durability of zinc).
  2. It can have a variable composition (different grades of brass can have 60% Cu + 40% Zn, or 70% Cu + 30% Zn, or 80% Cu + 20% Zn; it is not chemically fixed by a strict chemical formula).

Five Invariable Properties of a True Solution

  • 1. Strict Homogeneity: It is a homogeneous mixture. The solute particles distribute themselves evenly among the solvent molecules.
  • 2. Sub-nanometer Particle Size: The diameter of the solute particles is less than 1 nanometer (10⁻⁹ m or 10 Å). They cannot be seen even with the most powerful optical microscopes.
  • 3. No Light Scattering (Absence of Tyndall Effect): Because the solute particles are so unimaginably tiny, they cannot intercept or scatter a beam of visible light. Therefore, the path of a light beam passing through a true solution is completely invisible.
  • 4. High Kinetic Stability: The solute particles never settle down under gravity, even if the solution is left undisturbed for weeks or months.
  • 5. Perfect Filterability: Solute particles easily pass right through the microscopic pores of standard filter paper. A true solution cannot be separated by filtration.

2.5 Concentration of Solutions (ଦ୍ରବଣର ଗାଢ଼ତା)

If two students, Rohan and Priya, prepare a copper sulfate solution in their school laboratory: Rohan dissolves 5 g of blue copper sulfate in 100 mL of water, yielding a faint, light blue liquid. Priya dissolves 30 g in 100 mL of water, yielding an intense, dark blue liquid. Both are true solutions, but they differ drastically in their concentration.

Dilute vs Concentrated

Dilute Solution (ଲଘୁ ଦ୍ରବଣ): Contains a relatively small quantity of solute dissolved in a large quantity of solvent (e.g., Rohan's faint blue solution).

Concentrated Solution (ଗାଢ଼ ଦ୍ରବଣ): Contains a relatively large quantity of solute dissolved in a given amount of solvent (e.g., Priya's dark blue solution).

Note: "Dilute" and "concentrated" are comparative, qualitative terms.

Saturated vs Unsaturated

Saturated Solution (ସନ୍ତୃପ୍ତ ଦ୍ରବଣ): A solution in which no more solute can be dissolved at that specific, constant temperature.

Unsaturated Solution (ଅସନ୍ତୃପ୍ତ ଦ୍ରବଣ): A solution containing less solute than its maximum dissolving capacity at that temperature (more solute can easily dissolve).

Supersaturated Solution (ଅତିସନ୍ତୃପ୍ତ ଦ୍ରବଣ): Contains more dissolved solute than a saturated solution at that temperature (metastable state created by slow cooling).

What is Solubility? (ଦ୍ରାବ୍ୟତା) The solubility of a solute is the maximum amount of the solute (in grams) that can dissolve in 100 grams of a solvent at a specific specified temperature to form a saturated solution.
Effect of Temperature:
  • For most solid solutes (e.g., KNO₃, NaCl, sugar), solubility increases with an increase in temperature. As temperature rises, solvent molecules gain kinetic energy and create larger intermolecular spaces. If a hot saturated solution is cooled down, the excess solute precipitates out as pure solid crystals!
  • For gaseous solutes (e.g., O₂, CO₂ in water), solubility decreases with an increase in temperature. Warming soda water causes CO₂ bubbles to violently escape.

Mathematical Expressions of Concentration

In quantitative science, the concentration of a solution is defined as the amount of solute present in a given amount (mass or volume) of solution, or the amount of solute dissolved in a given mass or volume of solvent.

1. Mass by Mass Percentage (w/w %):
Concentration % = [ Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution ] × 100
Remember: Mass of Solution = Mass of Solute + Mass of Solvent
2. Mass by Volume Percentage (w/v %):
Concentration % = [ Mass of Solute in grams ÷ Volume of Solution in mL ] × 100
3. Volume by Volume Percentage (v/v %):
Concentration % = [ Volume of Solute ÷ Volume of Solution ] × 100

Step-by-Step Solved Concentration Numericals

Numerical Example 1 (NCERT Standard): 40 g of Salt in 320 g of Water
Step 1: Identify Given Quantities

Mass of solute (common salt) = 40 g
Mass of solvent (water) = 320 g

Step 2: Calculate Mass of Solution

Mass of Solution = Mass of Solute + Mass of Solvent = 40 g + 320 g = 360 g

Step 3: State the Appropriate Formula

Mass by Mass Percentage = [ Mass of Solute ÷ Mass of Solution ] × 100

Step 4: Execute the Calculation

Concentration % = [ 40 ÷ 360 ] × 100 = [ 1 ÷ 9 ] × 100 = 11.11% (w/w)

Step 5: Final Statement

The concentration of the common salt solution is 11.11% by mass.

Numerical Example 2: Volume by Volume Percentage of Rubbing Alcohol
Step 1: Identify Given Quantities

A disinfectant solution is prepared by mixing 50 mL of isopropyl alcohol with water to make a total solution volume of 250 mL.

Step 2: Verify Total Volume

Volume of solute (alcohol) = 50 mL
Total volume of solution = 250 mL (Notice here the problem gives the total solution volume directly!)

Step 3: Formula Application

Volume by Volume Percentage = [ Volume of Solute ÷ Volume of Solution ] × 100

Step 4: Execute Calculation

Concentration % = [ 50 ÷ 250 ] × 100 = [ 1 ÷ 5 ] × 100 = 20% (v/v)

Step 5: Final Statement

The concentration of isopropyl alcohol is 20% by volume.

2.6 Suspensions (ପ୍ରଲମ୍ବନ)

Now consider stirring a spoonful of powdered chalk or muddy soil from a garden into a beaker of water. The liquid immediately turns cloudy and opaque. Even after prolonged stirring, the chalk does not vanish. If you leave the beaker undisturbed on a table for 15 minutes, you will observe the chalk particles slowly sinking to the bottom, leaving clear water above. This system is a Suspension.

Core Definition: Suspension A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture in which the solid solute particles do not dissolve, but remain suspended throughout the bulk of the liquid medium, with particle dimensions greater than 100 nanometers (100 nm = 10⁻⁷ m).

Key Characteristics of Suspensions

  • 1. Heterogeneous Nature: Suspensions are visibly heterogeneous, consisting of solid particles dispersed in a liquid phase.
  • 2. Giant Particle Size (> 100 nm): Solute particles are so large that they can be easily seen with the unaided human eye or through a simple hand magnifying lens.
  • 3. Light Scattering & Transient Tyndall Effect: While particles remain suspended, they are large enough to intercept and scatter a beam of light passing through the liquid, rendering the path of the light beam visible. However, once the particles completely settle down, the supernatant liquid ceases to scatter light!
  • 4. Kinetic Instability (Sedimentation): Suspensions are thermodynamically and kinetically unstable. Due to their large mass, gravity pulls the suspended particles down to the bottom as sediment if left undisturbed.
  • 5. Filterability: Suspended particles cannot pass through the pores of filter paper. They can be 100% separated from the liquid by ordinary gravity filtration.

2.7 Colloidal Solutions (Colloids / କଲଏଡ୍)

Now consider a third fascinating scenario: mix a few drops of fresh milk or a spoonful of starch powder in water. The mixture looks completely uniform and milky. It does not separate into layers upon standing for hours. You cannot see individual milk particles with your naked eye. It almost looks like a true solution! But if you shine a laser pointer or flashlight through it in a dark room, the beam of light glows brilliantly inside the liquid! This wondrous state of matter is a Colloid.

Core Definition: Colloidal Solution (Colloid) A colloid is a heterogeneous mixture in which the particle size of the dispersed substance is intermediate between that of a true solution and a suspension—specifically between 1 nanometer and 100 nanometers (10⁻⁹ m to 10⁻⁷ m).

Pseudo-Homogeneity: Why Colloids Trick the Human Eye

Because the particles of a colloid are evenly distributed throughout the medium and measure between 1 nm and 100 nm, they are too small to be resolved by the naked eye. Consequently, a colloid appears homogeneous on macroscopic inspection. However, sophisticated ultra-microscopy confirms that it is fundamentally a heterogeneous multi-phase system.

Two Components of a Colloid

  • 1. Dispersed Phase (ବିକ୍ଷିପ୍ତ ପ୍ରାବସ୍ଥା): The solute-like substance or colloidal particles that are dispersed throughout the medium. In milk, the dispersed phase consists of microscopic globules of liquid butterfat and casein protein.
  • 2. Dispersion Medium (ବିକ୍ଷେପଣ ମାଧ୍ୟମ): The continuous phase/medium in which the colloidal particles are suspended. In milk, the dispersion medium is liquid water.

The Tyndall Effect (ଟିଣ୍ଡାଲ୍ ପ୍ରଭାବ)

Discovered by the British natural philosopher John Tyndall in 1869, the Tyndall Effect is the phenomenon of scattering of visible light rays by colloidal particles, which makes the illuminated pathway of the light beam distinctly visible as a bright, glowing cone (called the Tyndall Cone).

Four Classic Everyday Demonstrations of the Tyndall Effect:
  1. Sunbeam in a Dark Room: When a shaft of morning sunlight enters a dark, shuttered room through a small slit or keyhole, the path of the beam lights up brightly due to the scattering of light by suspended dust and smoke particles in the air.
  2. Canopy of a Dense Forest: In dense tropical forests (like Similipal or the Amazon), sunlight filters down through the tree canopy. Mist containing tiny colloidal water droplets suspended in air scatters the light, creating dramatic glowing sunbeams.
  3. Automobile Headlights in Fog: Driving on a misty winter highway at night, car headlights illuminate a distinct cone of glowing light due to water aerosol droplets scattering the beam.
  4. Cinema Projector Beam: In a movie theater, the projector beam is visible cutting across the hall because of microscopic dust particles floating in the auditorium.

Brownian Motion (ବ୍ରାଉନିଆନ୍ ଗତି)

In 1827, Scottish botanist Robert Brown looked through a microscope at pollen grains suspended in water and observed them executing a ceaseless, chaotic, zig-zag dance. This continuous, random, zig-zag motion of colloidal particles suspended in a dispersion medium is called Brownian Motion.

The Scientific Mechanism: Colloidal particles are constantly bombarded on all sides by the rapidly moving molecules of the dispersion medium. Because these collisions are unequal and unbalanced from different directions at any given split-second, the colloidal particle is kicked in an unpredictable zig-zag trajectory. Significance: Brownian motion provides an upward mechanical push that overcomes gravity, preventing colloidal particles from ever settling down and giving colloids their remarkable kinetic stability!

2.9 Physical vs Chemical Changes (ଭୌତିକ ଓ ରାସାୟନିକ ପରିବର୍ତ୍ତନ)

All changes occurring in matter can be classified into two fundamental scientific types: Physical changes and Chemical changes.

Physical Change (ଭୌତିକ ପରିବର୍ତ୍ତନ)

Definition: A change in which only the physical properties of a substance (such as shape, size, color, density, or physical state of matter) undergo a change, while its underlying chemical composition remains identical.

  • No New Substance Formed: The molecules remain chemically identical before and after the change.
  • Reversible in Nature: Most physical changes can be easily reversed by reversing the physical conditions (temperature, pressure).
  • Zero or Minimal Energy Transfer: Negligible net energy is exchanged.
  • Benchmark Examples:
    • Melting of ice cubes to liquid water, and boiling water to steam (all H₂O molecules!)
    • Tearing or shredding a sheet of paper
    • Dissolving common salt or sugar in water
    • Breaking a glass tumbler
    • Glowing of an electric tungsten bulb

Chemical Change (ରାସାୟନିକ ପରିବର୍ତ୍ତନ)

Definition: A change in which one or more completely new chemical substances possessing entirely different chemical compositions and chemical properties are created through the breaking and making of chemical bonds.

  • New Substance Formed: The original chemical identity is permanently lost.
  • Generally Irreversible: Cannot be reversed by simple physical methods.
  • Substantial Energy Transfer: Accompanied by significant absorption or evolution of energy in the form of heat, light, sound, or electricity.
  • Benchmark Examples:
    • Burning of coal, wood, or paper to produce CO₂, water vapor, and ash
    • Rusting of iron in humid air to form hydrated iron oxide (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O)
    • Cooking of food and digestion of food in the human stomach
    • Souring of milk into curd (fermentation of lactose to lactic acid)
    • Ripening of a green mango
The Famous CBSE Brainteaser: Burning of a Candle Question: Is the burning of a candle a physical change, a chemical change, or both? Justify your answer.
Scientific Verdict: The burning of a wax candle represents BOTH a physical change and a chemical change occurring simultaneously:
  1. Physical Change: When the candle is lit, heat from the flame melts the solid wax into liquid molten wax near the wick. On cooling, this molten wax solidifies back into solid wax without changing its chemical identity. This is purely a reversible physical change of state.
  2. Chemical Change: Liquid wax is drawn up through the cotton wick by capillary action and vaporizes. The wax vapor combusts with atmospheric oxygen (O₂) to form completely new chemical substances: Carbon Dioxide (CO₂), Water Vapor (H₂O), unburnt carbon soot, releasing heat and light energy. This combustion is irreversible and represents a chemical change.

2.10 Pure Substances: Elements and Compounds (ମୌଳିକ ଓ ଯୌଗିକ)

As established in Section 1, pure substances are chemically divided into two great pillars: Elements and Compounds.

1. Elements (ମୌଳିକ)

In 1661, Anglo-Irish scientist Robert Boyle was the first scientist to use the term "Element" in modern chemistry. Later, the great French chemist Antoine Laurent Lavoisier (1743–1794) established the first rigorous experimental definition:

Lavoisier's Definition of an Element An element is a basic form of matter that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any chemical reactions (heating, light, or electric current).

Today, 118 elements are known to science. 94 occur naturally on Earth, while the remainder are synthesized in nuclear physics laboratories. Elements are subdivided into three broad classes based on their physical and chemical behaviors:

2. Compounds (ଯୌଗିକ)

When two or more distinct elements chemically react and bind together, they create a Compound.

Core Definition: Chemical Compound A compound is a pure substance composed of two or more different elements chemically combined together in a fixed, definite proportion by mass.

The Law of Constant Proportions: In pure water (H₂O), the ratio of the mass of hydrogen to the mass of oxygen is always strictly 1 : 8 (e.g., 2 g H reacts with 16 g O). Whether water is collected from the Mahanadi River in Odisha, from Arctic iceberg ice, or synthesized in a laboratory, this mass ratio is eternal and invariable.

Transformation of Chemical Properties: The properties of a chemical compound are completely different from those of its constituent elements!
• Hydrogen gas is highly combustible and burns with an explosion.
• Oxygen gas is a vigorous supporter of combustion.
• Yet, their chemical combination produces water (H₂O)—a liquid that extinguishes fire!

2.11 The Landmark Iron and Sulfur Experiment: Mixture vs Compound

To fundamentally comprehend the profound difference between a physical mixture and a chemical compound, consider the classic NCERT laboratory experiment involving Iron filings (Fe) and Sulfur powder (S):

Diagnostic Test Performed Sample A: Physical Mixture (Fe + S) Sample B: Chemical Compound (Iron(II) Sulfide - FeS)
Preparation Method 5 g of grey iron filings and 3 g of yellow sulfur powder are simply crushed and mixed together in a mortar without heating. 5 g of iron filings and 3 g of sulfur powder are mixed and heated strongly in a china dish until the mixture glows with a red crust.
Visual Appearance Heterogeneous, yellowish-grey powder. Individual grey iron particles and yellow sulfur specks are distinctly visible. Homogeneous, hard, uniform black solid mass (Iron(II) Sulfide, FeS). No individual yellow specks visible.
Action of a Bar Magnet When a bar magnet is brought close, the iron filings are strongly attracted and cling to the magnet, leaving yellow sulfur behind! (Physical separation achieved). When a bar magnet is brought close, nothing is attracted! Iron has lost its individual magnetic property by chemically bonding with sulfur into FeS.
Action of Carbon Disulfide (CS₂) Solvent When carbon disulfide liquid is added and stirred, the yellow sulfur powder dissolves completely. Iron filings remain undissolved at the bottom. When carbon disulfide is added, no sulfur dissolves. FeS is completely insoluble in carbon disulfide.
Reaction with Dilute Sulfuric Acid (H₂SO₄) Iron reacts with the acid to evolve Hydrogen gas (H₂):
Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂↑
Hydrogen gas is colourless, odourless, and burns with a loud 'pop' sound when tested with a burning splinter.		 The compound reacts with acid to evolve Hydrogen Sulfide gas (H₂S):
FeS + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂S↑
Hydrogen sulfide gas is colourless but has a foul, nauseating, characteristic smell of rotten eggs!

2.12 Exhaustive Separation Techniques for Mixtures

Most natural substances are complex mixtures. To obtain pure substances for scientific research, pharmaceuticals, and manufacturing, chemists employ separation techniques. The foundational rule of separation science: Every technique exploits a specific difference in a physical property (such as boiling point, density, solubility, volatility, magnetic behavior, or sublimability) between the components of the mixture.

Technique 1: Evaporation (ବାଷ୍ପୀଭବନ)

  • Underlying Principle: Used to separate a non-volatile solid solute dissolved in a volatile liquid solvent. The volatile solvent vaporizes into the atmosphere upon heating, leaving behind the solid solute.
  • Laboratory Setup & Precaution: To separate coloured dye from black fountain pen ink, the ink is not heated directly over a flame (direct heat might burn or decompose the organic dyes). Instead, a watch glass containing a few drops of ink is placed on top of a beaker containing boiling water (indirect water bath heating).
  • Everyday Applications: Obtaining common salt (NaCl) from sea water in shallow salt pans (using natural solar evaporation).

Technique 2: Centrifugation (ଅପକେନ୍ଦ୍ରଣ)

  • Underlying Principle: When a mixture containing suspended or colloidal particles is rotated at very high speed in a centrifuge machine, denser particles are forced outward towards the bottom of the tube by centrifugal forces, while lighter particles remain suspended or float at the top.
  • Crucial Real-World Applications:
    1. Medical Diagnostic Laboratories: Used for blood tests and urine analysis. Centrifuging whole blood separates the denser red blood cells (RBCs) and white blood cells (WBCs) to the bottom, leaving the pale straw-colored blood plasma at the top.
    2. Dairies and Kitchens: Used to churn cream from whole milk, separating lighter butterfat from skimmed milk.
    3. Washing Machines: The high-speed spin-dryer cycle forces water droplets out of wet clothes through drum perforations.

Technique 3: Separating Funnel (ପୃଥକୀକରଣ ଫନେଲ୍)

  • Underlying Principle: Separating two immiscible liquids (liquids that do not mix, like oil and water). When left undisturbed inside a pear-shaped glass separating funnel, they separate into two distinct horizontal layers based on their densities. The denser liquid forms the bottom layer, and the lighter liquid floats on top.
  • Operation: The stopcock at the bottom of the funnel is carefully opened to drain out the lower, denser liquid layer into a beaker below, and shut off instantly as the boundary line reaches the tap.
  • Real-World Applications: Separating mustard oil/kerosene from water; in the industrial extraction of iron inside a blast furnace, the lighter molten slag floats on top of the heavier molten iron and is skimmed off.

Technique 4: Sublimation (ଊର୍ଦ୍ଧ୍ୱପାତନ)

  • Underlying Principle: Used to separate a sublimable component (a substance that changes directly from a solid to a gaseous state upon heating without passing through an intermediate liquid phase) from a non-sublimable solid impurity.
  • Sublimable Substances to Memorize: Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl), Camphor (କର୍ପୂର), Naphthalene balls, Anthracene, and Iodine (I₂).
  • Laboratory Setup: The mixture of NH₄Cl and common salt (NaCl) is placed in a porcelain china dish. An inverted glass funnel is placed over the dish, and the stem of the funnel is plugged with a cotton ball to trap escaping vapors. Upon gentle heating, NH₄Cl vaporizes, rises, and condenses on the cool upper walls of the funnel as pure solid sublimate. Common salt remains behind in the dish.

Figure 5.3: Sublimation Apparatus Setup. Ammonium chloride transitions directly to vapor upon heating and crystallizes on the cool inner funnel walls, completely separating from non-sublimable common salt.

Technique 5: Paper Chromatography (କ୍ରୋମାଟୋଗ୍ରାଫି)

  • Etymology & Discovery: Derived from the Greek word "Chroma", which means colour. It was invented in 1900 by Russian-Italian botanist Mikhail Tsvet to separate colourful plant pigments (chlorophylls and carotenoids).
  • Underlying Principle: Used for the separation of those solutes that dissolve in the same solvent, but have different solubilities.
    As the mobile liquid solvent (e.g., water) moves up the stationary porous strip of Whatman filter paper by capillary action, it carries the solute dyes along with it. The coloured component that is more soluble in water rises faster and travels higher up the paper strip, separating the colours into distinct bands!
  • Modern Scientific Applications:
    1. Separating multiple coloured chemical dyes present in fountain pen ink.
    2. Separating natural photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, xanthophylls, carotenoids) from green leaves.
    3. Forensic Science & Medicine: Separating and identifying traces of illegal drugs, narcotics, and poisons from blood and urine samples in police crime laboratories.

Figure 5.4: Paper Chromatography Principle. As water ascends the filter paper by capillary forces, dyes with greater solubility in water migrate faster and reach higher positions.

Technique 6: Simple Distillation (ସରଳ ପାତନ)

  • Underlying Principle: Used for the separation of components of a mixture containing two miscible liquids that boil without decomposition and have a sufficiently large difference in their boiling points (temperature difference greater than 25 K or 25 °C).
  • Operation: The liquid mixture is heated inside a distillation flask. The component with the lower boiling point vaporizes first. Its vapors rise into the neck of the flask, enter a water-cooled Liebig Condenser (where cold water constantly circulates around the inner glass tube), condense back into a pure liquid, and collect in a receiving beaker. The higher boiling liquid remains behind in the flask.
  • Classic Laboratory Example: Separating a mixture of Acetone (boiling point 56 °C / 329 K) and Water (boiling point 100 °C / 373 K). The boiling point difference is 100 - 56 = 44 °C, which is much greater than 25 °C!

Technique 7: Fractional Distillation (ଆଂଶିକ ପାତନ)

  • Underlying Principle: Used to separate a mixture of two or more miscible liquids for which the difference in boiling points is LESS than 25 K (25 °C).
  • The Critical Structural Addition — Fractionating Column: A simple distillation apparatus cannot separate liquids with close boiling points because both liquids would vaporize together. In fractional distillation, a fractionating column is fitted vertically between the distillation flask and the condenser.
    Function of Glass Beads: The fractionating column is packed with thousands of tiny glass beads. These beads provide an enormous surface area for repeated, multi-stage condensation and vaporisation cycles. As vapors rise through the column, the higher-boiling vapors condense on the glass beads and drip back down into the flask, while only the pure vapors of the lower-boiling liquid successfully reach the top and pass into the condenser.
  • Critical Real-World Applications:
    1. Separation of Gases from Air: Atmospheric air is filtered, compressed under high pressure, and cooled down to -200 °C to form liquid air. This liquid air is fed into a fractional distillation column and warmed slowly:
      • Nitrogen (N₂): Boiling point -196 °C (77 K). It has the lowest boiling point, so it boils off first at the top!
      • Argon (Ar): Boiling point -186 °C (87 K). Boils off second in the middle fraction.
      • Oxygen (O₂): Boiling point -183 °C (90 K). Has the highest boiling point, so it remains as a liquid at the bottom of the column!
    2. Petroleum Refining: Crude crude petroleum oil is separated into various fractions (petrol/gasoline, diesel, kerosene, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), paraffin wax, and bitumen) inside huge industrial fractionating towers.

Figure 5.5: Fractional Distillation Apparatus. The fractionating column packed with glass beads provides extensive surface area for repeated vaporisation-condensation cycles, successfully resolving liquids whose boiling points differ by less than 25 K.

Technique 8: Crystallisation (ସ୍ଫଟିକୀକରଣ)

  • Underlying Principle: Crystallisation is a process that separates a pure solid in the form of its geometric crystals from a saturated solution upon slow, undisturbed cooling.
  • Why Crystallisation is Far Superior to Simple Evaporation:
    1. Prevents Thermal Decomposition: Certain solids decompose or get charred (scorched into black carbon) upon direct heating to dryness (e.g., cane sugar chars into burnt black carbon during dry evaporation).
    2. Excludes Soluble Impurities: In simple evaporation, all soluble chemical impurities present in the liquid remain trapped inside the dried solid cake. In crystallisation, impurities remain dissolved behind in the remaining mother liquor, yielding ultra-pure crystals.
  • Standard Laboratory Method (Purifying Impure Copper Sulfate):
    1. Dissolve an impure sample of copper sulfate in the minimum volume of distilled water in a beaker.
    2. Filter out all insoluble impurities using filter paper.
    3. Evaporate water gently over a water bath until the crystallisation point (saturation point) is reached (verified when a crust of crystals forms on a cold glass rod dipped into the hot solution).
    4. Cover the beaker with filter paper and leave it completely undisturbed at room temperature to cool slowly overnight.
    5. Brilliant, deep-blue, pure triclinic crystals of copper sulfate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) grow and are separated by filtration!

Activity 5.1: Preparing and Distinguishing Different Mixtures
Step 1: Core Concept

Mixtures can be homogeneous (uniform single phase) or heterogeneous (non-uniform multi-phase) depending on particle distribution and solubility.

Step 2: Experimental Setup

Divide the class into 4 groups:
• Group A: 50 mL water + 1 spatula of Copper Sulfate (CuSO₄) powder.
• Group B: 50 mL water + 2 spatulas of Copper Sulfate powder.
• Group C: Copper sulfate powder + Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) or common salt.
• Group D: Chalk powder or wheat flour + water.

Step 3: Scientific Observations

1. Groups A and B obtain clear blue solutions with uniform color throughout. Group B has a deeper blue color than Group A, showing homogeneous mixtures can have variable composition.
2. Group C obtains a solid mixture with distinctly visible specks of different colors.
3. Group D obtains a cloudy, opaque liquid where chalk/flour particles remain suspended and slowly settle down.

Step 4: Inference

Groups A and B obtained homogeneous mixtures (true solutions). Groups C and D obtained heterogeneous mixtures (Group D specifically formed a suspension).

Step 5: Master Takeaway

Homogeneous mixtures possess uniform composition throughout, whereas heterogeneous mixtures exhibit physically distinct parts and visible boundaries.

Activity 5.2: Comparing True Solutions, Suspensions, and Colloids
Step 1: Core Concept

True solutions, suspensions, and colloids differ fundamentally in particle size, optical light scattering (Tyndall effect), kinetic stability, and filterability.

Step 2: Experimental Setup

Prepare four samples in separate beakers:
(a) Copper sulfate crystals in water (True Solution).
(b) Chalk powder in water (Suspension).
(c) Milk or ink diluted in water (Colloid).
(d) Direct a torch beam through each beaker and then filter each through Whatman filter paper.

Step 3: Scientific Observations

• Visibility of Light Beam: In beaker (a), the light beam path is invisible. In beakers (b) and (c), the path of light glows brightly!

Activity 5.3: Investigating Solubility and Saturated Solutions
Step 1: Core Concept

At any given temperature, a solvent can dissolve only a finite, maximum mass of solute. Heating increases solubility for solid solutes.

Step 2: Experimental Setup

Step 1: Core Concept

Chromatography separates solutes sharing the same solvent based on differences in their solubility and adsorption speed.

Activity 5.9: Separating Acetone and Water by Simple Distillation
Step 1: Core Concept

Simple distillation separates miscible liquids that boil without decomposition and have a boiling point difference greater than 25 K.

Step 2: Experimental Setup

Take an acetone-water mixture in a distillation flask fitted with a thermometer and a Liebig condenser. Heat slowly while watching the thermometer.

Step 3: Observations

• At 56 °C (329 K), the thermometer mercury level stabilizes. Acetone boils and vaporizes rapidly.
• Acetone vapors condense inside the water-cooled Liebig condenser and drip into the receiving flask as clear liquid acetone.
• Water (BP 100 °C / 373 K) remains in the distillation flask.

Difference in boiling points = 100 °C - 56 °C = 44 °C (> 25 K). Thus, simple distillation gives sharp, complete separation.

Step 5: Master Takeaway

Miscible liquids with boiling point differences > 25 K are effectively separated by simple distillation.

In-Text Question Set 1 (Page 15): Substance Definition & Types of Mixtures
Step 1: Core Concept

A substance is pure chemical matter with invariant composition, while mixtures contain two or more substances combined physically.

Step 2: Question 1 Answer (What is meant by a substance?)

In chemistry, a substance (pure substance) is a single, pure form of matter that has a fixed, uniform chemical composition and a specific set of properties throughout. It consists of only one kind of constituent particle (atoms or molecules) and cannot be separated into other kinds of matter by any physical process. Examples: pure water (H₂O), pure gold (Au), sodium chloride (NaCl).

Step 3: Question 2 Answer (Name the two types of mixtures)

The two fundamental classes of mixtures are:
1. Homogeneous Mixtures (uniform composition, single phase, e.g., salt dissolved in water, brass).
2. Heterogeneous Mixtures (non-uniform composition, multi-phase with visible boundaries, e.g., sand in water, oil and water).

Step 4: Pedagogical Note

Do not confuse ordinary colloquial "substance" (any object) with the precise chemical definition (pure substance).

Step 5: Master Takeaway

Substances possess identical particles; mixtures combine multiple substances in variable proportions.

In-Text Question Set 2 (Page 18): Differentiate Homogeneous vs Heterogeneous
Step 1: Core Concept

The distinction lies in macroscopic and microscopic uniformity of composition and the presence or absence of physical phase boundaries.

Step 2: Homogeneous Mixtures

Step 4: Tabular Comparison

Homogeneous mixtures behave as a single optical and physical continuum; heterogeneous mixtures retain distinguishable particulate domains.

Step 5: Master Takeaway

Homogeneity implies uniform single-phase distribution; heterogeneity indicates distinct phase separation.

Mass of Solvent = 200 g

Directions: Select (A) if both Assertion and Reason are true and Reason is the correct explanation; (B) if both are true but Reason is NOT the correct explanation; (C) if Assertion is true but Reason is false; (D) if Assertion is false but Reason is true.

Assertion-Reason 1: Classification of Brass as a Mixture

Assertion (A): Brass is classified by chemists as a mixture and not as a chemical compound, even though it possesses uniform composition and cannot be separated by physical filtration.
Reason (R): Brass exhibits the individual physical and chemical properties of its constituent elements (copper and zinc) and can have a variable percentage composition.

Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
Scientific Rationale: Compounds have fixed stoichiometric proportions and entirely novel properties. Alloys like brass retain the properties of their constituent metals and can be synthesized in variable ratios (e.g., 70% Cu + 30% Zn or 80% Cu + 20% Zn).

Assertion-Reason 2: Optical Scattering in Colloids

Assertion (A): When a beam of light is passed through milk, the path of the light beam becomes illuminated with a bright cone of light.
Reason (R): The colloidal fat and protein particles in milk have dimensions between 1 nm and 100 nm, which are comparable to the wavelengths of visible light and scatter the light rays.

Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
Scientific Rationale: This is the exact physical mechanism of the Tyndall effect. Sub-nanometer particles in true solutions cannot scatter visible light, but colloidal particles (1–100 nm) scatter light efficiently.

Assertion-Reason 3: Crystallisation vs Simple Evaporation

Assertion (A): Crystallisation is considered a superior technique to simple evaporation for obtaining pure solids from solutions.
Reason (R): Simple evaporation to dryness can decompose or char heat-sensitive solids, and leaves soluble impurities mixed with the dried solid residue.

Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
Scientific Rationale: In crystallisation, pure crystals grow selectively from the saturated solution during slow cooling, while soluble impurities remain locked in the mother liquor.

Assertion-Reason 4: Separation of Ethanol and Water

Assertion (A): A mixture of ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) and water (boiling point 100 °C) can be easily and completely separated using a simple distillation apparatus.
Reason (R): Simple distillation is used when the boiling point difference between two miscible liquids is less than 25 K.

Answer: (D) Assertion (A) is false, and Reason (R) is false.
Scientific Rationale: Boiling point difference = 100 - 78 = 22 °C (22 K), which is less than 25 K! Simple distillation fails here because ethanol and water vapors would distill over together. Fractional distillation with a fractionating column is mandatory. Furthermore, simple distillation is used when ΔBP is greater than 25 K, making Reason (R) chemically false.

Assertion-Reason 5: Burning of a Wax Candle

Assertion (A): The burning of a wax candle involves both physical and chemical changes taking place simultaneously.
Reason (R): Melting of solid wax to liquid wax is a reversible physical change of state, while the combustion of wax vapor at the wick is an irreversible chemical reaction forming CO₂ and H₂O.

Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
Scientific Rationale: Melting alters only physical state without creating new molecules; combustion of vaporized hydrocarbon wax with oxygen produces new chemical compounds (CO₂, H₂O) and releases energy.

Part 5C: Short Answer Questions (2M & 3M)

SAQ 1 (2 Marks): Why is a Colloid Pseudo-Homogeneous?

Question: Why does a colloidal solution appear homogeneous to our eyes, yet is scientifically classified as heterogeneous? (2M)

Model Answer:
1. Visual Appearance (1 Mark): Colloidal particles measure between 1 nm and 100 nm. Because they are uniformly dispersed and much smaller than the resolving limit of the unaided human eye (~100 μm), the mixture appears uniform and homogeneous macroscopically.
2. Scientific Reality (1 Mark): Under an ultra-microscope, two distinct physical phases coexist: the dispersed phase and the dispersion medium. Because distinct phase boundaries exist at the nanoscale, science classifies colloids as fundamentally heterogeneous mixtures.

SAQ 2 (3 Marks): Three Reasons Why Water is a Compound and Not a Mixture

Question: State three scientific reasons why water (H₂O) is considered a chemical compound and not a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gases. (3M)

Model Answer:
• 1. Fixed Mass Ratio (1 Mark): In water, hydrogen and oxygen are chemically combined in an invariant, fixed proportion of 1 : 8 by mass (Law of Constant Proportions). Mixtures have variable ratios.
• 2. Distinct Chemical Properties (1 Mark): Water has completely new properties: hydrogen is explosive and burns; oxygen supports combustion; but water is a non-flammable liquid that extinguishes fire! In a mixture, constituents retain their original properties.
• 3. Inseparable by Physical Means (1 Mark): Water cannot be separated into hydrogen and oxygen by filtration, distillation, or magnets; it requires an electrochemical reaction (electrolysis of water).

SAQ 3 (3 Marks): Separation Scheme for Salt, Sand, and Ammonium Chloride

Question: A student accidentally mixed common salt, ammonium chloride, and fine sand. Describe a sequential 3-step experimental procedure to separate all three components in pure form. (3M)

Model Answer:
• Step 1: Sublimation (1 Mark): Heat the dry mixture in a china dish covered with an inverted, cotton-plugged glass funnel. Ammonium chloride sublimes into vapor and condenses on the cold funnel walls as solid sublimate, isolating pure NH₄Cl.
• Step 2: Dissolution & Filtration (1 Mark): Add distilled water to the remaining mixture of salt and sand and stir thoroughly. Common salt dissolves completely in water, while sand remains insoluble. Filter through filter paper: pure sand is collected on the paper as residue.
• Step 3: Evaporation / Crystallisation (1 Mark): Heat the clear salt solution (filtrate) to evaporate the water. Pure common salt crystals remain behind in the evaporating dish.

SAQ 4 (3 Marks): Tyndall Effect: Conditions and Real-Life Examples

Question: What is the Tyndall effect? State two conditions required to observe it and give two real-life examples. (3M)

Model Answer:
• Definition (1 Mark): The Tyndall effect is the phenomenon of scattering of a beam of visible light by colloidal particles, rendering the path of the light beam visible.
• Two Conditions (1 Mark):
(i) The diameter of the dispersed particles must not be much smaller than the wavelength of the light used (particle size 1–100 nm).
(ii) There must be a large difference between the refractive indices of the dispersed phase and the dispersion medium.
• Two Real-Life Examples (1 Mark):
(i) Sunlight streaming through mist in the canopy of a dense forest.
(ii) Car headlights cutting through winter fog at night.

SAQ 5 (2 Marks): Numerical: 25 g Glucose in 225 g Water

Question: Calculate the mass by mass percentage concentration of a solution prepared by dissolving 25 g of glucose in 225 g of distilled water. What happens to its concentration if the solution is heated in a closed container? (2M)

Model Answer:
• Calculation (1.5 Marks):
Mass of solute (glucose) = 25 g
Mass of solvent (water) = 225 g
Mass of solution = 25 + 225 = 250 g
Concentration % = [25 ÷ 250] × 100 = 10.0% (w/w).
• Effect of Heating in Closed Container (0.5 Mark): Because the container is closed, no water vapor escapes. Mass of solute and mass of solution remain unchanged; hence the mass percentage concentration remains exactly 10.0%.

SAQ 6 (3 Marks): Simple vs Fractional Distillation & Role of Glass Beads

Question: Distinguish between simple distillation and fractional distillation. What is the specific structural role of the glass beads packed inside a fractionating column? (3M)

Model Answer:
• Distinction (1.5 Marks):
1. Simple Distillation: Used for miscible liquids having a large boiling point difference (ΔBP > 25 K). Involves a single vaporization-condensation cycle.
2. Fractional Distillation: Used for miscible liquids having close boiling points (ΔBP < 25 K). Uses a specialized fractionating column between the flask and condenser.
• Role of Glass Beads (1.5 Marks): The packed glass beads provide an extensive surface area for repeated, multi-stage condensation and vaporisation cycles. Higher-boiling vapors condense on the cold bead surfaces and trickle back into the flask, while lower-boiling vapors pass through to the condenser, ensuring high purity separation.

Part 5D: Long Answer Questions (5M) with Marking Schemes

LAQ 1 (5 Marks): Comprehensive Comparison of Solutions, Colloids, and Suspensions

Question: Compare True Solutions, Colloidal Solutions, and Suspensions across five distinct physical parameters: (a) Particle size, (b) Homogeneity, (c) Filterability through filter paper, (d) Kinetic stability, and (e) Tyndall effect. Provide one real-world example of each. (5M)

CBSE Marking Scheme & Model Answer:
• (a) Particle Size (1 Mark): True Solution: < 1 nm (< 10⁻⁹ m). Colloid: 1 nm to 100 nm (10⁻⁹ m to 10⁻⁷ m). Suspension: > 100 nm (> 10⁻⁷ m).
• (b) Homogeneity (1 Mark): True Solution: Strictly homogeneous single phase. Colloid: Heterogeneous (appears homogeneous macroscopically). Suspension: Visibly heterogeneous multi-phase system.
• (c) Filterability (1 Mark): True Solution: Passes completely through filter paper without residue. Colloid: Passes through ordinary filter paper (retained only by ultra-filters). Suspension: Retained completely on filter paper as solid residue.
• (d) Kinetic Stability (1 Mark): True Solution: Highly stable; solute never settles down. Colloid: Stable; particles do not settle due to Brownian motion. Suspension: Unstable; solid particles settle down at bottom as sediment under gravity.
• (e) Tyndall Effect & Examples (1 Mark): True Solution: Does NOT scatter light (path invisible); e.g., sugar in water. Colloid: Exhibits brilliant Tyndall light scattering (glowing cone); e.g., fresh milk. Suspension: Scatters light when unsettled; e.g., chalk powder stirred in water.

LAQ 2 (5 Marks): Fractional Distillation of Liquefied Air for N₂, Ar, and O₂

Question: Atmospheric air is a homogeneous mixture of various gases. Describe the complete industrial process of separating Nitrogen, Argon, and Oxygen from air by fractional distillation. Include boiling points and sequence of separation. (5M)

CBSE Marking Scheme & Model Answer:
• Step 1: Purification & Compression (1.5 Marks): Atmospheric air is filtered to remove suspended dust particles. It is then compressed to high pressure (around 200 atm). Compression heats the air, so it is cooled by circulating cold water.
• Step 2: Liquefaction & Freezing of CO₂ and H₂O (1.5 Marks): The compressed air expands rapidly through a jet nozzle into a low-pressure chamber (Joule-Thomson cooling). Water vapor freezes out as ice, and carbon dioxide freezes out as solid dry ice (CO₂ sublimes at -78.5 °C). Repeated cycles cool the remaining air down to -200 °C, turning it into liquid air.
• Step 3: Fractional Distillation Column (2 Marks): Liquid air is pumped into a tall fractional distillation column and allowed to warm slowly:
1. Nitrogen (N₂): Boiling point = -196 °C (77 K). Lowest boiling point; boils off first and collects as gas at the top of the column (~78% volume).
2. Argon (Ar): Boiling point = -186 °C (87 K). Boils off second and is collected in the middle fraction (~0.9% volume).
3. Oxygen (O₂): Boiling point = -183 °C (90 K). Highest boiling point; remains behind as liquid oxygen at the bottom of the column (~21% volume).

LAQ 3 (5 Marks): Complete Study of Crystallisation for Copper Sulfate

Question: (a) What is crystallisation? (b) Describe the step-by-step laboratory method to obtain pure copper sulfate crystals from an impure commercial sample. (c) State two distinct reasons why crystallisation is preferred over simple evaporation. (5M)

CBSE Marking Scheme & Model Answer:
• (a) Definition (1 Mark): Crystallisation is a physical separation process that isolates a pure solid substance in the form of geometric crystals from its saturated solution upon slow, undisturbed cooling.
• (b) Laboratory Method (2.5 Marks):
1. Dissolution: Dissolve about 5–10 g of impure copper sulfate in the minimum volume of warm distilled water in a beaker.
2. Filtration: Filter the hot solution to remove insoluble dirt and sand impurities.
3. Concentration: Heat the clear blue filtrate gently over a water bath until the crystallisation point is reached (tested when a crust forms on a cold glass rod dipped into the liquid).
4. Slow Cooling: Turn off heat, cover the beaker with filter paper, and allow it to cool slowly at room temperature overnight without moving or agitating.
5. Separation: Beautiful deep-blue triclinic crystals of pure CuSO₄·5H₂O grow. Separate crystals by filtration and dry between filter paper pads.
• (c) Two Reasons for Superiority (1.5 Marks):
1. Prevents Decomposition: Direct dry heating causes many solids to decompose or char (e.g., sugar chars to black carbon). Crystallisation avoids high dry temperatures.
2. Excludes Impurities: In dry evaporation, all soluble chemical contaminants remain in the solid cake. In crystallisation, impurities remain dissolved in the leftover mother liquor.

LAQ 4 (5 Marks): In-Depth Investigation of Iron + Sulfur Reaction

Question: Two students, Ananya and Bikash, are given 7 g of iron filings and 4 g of sulfur powder. Ananya merely grinds them together in a mortar (Sample A). Bikash heats the mixture strongly in a china dish until it glows red hot and forms a black mass (Sample B). Describe what happens when both samples are subjected to: (i) A bar magnet, (ii) Carbon disulfide (CS₂), and (iii) Dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl). Include chemical equations and identify which sample is a mixture and which is a compound. (5M)

CBSE Marking Scheme & Model Answer:
• Identification (1 Mark): Sample A is a heterogeneous physical mixture (Fe + S). Sample B is a chemical compound, Iron(II) Sulfide (FeS).
• (i) Magnet Test (1 Mark):
- Sample A: Magnet attracts and pulls out all grey iron filings, separating them from yellow sulfur.
- Sample B: Magnet fails to attract anything. Iron has lost its individual magnetic property by chemically binding with sulfur into FeS.
• (ii) CS₂ Solvent Test (1 Mark):
- Sample A: Yellow sulfur dissolves completely in carbon disulfide liquid, leaving iron filings.
- Sample B: Insoluble; no sulfur dissolves because elemental sulfur is no longer present.
• (iii) Dilute HCl Test (2 Marks):
- Sample A: Iron reacts to evolve colourless, odourless Hydrogen gas (H₂) that burns with a 'pop' sound: Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂↑.
- Sample B: Iron(II) sulfide reacts to evolve toxic Hydrogen sulfide gas (H₂S), which has a distinct, suffocating smell of rotten eggs: FeS + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂S↑.

Part 5E: Competency-Based Case Studies

Case Study 1: Forensic Toxicology and Sports Doping Control
Case Narrative: During the National Athletics Championship, an athlete's urine sample was flagged for suspected performance-enhancing anabolic steroid abuse. A forensic laboratory received a 5 mL urine sample along with a disputed ransom note written in black fountain pen ink. The toxicologist used paper chromatography to analyze the black ink against three suspect pens (Pen X, Pen Y, Pen Z) and used gas-liquid chromatography to separate trace drug metabolites from blood serum proteins.

Sub-questions & Step-by-Step Solutions:

Q1.1: What is the fundamental physical principle behind paper chromatography? (1M)
Answer: It is based on the differential solubility and differential rate of adsorption of various solute components in the same mobile solvent as it ascends a stationary porous paper medium by capillary action.

Q1.2: Why must the baseline pencil mark be drawn above the solvent level in the chromatography jar? (1M)
Answer: If the ink spot is submerged below the solvent level, the ink will dissolve directly into the bulk solvent pool in the beaker instead of rising up the paper strip, ruining the chromatogram.

Q1.3: If Component A travels 4 cm and the solvent front travels 8 cm, calculate the Retention Factor (Rf) of Component A. (1M)
Answer: Rf = [Distance travelled by solute ÷ Distance travelled by solvent front] = 4 cm ÷ 8 cm = 0.5 (Rf is dimensionless).

Q1.4: Before running chromatography on the athlete's blood sample, which separation technique is used in diagnostic labs to isolate clear plasma from blood cells? (1M)
Answer: Centrifugation (spins at 3000–5000 RPM to force dense blood cells to the bottom, separating clear plasma at the top).

Case Study 2: Municipal Water Purification Crisis in Coastal Odisha
Case Narrative: Following a severe monsoon cyclonic storm in coastal Odisha, the Mahanadi River swelled with heavy mud, silt, and bacterial runoff. The municipal water treatment plant at Cuttack faced immense turbidity. The chief water engineer supervised a 4-tank treatment plant consisting of a Sedimentation tank, Loading tank, Filtration bed, and Chlorination chamber to restore clean drinking tap water to 800,000 citizens.

Sub-questions & Step-by-Step Solutions:

Q2.1: Why does gravity alone in the sedimentation tank fail to clear the finest muddy clay particles? (1M)
Answer: Fine clay particles exist as colloidal suspensions (1–100 nm). Their extremely tiny mass and continuous Brownian collisions overcome gravity, keeping them suspended indefinitely.

Q2.2: Which chemical compound is added in the Loading Tank, and what is its scientific role? (1M)
Answer: Alum (Potash Alum / ଫିଟିକିରି) is added. Alum provides Al³⁺ ions that neutralize the negative surface charges on colloidal clay particles, causing them to aggregate (flocculate) into heavy clumps that rapidly settle to the bottom.

Q2.3: Name the three distinct material layers present inside the Filtration Bed from top to bottom. (1M)
Answer: Top layer: Fine Sand; Middle layer: Coarse Sand; Bottom layer: Gravel / Pebbles.

Q2.4: Why is chlorination carried out as the final step rather than the first step of water purification? (1M)
Answer: If chlorine is added first, it reacts with bulk organic mud and is wasted. After all physical particles and colloidal clay are removed by filtration, a small, safe dose of chlorine gas or bleaching powder effectively disinfects the water by killing pathogenic bacteria and water-borne pathogens.

PYQ 3 [CBSE 2022]: Boiling Point Behavior: Pure Substance vs Mixture (3M)

Question: Why does the temperature remain constant during the boiling of a pure substance, while a liquid mixture boils over a temperature range? (3M)

CBSE Model Answer:
1. Pure Substance (1.5 Marks): In a pure substance (like pure water), all molecules are identical. During boiling at 100 °C, the supplied heat energy is completely consumed as latent heat of vaporization to break intermolecular hydrogen bonds between identical molecules. Hence, the temperature remains strictly constant at 100 °C until the phase change is complete.
2. Mixture (1.5 Marks): In a mixture (like saltwater or ethanol-water), different components have different boiling points and intermolecular attractions. As the more volatile component vaporizes, the concentration and composition of the remaining liquid constantly changes, causing the boiling point to rise continuously across a broad temperature range.

PYQ 4 [CBSE 2020 / BSE 2022]: Why Colloidal Particles Never Settle (2M)

Question: Explain why colloidal particles do not settle down under the influence of gravity when left undisturbed on a laboratory table for days. (2M)

CBSE Model Answer:
1. Brownian Motion (1 Mark): Colloidal particles are subjected to continuous, rapid, chaotic zig-zag bombardment by moving molecules of the dispersion medium. This thermal kinetic motion provides an upward mechanical force that counteracts the downward gravitational pull.
2. Electrostatic Charge Repulsion (1 Mark): All colloidal particles in a given sol carry the same sign of electric charge (either all positive or all negative). When particles approach each other, electrostatic repulsion prevents them from coalescing into larger, heavier clumps, ensuring permanent stability.

5 Common Student Blunders to Avoid in Exam Hall

Blunder 1: Dividing by Solvent Mass in Concentration Formulas The Mistake: In numerical problems like "40 g salt dissolved in 160 g water", writing [40 ÷ 160] × 100 = 25%.
The Correction: The denominator in the formula is Mass of Solution, NOT mass of solvent! Always calculate: Mass of Solution = Mass of Solute + Mass of Solvent = 40 + 160 = 200 g. Correct answer: [40 ÷ 200] × 100 = 20%.
Blunder 2: Claiming Alloys are Chemical Compounds The Mistake: Thinking that because Brass or Stainless Steel is a solid that looks completely uniform and cannot be separated by filter paper, it must be a compound.
The Correction: Alloys are homogeneous mixtures! They retain the individual properties of their constituent metals and have variable percentage compositions. Chemical compounds have strictly fixed stoichiometric ratios.
Blunder 3: Calling Fresh Cow Milk a "Pure Substance" or "True Solution" The Mistake: Confusing grocery shop purity ("unadulterated") with chemical purity, or assuming milk is a true solution because it looks uniform.
The Correction: Chemically, milk is an emulsion (colloid) of liquid butterfat globules dispersed in water with proteins and lactose. It exhibits the Tyndall effect and can be separated by centrifugation.
Blunder 4: Choosing Fractional Distillation When BP Difference is Large The Mistake: Recommending fractional distillation for acetone (BP 56 °C) and water (BP 100 °C).
The Correction: The difference in boiling points is 100 - 56 = 44 °C, which is much greater than 25 K! A simple distillation apparatus is simpler, faster, and completely sufficient. Reserve fractional distillation strictly for mixtures where ΔBP is less than 25 K.
Blunder 5: Mislabeling Candle Burning as Strictly Physical or Strictly Chemical The Mistake: Categorizing candle burning as only physical (because wax melts) or only chemical (because wax burns).
The Correction: Candle burning is the classic textbook example of BOTH changes happening simultaneously. Melting of solid wax is a physical change; combustion of wax vapor at the wick is a chemical change.

15 High-Yield Rapid Revision Anchors

  1. Pure Substance: Consists of a single type of constituent particle with invariant chemical composition (e.g., pure water, gold).
  2. Mixture: Physical combination of two or more substances in arbitrary proportions, retaining their original properties.
  3. Homogeneous vs Heterogeneous: Homogeneous has uniform composition and single phase (brine, air); heterogeneous has visible phase boundaries (sand in water).
  4. True Solution: Homogeneous mixture with solute particle size < 1 nm (10⁻⁹ m); does not scatter light; passes through filter paper.
  5. Alloys: Solid-in-solid homogeneous mixtures of metals (e.g., Brass = 70% Cu + 30% Zn) that retain constituent properties.
  6. Concentration: Mass % = [Mass of solute ÷ (Mass of solute + Mass of solvent)] × 100.
  7. Solubility: Maximum grams of solute dissolved in 100 g solvent at a given temperature; increases with temperature for most solids.
  8. Suspension: Heterogeneous mixture with particle size > 100 nm; unstable; settles on standing; scatters light when unsettled.
  9. Colloid: Heterogeneous system with particle size 1 nm to 100 nm; stable; exhibits Tyndall scattering and Brownian motion.
  10. Tyndall Effect: Scattering of visible light rays by colloidal particles, rendering the light path visible (John Tyndall, 1869).
  11. 8 Colloid Classes: Aerosol (fog/smoke), Foam (shaving cream), Emulsion (milk), Sol (milk of magnesia), Solid Foam (sponge), Gel (jelly), Solid Sol (gemstones).
  12. Elements vs Compounds: Elements (metals, non-metals, metalloids) cannot be chemically decomposed; compounds (H₂O, FeS) combine in fixed mass ratios.
  13. Sublimation: Direct phase change from solid to gas on heating; separates NH₄Cl, camphor, naphthalene, or iodine from non-sublimable salts.
  14. Chromatography: Separates solutes in the same solvent based on differential solubility and capillary travel speed.
  15. Simple vs Fractional Distillation: Simple distillation requires ΔBP > 25 K; Fractional distillation (with packed glass beads) is required when ΔBP < 25 K (liquid air gases, petroleum).
