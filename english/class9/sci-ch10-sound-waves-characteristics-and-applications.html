2.1 What is Sound and How is It Produced?

Sound is a form of mechanical energy that creates the sensation of hearing in our ears. Just like light and heat, sound is an energy form that obeys the universal Law of Conservation of Energy: sound energy is neither created from nothing nor destroyed; it is transformed from other forms of energy (principally mechanical kinetic energy).

The Universal Cause of Sound: Vibrations. Every sound in the universe originates from rapid back-and-forth or to-and-fro oscillatory motions called vibrations. When mechanical work is done on an elastic object—such as plucking a guitar string, striking a metallic bell, blowing through an air pipe, or speaking—the kinetic energy imparts oscillatory motion to the molecules of the object:

  • Vibrating Strings: In stringed musical instruments such as the sitar, guitar, veena, and violin, stretched strings are plucked, bowed, or struck. The high-speed lateral vibration sets adjacent air molecules into rhythmic oscillation.
  • Vibrating Membranes / Skins: In percussion instruments such as the tabla, mridangam, dholak, and drum, a tightly stretched flexible leather or plastic membrane vibrates up and down when struck, compressing and rarefying the surrounding air.
  • Vibrating Air Columns: In wind instruments like the flute, shehnai, trumpet, and harmonium, air blown past a sharp reed or aperture creates stationary standing waves within an internal resonant air column.
  • Vibrating Tuning Fork: When the steel prong of a tuning fork is struck against a soft rubber pad, the prongs vibrate outward and inward hundreds of times per second. Touching the vibrating prong to the surface of water in a beaker causes dramatic ripples and water droplets to splash outward, proving physical oscillatory motion.
  • Human Voice (Vocal Cords in the Larynx): Sound in human speech is generated within the voice box or larynx situated at the top of the windpipe (trachea). Two stretched fibrous muscular bands called vocal cords leave a narrow slit (glottis) between them. When the lungs force a stream of air through this slit, the vocal cords vibrate rhythmically, producing sound. Muscular tension alters the thickness and tension of the vocal cords, modulating the frequency and pitch of our voice.
Everyday Laboratory Proof • How We Know Sound is Vibration Take a lightweight pith ball or table tennis ball suspended from a retort stand by a silk thread. Strike a tuning fork firmly on a rubber pad and gently bring one vibrating prong into contact with the ball. The ball is violently kicked away repeatedly! This direct kinetic kick proves that sound production is accompanied by real, physical mechanical vibrations.

2.2 Propagation of Sound & The Material Medium Requirement

When an object vibrates, it does not shoot air molecules across the room like bullets. Instead, it creates a local mechanical disturbance in the medium immediately adjacent to it:

  1. Disturbance vs Particle Motion: The vibrating object pushes on the adjacent layer of air particles. These particles move forward, collide with neighboring particles, transfer their momentum and kinetic energy, and then rebound back toward their initial equilibrium positions due to the elastic restoring forces of the medium.
  2. Chain Collision Dynamics: The second layer of particles in turn collides with the third layer, and so on. In this way, the mechanical disturbance travels progressively forward through the medium from the source to the listener's ear, while each individual particle merely oscillates back and forth around its own mean position.

Sound Requires a Material Medium to Travel: Because sound is a mechanical wave that propagates purely through intermolecular collisions and elastic restoring forces, it cannot travel through empty space where no particles exist. Sound requires a solid, liquid, or gas to propagate. It cannot propagate through a vacuum.

Figure 10.2: Experimental demonstration of sound requiring a material medium. The sound of the electric bell vanishes when air is evacuated from the bell jar.

The Bell-Jar Vacuum Demonstration (Robert Boyle, 1660) An electric bell is suspended inside an airtight glass bell jar mounted on the smooth plate of a vacuum pump. When the electrical switch is pressed, the hammer strikes the gong repeatedly, and a loud ringing sound is heard outside.

Next, the vacuum pump is turned on to evacuate the air from inside the jar. As air is pumped out, the sound becomes progressively fainter and fainter. When a high vacuum is achieved, the hammer can still be clearly seen striking the metallic gong, but absolutely zero sound is heard! When air is allowed back into the bell jar, the ringing sound is immediately restored. This conclusively proves that sound cannot propagate through a vacuum.
Exam Classic PYQ • Astronauts on the Moon Question: Why can two astronauts on the surface of the Moon not talk directly to each other even if they are standing face to face?
Topper Answer: The Moon has no atmosphere (vacuum). Because sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave that requires a material medium to propagate, sound waves produced by an astronaut's vocal cords cannot travel through the lunar vacuum. Therefore, astronauts must communicate using radio waves, which are electromagnetic waves that do not require any material medium for propagation.

2.3 Wave Nature of Sound • Longitudinal vs Transverse Waves

A wave is a periodic disturbance produced in a medium that travels through the medium transferring energy and momentum from one point to another without causing any permanent displacement or mass transport of the medium itself.

Waves are classified into two primary categories based on the relationship between the direction of particle oscillation and the direction of wave propagation:

A. Longitudinal Waves (The True Nature of Sound in Air)

A wave in which the particles of the medium oscillate back and forth parallel to the direction in which the wave travels is called a longitudinal wave.

  • Compressions (C): When the vibrating object moves outward (forward), it pushes and compresses the air in front of it. This creates a localized region where the particles are crowded close together, resulting in high density and high pressure.
  • Rarefactions (R): When the vibrating object moves inward (backward), it creates a region of reduced pressure. Surrounding air particles expand into this void, resulting in a region where particles are spread farther apart, characterized by low density and low pressure.
  • As the source oscillates continuously, a continuous train of alternating compressions and rarefactions travels through the medium. Hence, sound wave propagation in air is literally the propagation of density and pressure variations.
  • Slinky Spring Analogy: Take a long helical coil spring (slinky). Hold one end fixed and push and pull the free end rhythmically along the length of the spring. You will see dense bunches of coils (compressions) and stretched-out loose coils (rarefactions) moving forward along the spring. This is a longitudinal wave.

B. Transverse Waves

A wave in which the particles of the medium oscillate up and down perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of wave propagation is called a transverse wave.

  • Transverse waves travel in the form of alternating crests (elevations / peaks above the mean equilibrium position) and troughs (depressions / valleys below the mean equilibrium position).
  • Examples: Ripples formed on the surface of water when a pebble is dropped; waves traveling along a stretched horizontal rope when one end is shaken up and down; light and all electromagnetic waves (which travel as transverse oscillations of electric and magnetic fields).
  • Note for Secondary Science: Sound waves in gases and liquids are always purely longitudinal because fluids cannot sustain shear stresses. In elastic solids, sound can propagate as both longitudinal waves (compression waves) and transverse waves (shear waves).

2.4 Characteristics of a Sound Wave & Graphical Wave Kinematics

A sound wave is graphically represented by plotting density or pressure on the vertical axis against distance or time on the horizontal axis (as shown in Figure 10.1). On this graph:

  • The positive peaks (crests) represent regions of maximum density and pressure, which correspond to Compressions (C).
  • The negative valleys (troughs) represent regions of minimum density and pressure, which correspond to Rarefactions (R).
  • The center horizontal axis represents the average or normal undisturbed atmospheric density and pressure of the medium.

A sound wave is uniquely characterized by five fundamental physical quantities:

1. Wavelength (λ, Greek letter Lambda)

Definition: The distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions of a sound wave is called its wavelength. On a transverse wave graph, it is the distance between two successive crests or two successive troughs.
SI Unit: Metre (m). Symbol: λ.

2. Frequency (ν, Greek letter Nu, or f)

Definition: The number of complete oscillations or cycles executed by a particle of the medium per second (or the number of compressions or rarefactions passing a given point per unit time) is called the frequency of the wave.
SI Unit: Hertz (Hz), named in honor of German physicist Heinrich Rudolf Hertz (1857–1894), who first conclusively proved the existence of electromagnetic waves.
1 Hz = 1 oscillation per second = 1 s−¹. Larger engineering units include the kilohertz (1 kHz = 1000 Hz) and megahertz (1 MHz = 10&sup6; Hz).

3. Time Period (T)

Definition: The time taken by a particle of the medium to complete one full oscillation (or the time taken by two consecutive compressions to pass a fixed point) is called the time period of the wave.
SI Unit: Second (s). Symbol: T.
Fundamental Inverse Relation: Frequency and time period are reciprocals of each other:
T = 1 / ν   ⇔   ν = 1 / T

4. Amplitude (A)

Definition: The maximum magnitude of displacement of the particles of the medium from their original mean equilibrium position on either side is called the amplitude of the sound wave.
SI Unit: Metre (m) for particle displacement; Pascal (Pa or N/m²) when describing pressure wave amplitude.
Physical Significance: Amplitude is a direct measure of the energy carried by the wave. Striking a drum lightly produces small amplitude vibrations (soft sound), while striking it with greater force imparts large mechanical energy, creating large amplitude vibrations (loud sound).

5. Wave Speed (v)

Definition: The distance traveled by a point on a sound wave (such as a compression crest) per unit time is called the speed of sound.
SI Unit: Metre per second (m/s or m s−¹).

Mathematical Derivation • Universal Wave Speed Equation By definition of linear speed:
Speed (v) = Distance traveled / Time taken
In the duration of one complete time period (t = T), the wave travels a distance exactly equal to one wavelength (λ).
Substituting these fundamental quantities:
v = λ / T
Since frequency ν = 1 / T, we replace 1 / T with ν:
v = ν × λ
Speed = Frequency × Wavelength. This master equation applies universally to all mechanical and electromagnetic waves in nature!

Perceptual Auditory Characteristics of Sound

Human ears evaluate sounds based on three distinct auditory sensations:

  • 1. Pitch (Governed by Frequency): Pitch is the subjective sensation by which our brain interprets the frequency of sound.
    • High Frequency = High Pitch (Shrill / Sharp sound): A whistle, violin, buzzing mosquito, flute, bird chirping, and child's or woman's voice have high frequencies and hence high pitch.
    • Low Frequency = Low Pitch (Grave / Bass sound): A bass drum, cello, lion's roar, thunderclap, and adult man's voice have low frequencies and hence low pitch.
  • 2. Loudness (Governed by Amplitude): Loudness is the auditory sensation that enables us to distinguish a loud sound from a faint (soft) sound.
    • Loudness is directly proportional to the square of the amplitude of vibration:
    Loudness ∝ (Amplitude)²
    • If the amplitude of a sound wave is doubled (2×), its loudness increases fourfold (2² = 4×)!
    • Loudness is expressed logarithmically on the decibel (dB) scale. A faint whisper is ∼30 dB, normal conversation is ∼60 dB, heavy city traffic is ∼80 dB, and sounds exceeding 80 dB become physically distressing and noise-polluting, with 120 dB marking the human threshold of pain.
  • 3. Quality or Timbre (Governed by Waveform & Harmonics): Quality (timbre) is that auditory characteristic which enables us to distinguish between two sounds having the exact same loudness and the exact same pitch, but produced by two different musical instruments or individuals.
    • If a piano, a flute, and a violin all play the musical note middle C (261.6 Hz) at the exact same loudness, we can instantly tell which instrument is playing. This is because no real musical instrument produces a pure single frequency. Instead, it produces a fundamental frequency accompanied by a unique mixture of higher secondary frequencies called overtones or harmonics, creating a distinctive complex wave shape.
  • Tone vs Note:
    • Tone: A sound consisting of a single pure frequency (such as the sound produced by an ideal tuning fork).
    • Note: A sound produced by a mixture of several distinct frequencies that is pleasant to hear (such as music played on an acoustic instrument).
  • Loudness vs Intensity (Crucial Scientific Distinction):
    • Intensity of Sound: The amount of sound energy passing per second through a unit area held perpendicular to the direction of propagation (SI unit: W/m²). Intensity is an objective physical quantity that can be measured precisely using electronic sensors, regardless of whether a human ear is listening.
    • Loudness: The subjective physiological response of the human ear to sound intensity. Two sounds of identical physical intensity (W/m²) may not be perceived as equally loud if the ear is more sensitive to one frequency (e.g., human ears are far more sensitive to 1000–3000 Hz than to 50 Hz).

2.5 Speed of Sound in Different Media & Sonic Booms

The speed of sound depends on the fundamental physical properties of the medium through which it travels—chiefly its elasticity (how quickly particles spring back after displacement) and its density.

Speed Hierarchy Across States of Matter:

Speed in Solids > Speed in Liquids > Speed in Gases

In solids, atoms and molecules are packed tightly together with powerful intermolecular forces and high elasticity (Young's modulus). When an atom vibrates, it immediately exerts force on its immediate neighbor, transmitting the compression disturbance with extreme rapidity. In liquids, particles are spaced farther apart with weaker bonds, so sound travels slower. In gases, molecules are widely separated and only interact during random collisions, making sound propagation slowest.

State of Matter Substance / Medium Speed at 25°C (m/s) Physical Reason & Exam Context

Solid Nickel / Steel / Iron 6040 / 5960 / 5950 m/s Railway tracks carry sound of distant approaching trains long before air.
Solid Glass (Flint) 3980 m/s Rigid silicate lattice allows rapid acoustic transmission.
Liquid Sea Water 1531 m/s Dissolved salts increase bulk modulus; standard speed used in naval SONAR.
Liquid Pure Distilled Water 1498 m/s Submarines and whales use underwater acoustic communication.
Liquid Ethanol 1207 m/s Lower density and elasticity than water.
Gas Hydrogen Gas 1284 m/s Lightest gas molecules travel at extremely high thermal velocities.
Gas Air (at 0°C) 331 m/s Standard thermodynamic baseline at freezing temperature.
Gas Air (at 22°C / Room Temp) 344 m/s Standard benchmark speed for school numerical calculations.
Gas Oxygen Gas (at 25°C) 316 m/s Heavier diatomic molecules (O&sub2; = 32 u) compared to N&sub2; (28 u).

Environmental Factors Affecting Speed of Sound in Air:

  • Temperature: The speed of sound in a gas is directly proportional to the square root of its absolute temperature (v ∝ √T). As temperature rises, gas molecules possess greater kinetic energy and collide more frequently, transmitting compression waves faster. In air, sound speed increases by approximately 0.61 m/s for every 1°C rise in temperature (v ≈ 331 + 0.61 × t °C).
  • Humidity: Sound travels faster in humid (moist) air than in dry air. Water vapor (H&sub2;O, molecular mass 18 u) is lighter than dry air (predominantly N&sub2; 28 u and O&sub2; 32 u, average molecular mass ∼29 u). Therefore, moist air has a lower density than dry air at the same pressure, allowing acoustic disturbances to propagate faster.
  • Pressure (No Direct Effect at Constant Temperature): Changes in atmospheric pressure at constant temperature do not change the speed of sound because gas density changes in direct proportion to pressure, leaving the ratio P / ρ constant.

Supersonic Speed & Sonic Booms

When an object moves through air at a speed greater than the local speed of sound (>344 m/s or >1238 km/h), it is said to travel at supersonic speed (e.g., supersonic fighter jets, rifle bullets, space shuttle reentry vehicles).

The Physics of a Sonic Boom: When a supersonic aircraft flies through the sky, it moves faster than the pressure waves it generates. The individual sound wavefronts cannot escape ahead of the aircraft; instead, they pile up and coalesce behind the aircraft into an intensely compressed conical shock wave (Mach cone). When this high-energy shock wave sweeps across the ground, it produces an explosive, thunderous blast called a sonic boom. The sudden pressure jump can shatter window panes, crack plaster walls, and damage buildings.

2.6 Reflection of Sound Waves & Echo Mechanics

Just like light waves, when a sound wave encounters a hard surface (such as a concrete wall, cliff face, metal board, or mountain side), it bounces back into the same medium. This phenomenon is called the reflection of sound.

Sound waves obey the exact same two fundamental Laws of Reflection as light:

  1. The angle of incidence (∠i) is always equal to the angle of reflection (∠r) with respect to the normal at the point of incidence (∠i = ∠r).
  2. The incident sound wave, the reflected sound wave, and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same geometric plane.

Echo: Definition & Persistence of Hearing

If we shout or clap near a suitable tall reflecting object—such as a high building, a rocky cliff face, or a deep well—we hear our own sound repeated a short time later. This reflected sound heard distinctly after the original sound has ceased is called an echo.

Persistence of Hearing • The Biological Condition for Echoes The sensation of sound persists in the human brain for approximately 0.1 second (1/10th of a second). This physiological retention time is called the persistence of hearing.

If a reflected sound arrives at our ears before 0.1 s has elapsed, the human brain cannot distinguish it as a separate sound; it simply blurs into the original sensation. Therefore, to hear a distinct, separate echo, the time interval between the original sound and its reflection must be at least 0.1 second (t ≥ 0.1 s).
Mathematical Derivation • Minimum Distance for Hearing an Echo Let the distance between the source of sound (speaker) and the reflecting obstacle be d.
The sound must travel forward from the source to the obstacle (d) and then bounce back from the obstacle to the listener's ear (d).
Total distance traveled by the sound wave = 2 × d.

Using the fundamental kinematic relation:
Total Distance = Speed of sound × Time taken
2d = v × t   ⇒   d = (v × t) / 2

Taking the standard speed of sound in air at 22°C as v = 344 m/s and minimum time t = 0.1 s:
dmin = (344 m/s × 0.1 s) / 2 = 34.4 / 2 = 17.2 metres

Universal Takeaway: To hear a clear, distinct echo in air at room temperature (22°C), the reflecting surface must be at an absolute minimum distance of 17.2 metres from the source. At 0°C (where v = 331 m/s), the minimum distance is 16.55 m.

Multiple Echoes & The Rolling of Thunder

When sound encounters multiple reflecting surfaces situated at varying distances, it undergoes successive reflections, producing a series of repeated echoes.
Rolling of Thunder: The prolonged rumbling or rolling sound of thunder during a storm is caused by the successive reflections of sound from multiple layers of clouds and different elevated terrestrial landforms (hills, buildings, ground).

2.7 Reverberation & Architectural Acoustics

In an enclosed hall, auditorium, or classroom, a sound created at the stage reflects repeatedly off the ceiling, walls, and floor. Because the distances between opposite walls are typically less than 17.2 m, the reflections arrive in rapid succession at intervals shorter than 0.1 s.

The persistence of sound in an enclosed space as a result of repeated, overlapping reflections after the source has stopped vibrating is called reverberation.

  • When Reverberation is Excessive: If reverberation is too long, the trailing reflections of one spoken syllable overlap with the subsequent syllable, transforming speech and music into an unintelligible, muddy roar.
  • Acoustic Reverberation Time: Acoustical engineers design halls so that reverberation is neither too long (muddy) nor zero (acoustically "dead"). A modest reverberation time (∼1 to 1.5 s) gives musical performances richness and warmth.

Engineering Methods to Reduce Unwanted Reverberation:

  1. Sound-Absorbing Wall Linings: Covering the walls with highly porous acoustic materials such as compressed fiberboard, perforated acoustic plaster, glass wool, or acoustic foam. The porous air pockets trap sound waves, converting acoustic energy into microscopic thermal friction.
  2. Heavy Draperies & Curtains: Hanging thick, pleated velvet curtains along walls and over windows to absorb incident sound reflections.
  3. Upholstered Seating: Installing plush, padded, fabric-upholstered seats that absorb sound whether an audience member is seated or not, maintaining consistent hall acoustics.
  4. Rough-Textured Floor Carpets: Laying heavy carpets on aisles and floors to prevent hard specular floor reflections and dampen footstep noise.

Practical Applications Utilizing the Reflection of Sound:

  • Megaphones, Horns, and Trumpets: These instruments feature a tube terminating in an expanding conical flare. Multiple internal reflections direct the expanding sound waves primarily forward into a narrow beam, preventing acoustic energy from spreading omnidirectionally.
  • Stethoscope: A vital medical diagnostic instrument used by doctors to listen to internal heartbeats and lung sounds. Sound waves generated by the patient's heart strike the chest diaphragm and travel up through rubber or plastic tubing via multiple internal reflections directly into the physician's earpieces without significant loss of acoustic energy.
  • Curved Ceilings of Concert Halls: The ceilings of auditoriums, cinema halls, and conference halls are deliberately designed with concave parabolic curves (Figure 10.4). Sound reflecting off the curved surface spreads out evenly across the entire hall, ensuring that listeners in the back rows hear with the same clarity as listeners in the front.
  • Curved Soundboards Behind the Speaker: A concave parabolic acoustic board is mounted behind the podium or stage. The speaker stands at the geometric focus; sound reflecting off the concave board emerges as a parallel beam directed forward across the audience.

2.8 Audible Range of Hearing • Infrasound and Ultrasound

The human auditory system is sensitive only to a specific frequency band. Sound waves outside this frequency window cannot be detected by normal human ears, regardless of how intense they are:

Acoustic Category Frequency Range Natural Sources & Organisms Key Physical Properties
Infrasound (Infrasonic) Below 20 Hz (< 20 s−¹) Earthquake P-waves, volcanic eruptions, ocean swells, elephants, blue whales, rhinoceroses (5 Hz). Very long wavelengths; minimal atmospheric absorption; travels vast distances through earth and ocean.
Audible Sound 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 Hz – 20 kHz) Human vocal cords, speech, music, birds, thunder, common environmental sounds. Optimal human cochlear sensitivity lies between 1000 Hz and 3000 Hz. Hearing range narrows with age.
Ultrasound (Ultrasonic) Above 20,000 Hz (> 20 kHz) Bats, dolphins, porpoises, moths, dogs (up to 40 kHz), children under 5 (up to 25 kHz). Extremely short wavelengths; travels along narrow directed paths without spreading; high penetration power.
Curious Biology • Why Dogs Bark Before Earthquakes Strike Earthquakes generate low-frequency infrasonic precursor waves (below 20 Hz) several seconds or minutes before the destructive high-amplitude transverse surface waves hit. Animals like elephants, dogs, and birds easily detect these infrasound vibrations, alerting them to flee or show distress well before human beings realize an earthquake is occurring!

2.9 Technological & Medical Applications of Ultrasound

Ultrasound consists of high-frequency acoustic waves (>20,000 Hz). Because of their very high frequency, ultrasonic waves have extremely short wavelengths. Consequently, they can travel along sharp, narrow, well-defined straight paths without suffering significant beam spreading or diffraction around obstacles. This unique property makes ultrasound invaluable across modern industry and medicine:

1. Industrial Cleaning of Inaccessible Parts

Ultrasound is routinely used to clean intricate mechanical parts, spiral tubes, odd-shaped components, precision electronic assemblies, and jewelry. The objects are immersed in an ultrasonic cleaning tank filled with a specialized detergent solution. High-frequency ultrasonic waves (∼40 kHz) transmitted through the liquid produce millions of microscopic vacuum cavitation bubbles. These bubbles violently collapse (implode), generating intense localized micro-shockwaves that blast away grease, dirt, oil, and dust particles from the most inaccessible internal crevices.

2. Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) & Metal Crack Detection

Massive metallic components used in construction—such as aircraft wings, bridge girders, high-speed rail tracks, and nuclear reactor pressure vessels—must be completely free of internal invisible flaws, cracks, and gas cavities.
Ultrasound waves are passed through one side of the metal block, while sensitive electronic detectors are placed on the opposite side:

  • If the metallic block is completely homogeneous and flawless, the ultrasound passes straight through unimpeded and registers full intensity on all detectors.
  • If there is even an invisible microscopic crack or air void inside the block, the ultrasound wave is instantly reflected back from the defect boundary. The detector placed directly behind that region shows a drop or absence of transmitted signal, pinpointing the exact internal location and depth of the defect without cutting or destroying the expensive component!

3. Medical Echocardiography (ECHO)

Ultrasonic pulses are directed into the human chest and reflected from various cardiac structures (heart walls, atria, ventricles, and heart valves). The reflected echoes are captured by an electronic transducer and processed by a computer to construct real-time dynamic moving images of the beating heart. This technique is called echocardiography and is used to diagnose valve regurgitation, septal defects, and cardiac muscle damage.

4. Medical Ultrasonography (USG Scanner)

Ultrasonography is a safe, radiation-free medical imaging technique used to examine internal soft organs such as the liver, gallbladder, pancreas, kidneys, spleen, and uterus:

  • A handheld probe (transducer) containing piezoelectric crystals is pressed against the patient's skin (coated with acoustic coupling gel).
  • The transducer sends ultrasonic pulses into the body. Whenever a pulse crosses an anatomical boundary between tissues of differing acoustic densities (e.g., between kidney parenchyma and a calcified kidney stone), a portion of the wave is reflected back as an echo.
  • The computer converts these reflected echo signals into electrical voltages that produce high-resolution cross-sectional images of the internal organs on a monitor screen.
  • Obstetric Imaging: Ultrasonography is universally used during pregnancy to monitor fetal growth, calculate gestational age, and detect congenital abnormalities without exposing the delicate fetus to harmful ionizing X-rays.

5. Ultrasonic Lithotripsy (Kidney Stone Pulverization)

High-energy focused ultrasound shockwaves are targeted with pinpoint accuracy onto renal calculi (kidney stones) or gallstones inside the patient's body. The repetitive mechanical stress pulverizes the hard stones into microscopic, sand-sized fragments that pass harmlessly out of the body through urine, completely avoiding invasive surgery!

2.10 SONAR • Sound Navigation and Ranging

SONAR is an acronym that stands for SOund NAvigation and Ranging. It is an electro-acoustic apparatus installed on sea vessels to detect underwater obstacles, locate submarines, and measure the depth of the ocean.

2 × d (Down & Up) Time Elapsed between Pulse & Echo = t Speed of Ultrasound in Seawater = v (~1531 m/s) 2d = v × t d = (v × t) ÷ 2

Figure 10.5: Working mechanism of SONAR (SOund Navigation And Ranging). Transmitter (T) emits ultrasonic waves and Receiver (R) detects the echo to compute distance via 2d = v × t.

Architecture and Working of a SONAR System:

  1. Transmitter (Tx): Mounted on the ship's underside (hull), the transmitter consists of piezoelectric crystals that convert electrical oscillations into powerful, directed beams of high-frequency ultrasonic waves.
  2. Underwater Propagation: The ultrasound pulse travels downwards through seawater at speed v (typically ∼1531 m/s).
  3. Reflection: When the beam strikes an underwater boundary—such as the ocean floor, a submerged submarine, an iceberg, a sunken wreck, or a school of fish—it reflects back as an acoustic echo.
  4. Receiver / Detector (Rx): Mounted near the transmitter, the receiver captures the reflected ultrasonic waves and converts them into electrical signals that are recorded on an electronic chronometer.
The Echo-Ranging Mathematical Equation Let the depth of the seabed or distance of the underwater obstacle be d.
The ultrasonic pulse must travel downwards to the obstacle (d) and return upwards to the ship (d).
Therefore, total distance traveled = 2 × d.
If the speed of ultrasound in seawater is v and the total round-trip time recorded between transmission and echo reception is t:

2d = v × t   ⇒   d = (v × t) / 2

This method of measuring distance using reflected ultrasound pulses is called echo-ranging.

Natural Echolocation in Animals (Nature's Living SONAR)

  • Bats: Bats are nocturnal mammals that hunt flying insects in complete darkness. A bat emits very high-frequency ultrasonic squeaks (>50 kHz to 120 kHz). These sound waves bounce off flying insects or obstacles and return to the bat's specialized ears. By analyzing the time delay, frequency shift (Doppler effect), and loudness of the returning echoes, the bat's brain calculates the exact distance, flight direction, and size of the insect with sub-millimeter precision!
  • Marine Mammals: Porpoises, dolphins, and killer whales emit rapid ultrasonic click trains to navigate murky waters and hunt schools of fish via echolocation.

2.11 Anatomy & Working Mechanism of the Human Ear

The human ear is an extraordinary biological transducer that converts acoustic pressure variations in air into mechanical lever vibrations, then into fluid pressure waves, and ultimately into electrochemical nerve impulses interpreted by the brain as sound.

Figure 10.6: Complete anatomical cross-section of the human ear displaying the outer ear collector, middle ear mechanical amplifier ossicles, and inner ear cochlear electrical transducer.

Anatomically, the human ear is divided into three distinct compartments:

1. The Outer Ear (Collector & Funnel)

  • Pinna (Auricle): The visible, curved cartilaginous flap on the side of the head. Its funnel-like shape collects sound waves from all surrounding directions and channels them into the auditory canal.
  • Auditory Canal (Ear Canal): A tube approximately 2.5 cm long lined with fine hairs and ceruminous (wax) glands that direct the collected acoustic waves inwards while trapping dust and foreign particles.
  • Tympanic Membrane (Eardrum): A delicate, tightly stretched circular fibrous membrane situated at the inner termination of the auditory canal. When a compression arrives, the external pressure increases, pushing the eardrum inward. When a rarefaction arrives, external pressure decreases, drawing the eardrum outward. Thus, the eardrum vibrates back and forth in exact synchrony with the incoming sound wave.

2. The Middle Ear (Mechanical Amplifier & Equalizer)

  • The Three Auditory Ossicles (Bones): The middle ear is a small, air-filled cavity containing the three smallest bones in the human body, arranged in an articulated lever chain:
    1. Malleus (Hammer): The handle of the hammer is firmly attached to the inner surface of the vibrating eardrum.
    2. Incus (Anvil): The intermediate bone linked between the hammer and the stirrup.
    3. Stapes (Stirrup): The innermost stirrup-shaped bone whose base plate fits snugly against the membrane of the oval window leading to the inner ear.
  • The 20× to 30× Pressure Amplification Mechanism: Vibrations arriving at the large eardrum area (∼55 mm²) are concentrated down onto the tiny oval window footprint (∼3.2 mm²). Since Pressure = Force / Area, this mechanical lever advantage amplifies the acoustic pressure fluctuations by approximately 20 to 30 times, enabling the sound to efficiently drive the dense, viscous fluid of the inner ear!
  • Eustachian Tube: A narrow duct connecting the middle ear cavity to the back of the nasopharynx (throat). It equalizes air pressure on both sides of the eardrum, preventing eardrum rupture during rapid atmospheric changes (such as takeoff in an airplane or descending into deep tunnels).

3. The Inner Ear (Liquid Transduction & Neural Processing)

  • Cochlea: A delicate, snail-shell-shaped spiral bony canal filled with viscous fluid (perilymph and endolymph).
  • Organ of Corti & Sensory Hair Cells: Inside the cochlea, resting on the basilar membrane, are tens of thousands of microscopic microscopic sensory hair cells. When the stapes pushes against the oval window, it generates hydrodynamic pressure ripples in the cochlear fluid. These ripples physically bend the hair cells.
  • Auditory Nerve Transduction: The mechanical bending of hair cells depolarizes their membranes, generating electrical action potentials (nerve impulses). These electrical impulses travel along the auditory nerve directly into the auditory cortex located in the temporal lobe of the brain, which decodes the signals as speech, music, or environmental sounds.
  • Semicircular Canals: Three fluid-filled loops situated above the cochlea that monitor head position and angular acceleration, maintaining physical balance and dynamic equilibrium.

Activity 10.1: Striking a tuning fork, touching water surface in a beaker, and observing splashes
Step 1: Scientific Objective & Principle

To experimentally prove that sound is produced by physical mechanical vibrations and that a vibrating body possesses real kinetic energy capable of doing mechanical work on adjacent matter.

Step 2: Experimental Setup & Materials

• Steel tuning fork (frequency 256 Hz or 512 Hz).
• Soft rubber striker pad.
• Glass beaker filled to the brim with clean water.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Strike one prong of the tuning fork firmly against the rubber pad to set it vibrating.
2. Hold the fork near your ear to confirm the emission of a clear acoustic tone.
3. While still vibrating, touch the tip of one prong lightly to the surface of water in the beaker.
4. In a second step, plunge both vibrating prongs slightly below the surface of the water.

Step 4: Observations & Physics Analysis

Visual Observation: The moment the vibrating prong touches the water, dramatic concentric ripples spread across the surface, and fine water droplets are violently splashed out of the beaker.
Physical Reason: The prongs oscillate back and forth hundreds of times per second. Their mechanical momentum and kinetic energy are directly transferred to the water molecules at the interface, overcoming the surface tension of water and causing splashes.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Practical Tip

Conclusion: Sound production is strictly inseparable from physical mechanical vibrations.
Teacher's Tip: Never strike a tuning fork against hard glass or a metal bench; this can deform the steel prongs and alter the calibrated frequency permanently. Always strike on rubber!

Activity 10.2: Suspending a table tennis or pith ball and touching it with a vibrating tuning fork
Step 1: Scientific Objective & Principle

To demonstrate that the mechanical vibrations producing sound involve macroscopic back-and-forth physical displacement by using a lightweight suspended ball as an oscillation amplifier.

Step 2: Experimental Setup & Materials

• Lightweight table tennis ball or dry thermocol/pith ball.
• Fine silk or nylon thread (length ∼40 cm).
• Retort stand with clamp.
• Tuning fork and rubber pad.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Suspend the ball vertically from the clamp using the thread so that it hangs stationary.
2. Strike the tuning fork on the rubber pad to produce sound.
3. Gently bring the vibrating prong into side contact with the resting suspended ball.

Step 4: Observations & Physics Analysis

Observation: The suspended ball is kicked violently outward to a considerable angle. As it swings back, it is kicked away repeatedly until the tuning fork ceases vibrating.
Physics Explanation: The rapid outward stroke of the vibrating prong exerts an impulsive contact force on the ball (F = Δp / Δt), imparting kinetic energy and angular displacement.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Key Takeaway

Conclusion: Although the vibration amplitude of a tuning fork is too microscopic to see clearly with the naked eye, its kinetic impact on a lightweight object renders the oscillatory motion vividly visible.

Activity 10.3: Stretching a rubber band and plucking it to observe sound and vibration
Step 1: Scientific Objective & Principle

To explore the relationship between mechanical tension, oscillatory motion, and sound production in an elastic stretched filament.

Step 2: Experimental Setup & Materials

• Two stationary points (or fingers of both hands).

Step 3: Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Stretch the rubber band firmly between your thumb and forefinger.
2. Pluck the rubber band at its midpoint and release it.
3. Observe its visual appearance and listen closely to the sound produced.
4. Stretch the band tighter and pluck again.

Observation: The plucked band appears blurred due to high-frequency lateral oscillation, and a distinct humming sound is heard. When stretched tighter, the pitch becomes noticeably sharper (higher frequency). When you touch the band with your finger to halt the vibration, the sound stops instantaneously.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Key Takeaway

Conclusion: Sound is emitted only as long as the object vibrates. Stopping the vibration terminates the sound immediately.

Activity 10.5: Slinky spring demonstration of longitudinal wave compressions and rarefactions
Step 1: Scientific Objective & Principle

To visualize the macroscopic mechanics of a longitudinal wave where particle oscillation is parallel to the direction of wave propagation, showing compressions and rarefactions.

Step 2: Experimental Setup & Materials

• Long, flexible helical wire spring (slinky).
• Smooth floor or long tabletop.
• Two experimenters holding opposite ends.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Stretch the slinky horizontally between two persons along the floor.
2. Person A holds their end stationary. Person B gives a sharp, periodic push and pull along the axis of the spring.
3. Mark one individual coil with a small piece of bright red tape and observe its motion.

Step 4: Observations & Physics Analysis

Observation: Bunches of closely spaced coils (Compressions) and spread-out loosely spaced coils (Rarefactions) travel progressively down the length of the spring.
Motion of Marked Coil: The coil tagged with red tape does NOT travel to the opposite end. It merely oscillates back and forth about its mean resting position parallel to the length of the spring.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Key Takeaway

Conclusion: In a longitudinal wave, the disturbance travels through the medium, but the particles of the medium merely oscillate locally along the direction of wave travel.

Activity 10.6: Slinky spring demonstration of transverse waves (crests and troughs)
Step 1: Scientific Objective & Principle

To demonstrate a transverse wave where the particles of the medium oscillate perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation.

Step 2: Experimental Setup & Materials

• Long slinky or flexible rope.
• Fixed hook or partner holding the opposite end.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Stretch the slinky or rope horizontally.
2. Shake the free end vigorously up and down (or side to side) at right angles to the stretched length.
3. Track the movement of wave humps along the spring.

Step 4: Observations & Physics Analysis

Observation: Wavy ripples consisting of alternating peaks (crests) and valleys (troughs) move forward along the spring from your hand to the far end. Any single coil moves purely up and down (perpendicular to the forward motion of the wave pulse).

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Key Takeaway

Conclusion: Demonstrates the fundamental geometric distinction between transverse waves (⊥ oscillation) and longitudinal sound waves (∥ oscillation).

Activity 10.7: The Bell-Jar vacuum experiment showing sound requires a material medium
Step 1: Scientific Objective & Principle

To prove experimentally that sound cannot propagate through a vacuum and strictly requires a material medium (air molecules) to transfer acoustic energy.

Step 2: Experimental Setup & Materials

• Heavy glass bell jar with ground-glass base rim.
• Electric bell suspended by insulated wires passing through an airtight cork.
• Battery and push-key switch.
• Rotary vacuum pump connected to the base plate outlet.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Place the electric bell inside the bell jar and make the rim airtight with vacuum grease.
2. Close the electrical circuit; note the loud ringing sound of the bell.
3. Turn on the vacuum pump to evacuate air progressively from inside the jar.
4. Observe the loudness of sound as the internal air pressure drops.
5. Re-admit atmospheric air through the valve and observe.

Step 4: Observations & Physics Analysis

Observation: As air is pumped out, the sound becomes progressively fainter until, under high vacuum, no sound can be heard at all, even though the hammer is visibly seen striking the gong! When air is readmitted, the ringing sound immediately returns.
Physics Explanation: In a vacuum, there are no gas molecules to form compressions and rarefactions. Without particle collisions, the mechanical energy cannot travel from the bell to the glass wall.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Key Takeaway

Conclusion: Sound is a mechanical wave that requires a material medium; it cannot travel through a vacuum.

Activity 10.8: Sound propagation through liquids (clapping or tapping stones underwater)
Step 1: Scientific Objective & Principle

To demonstrate that sound travels efficiently through liquids (water) as a longitudinal mechanical wave.

Step 2: Experimental Setup & Materials

Set 1 • Q1: How does the sound produced by a vibrating object in a medium reach your ear?
Step 1: Core Phenomenon Identified

Propagation of a mechanical longitudinal wave through particle collisions in an elastic medium.

Step 2: Underlying Mechanism

When an object vibrates, it pushes against the immediately adjacent layer of medium particles, creating an alternating series of high-pressure Compressions (C) and low-pressure Rarefactions (R).

Step 3: Step-by-Step Propagation Pathway

1. The displaced particles collide with adjacent particles, transferring their kinetic energy and momentum.
2. After collision, the original particles rebound back toward their equilibrium positions due to elastic restoring forces.
3. The next layer of particles repeats this process with subsequent layers in a chain reaction.
4. In this manner, the acoustic disturbance travels progressively through the medium until it reaches the listener's ear.

Step 4: Transduction at the Ear

Upon reaching the ear, the alternating compressions and rarefactions push and pull the tympanic membrane (eardrum), setting it into corresponding mechanical vibrations that the brain decodes as sound.

Step 5: CBSE Scoring Summary

Key Sentence: The particles of the medium do not travel from the source to the ear; only the disturbance (energy) travels via particle oscillations.

Set 1 • Q2: Explain how sound is produced by your school bell.
Step 1: Mechanical Source Action

When the school bell is struck with a hammer, the mechanical impact does work on the metal body, imparting kinetic energy that sets the metallic bell into rapid back-and-forth vibrations.

Step 2: Compression Formation

As the vibrating bell wall flexes outward, it pushes and compresses the air immediately in front of it, creating a region of high density and high pressure known as a compression.

Step 3: Rarefaction Formation

As the bell wall springs back inward, it leaves a void of reduced pressure. Surrounding air particles expand into this space, creating a region of low density and low pressure known as a rarefaction.

Step 4: Longitudinal Wave Train

As the bell continues to vibrate rapidly, a continuous succession of compressions and rarefactions propagates outward through the air in all directions as a longitudinal sound wave.

Step 5: Key Takeaway

Takeaway: If you touch the ringing school bell with your hand, you immediately feel the vibrations. Holding the bell firmly halts the vibrations, and the sound stops instantly.

A wave that requires a physical material medium (possessing mass, inertia, and elasticity) for its propagation is called a mechanical wave.

Step 2: Physics Justification

Sound waves propagate exclusively through the physical collisions and oscillatory displacements of particles of the medium (molecules of a solid, liquid, or gas). They transfer energy through intermolecular elastic forces.

Step 3: Inability to Travel in Vacuum

Because sound waves depend on particle interactions, they cannot travel through a vacuum, as demonstrated by the Bell-Jar experiment.

Step 4: Contrast with Non-Mechanical Waves

Unlike electromagnetic waves (such as light, X-rays, and radio waves), which propagate via oscillating electric and magnetic fields through empty space, sound has no mechanism to propagate in the absence of matter.

Set 2 • Q3: What are wavelength, frequency, time period and amplitude of a sound wave?
Step 1: Wavelength (λ)

Wavelength: The minimum distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions of a sound wave. SI unit: metre (m).

Step 2: Frequency (ν)

Frequency: The number of complete oscillations or cycles executed by a particle of the medium per second (or number of compressions passing a given point per second). SI unit: Hertz (Hz).

Step 3: Time Period (T)

Time Period: The time taken by a particle of the medium to complete one full oscillation cycle. SI unit: second (s). Relation: T = 1 / ν.

Step 4: Amplitude (A)

Amplitude: The maximum displacement of the particles of the medium from their original mean equilibrium position on either side. SI unit: metre (m).

Step 5: Key Takeaway

These four parameters completely define the kinematic and energetic state of any sound wave.

Set 2 • Q4: How are the wavelength and frequency of a sound wave related to its speed?

Speed is defined as the distance traveled per unit time: Speed (v) = Distance / Time.

Step 2: Substitution of Wave Quantities

In the duration of one complete time period (T), the wave travels forward a distance equal to one wavelength (λ):
v = λ / T.

Step 3: Introduction of Frequency

Since frequency is the reciprocal of time period (ν = 1 / T), substituting 1 / T with ν gives:
v = ν × λ.

Step 4: Statement in Words

Speed = Frequency × Wavelength.

Step 5: Physical Implication

In a given medium at constant temperature, speed v is constant. Therefore, wavelength is inversely proportional to frequency (λ ∝ 1 / ν): higher frequency waves have shorter wavelengths.

Exercise Q11: When a sound is reflected from a distant object, an echo is produced. Let the distance between the reflecting surface and the source of sound production remains the same. Do you hear echo sound on a hotter day?
Step 1: Temperature-Speed Relationship

The speed of sound in air is directly proportional to the square root of absolute temperature: v ∝ √T. On a hotter day, the ambient temperature is higher, which means the speed of sound (v) increases.

Step 2: Effect on Echo Return Time

The time taken for sound to travel to the obstacle and return is:
t = 2d / v.
Since the distance d remains constant, an increase in speed v causes the echo return time t to decrease.

Step 3: Condition for Hearing an Echo

To hear a distinct echo, the reflected sound must arrive after the human persistence of hearing window (t ≥ 0.1 s).

Step 4: Two Critical Cases

• Case A (If distance d is significantly greater than 17.2 m): The echo will still be heard, but it will arrive sooner (after a shorter time delay).
• Case B (If distance d was borderline, exactly 17.2 m on a cooler day): At higher temperature, t = 2d / v will drop below 0.1 s. In this borderline situation, no distinct echo will be heard because the reflection merges with the original sound.

Step 5: CBSE Exam Model Answer

Topper Answer: On a hotter day, the speed of sound increases, so the minimum distance required to hear an echo (dmin = v × 0.1 / 2) increases. If the existing distance is less than this new threshold, the echo will not be heard; if the distance is sufficiently large, the echo is heard sooner.

Exercise Q16: What is loudness of sound? What factors does it depend on?

Exercise Q17: Explain how bats use ultrasound to catch prey.

SAQ 3 (3 Marks): What is a sonic boom? How is it produced, and what are its destructive physical consequences?
Point 1: Definition of Sonic Boom

A sonic boom is an intense, explosive acoustic shock wave produced by an object (such as a supersonic fighter jet or rocket) traveling at a speed greater than the local speed of sound in air (supersonic speed, v > 344 m/s).

Point 2: Mechanism of Production

As the aircraft travels faster than sound, it outruns the pressure disturbance waves it produces. These expanding wavefronts pile up and compress violently behind the aircraft, forming a conical envelope of intense air compression called a Mach shock cone.

Point 3: Destructive Consequences

When the high-pressure trailing edge of the shock wave sweeps across the ground, the sudden abrupt pressure discontinuity creates an explosive thunderclap sound that can shatter window glass panes, crack building plaster, and damage fragile structures. For this reason, supersonic civilian flights over populated landmasses are strictly banned by aviation authorities.

The middle ear contains three tiny articulated bones (the smallest in the human body):
1. Malleus (Hammer): Attached directly to the inner surface of the vibrating eardrum.
2. Incus (Anvil): Interconnected between the hammer and the stirrup.

Point 3: Physiological Purpose

This pressure amplification is vital because the inner ear cochlea is filled with liquid (perilymph), which has much higher acoustic impedance than air and requires substantial pressure to be set into motion.

Diffraction (the bending of waves around obstacles and through doorways) occurs noticeably only when the wavelength of the wave is comparable to or larger than the size of the obstacle or aperture (λ ∼ d).

Point 2: Wavelength of Audible Sound

5.4 Long Answer Questions (5-Mark CBSE Format with Step-by-Step Marking Schemes)

LAQ 1 (5 Marks): Describe the physical mechanism of production and propagation of a longitudinal sound wave in air. Derive algebraically the universal wave equation v = νλ.
CBSE 5-Mark Marking Scheme Breakdown • 1 Mark: Defining sound as mechanical vibration energy.
• 1 Mark: Detailed description of Compression (C) formation with density/pressure analysis.
• 1 Mark: Detailed description of Rarefaction (R) formation with density/pressure analysis.
• 1 Mark: Defining longitudinal wave particle oscillation parallel to propagation.
• 1 Mark: Step-by-step algebraic derivation of wave formula v = ν × λ.
Part A: Sound Production by Mechanical Vibrations (1M)

Sound is a form of mechanical energy generated by the rapid oscillatory vibrations of an elastic body (such as a struck tuning fork). As kinetic energy is imparted to the source, its prongs execute periodic to-and-fro displacements.

Part B: Compression Formation (1M)

When the vibrating prong moves rapidly outward (forward), it exerts a compressive force on the adjacent air molecules, pushing them closer together. This localized region of high particle density and high pressure is called a Compression (C). It corresponds to a crest on a density-distance graph.

Part C: Rarefaction Formation (1M)

When the prong flexes backward (inward), it leaves a region of reduced pressure. Surrounding air particles expand into this space, creating a localized zone of low particle density and low pressure called a Rarefaction (R). It corresponds to a trough on a density-distance graph.

Part D: Longitudinal Wave Propagation (1M)

As the source oscillates continuously, alternating compressions and rarefactions travel through the air. The air particles do not travel across the room; they oscillate back and forth about their own mean equilibrium positions parallel to the direction of wave travel. Hence, sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave.

Part E: Algebraic Derivation of v = νλ (1M)

By definition, linear speed is given by:
Speed (v) = Distance Traveled / Time Taken.
During the time period of one complete oscillation (t = T), the wave disturbance travels a distance of exactly one wavelength (λ):
v = λ / T.
Since frequency ν = 1 / T, substituting 1 / T with ν yields:
v = ν × λ.
Speed = Frequency × Wavelength. (Hence derived).

LAQ 2 (5 Marks): (a) What is an echo? State the essential conditions required to hear a distinct echo. (b) Calculate the minimum obstacle distance in air at 22°C. (c) Will this minimum distance increase or decrease on a hot summer day? Explain mathematically.

• 2.0 Marks: Complete derivation of minimum distance formula (2d = vt ⇒ d = vt/2) giving exactly 17.2 m at 22°C.
• 1.5 Marks: Temperature variation analysis showing speed increase causes minimum distance to increase on hot summer days.
Part (a): Definition & Persistence of Hearing (1.5M)

Echo: The distinct repetition of original sound heard after reflection from a distant hard obstacle (such as a high wall, cliff, or hill).
Biological Condition: The sensation of sound persists in the human brain for approximately 0.1 second (1/10th of a second). To distinguish the reflected sound as a separate, distinct echo, the time gap between the emission of the original sound and the arrival of the reflected echo must be at least 0.1 second (t ≥ 0.1 s).

Part (b): Mathematical Derivation of Minimum Distance at 22°C (2.0M)

Let the distance between the source and the reflecting obstacle be d.
Total distance traveled by the sound wave going and returning = 2d.
Using Distance = Speed × Time:
2d = v × t  ⇒  d = (v × t) / 2.
Taking speed of sound in air at 22°C as v = 344 m/s and minimum time t = 0.1 s:
dmin = (344 × 0.1) / 2 = 34.4 / 2 = 17.2 metres.
Hence, the reflecting surface must be at an absolute minimum distance of 17.2 m from the source.

Part (c): Effect on a Hot Summer Day (1.5M)

On a hot summer day, the atmospheric temperature is higher. Because the speed of sound is directly proportional to the square root of absolute temperature (v ∝ √T), the speed of sound (v) increases (e.g., at 35°C, v ≈ 352 m/s).
Since dmin = (v × 0.1) / 2, as speed v increases, the minimum distance dmin directly INCREASES (e.g., at 35°C, dmin = 35.2 / 2 = 17.6 m).
Therefore, on a hotter day, the obstacle must be located farther away to produce a distinct echo!

SONAR (Sound Navigation and Ranging) operates on the principle of echolocation using high-frequency ultrasound waves.
1. The transmitter installed on the ship's hull emits high-frequency ultrasound pulses downwards into seawater.
2. The waves travel through water at speed v, strike the seabed or submerged object, and reflect back.
3. The receiver (detector) detects the reflected echo and converts it into electrical signals, recording the total round-trip elapsed time t.
4. Depth is computed using: d = (v × t) / 2.

• Measuring ocean depth (bathymetry) and mapping submarine trenches, ridges, and valleys.
• Detecting submerged navigational hazards such as icebergs, sunken wrecks, and sea mines.

• Commercial tracking of large fish shoals.

LAQ 4 (5 Marks): Give a comprehensive account of the anatomy of the human ear. Describe the step-by-step pathway of auditory transduction from acoustic pressure waves to cerebral nerve signals.
CBSE 5-Mark Marking Scheme Breakdown • 1.5 Marks: Outer ear (Pinna, ear canal, tympanic membrane) structure and role.
• 1.5 Marks: Middle ear (3 ossicles: Malleus, Incus, Stapes + Eustachian tube) and 20× lever amplification.
• 1.5 Marks: Inner ear (Cochlea, fluid waves, sensory hair cells, auditory nerve to brain).
• 0.5 Marks: Sequential physiological flowchart summary.
1. The Outer Ear (1.5M)

• Pinna: The outer cartilaginous funnel that intercepts acoustic compression waves from the surrounding environment and focuses them into the ear canal.
• Auditory Canal: A 2.5 cm conduit directing sound waves to the eardrum.
• Tympanic Membrane (Eardrum): A thin, elastic fibrous membrane. Incoming compressions exert positive pressure pushing the eardrum inward; rarefactions decrease pressure, drawing it outward. Thus, acoustic energy is converted into mechanical membrane vibration.

2. The Middle Ear (1.5M)

• The Three Ossicles (Hammer, Anvil, Stirrup): Articulated bone levers that mechanically transmit vibrations from the eardrum to the oval window.
• Pressure Amplification: Concentrating the force from the large eardrum onto the tiny oval window footprint magnifies acoustic pressure by 20 to 30 times.
• Eustachian Tube: Connects the middle ear to the throat, equalizing air pressure across the eardrum to ensure symmetrical mechanical flexing.

3. The Inner Ear (1.5M)

• Cochlea: A snail-shell-like coiled organ filled with viscous fluid (perilymph). The piston-like pumping of the stapes on the oval window sets up hydraulic pressure ripples inside the cochlear fluid.
• Sensory Hair Cells (Organ of Corti): Fluid waves deflect microscopic hair cells on the basilar membrane, opening ion channels that generate electrical action potentials (nerve impulses).
• Auditory Nerve: Bundled sensory nerve fibers that transmit the electrical signals to the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe of the brain for conscious auditory interpretation.

4. Step-by-Step Transduction Flowchart (0.5M)

Acoustic Waves in Air → Pinna Funneling → Eardrum Mechanical Vibration → 3-Bone Lever Amplification (20×) → Oval Window Piston Action → Cochlear Fluid Waves → Hair Cell Shearing → Electrical Nerve Impulses → Auditory Nerve → Brain Perception.

5.5 Competency-Based Case Studies (NEP CBE Format)

Case Study 1: Architectural Acoustics & Reverberation Control

A newly constructed multipurpose school auditorium with a seating capacity of 1,200 students in Bhubaneswar was inaugurated. During the opening ceremony speech, teachers and students complained that the principal's words were unintelligible, echoing persistently and producing a hollow, blurred boom. An acoustical engineer was consulted to perform an audit. The engineer measured the room dimensions (length 35 m, width 20 m, height 10 m) with flat concrete walls, a flat plastered ceiling, polished granite flooring, and bare wooden chairs. The measured reverberation time was 4.2 seconds, far above the recommended standard of 1.1 seconds for speech clarity. The engineer recommended immediate installation of perforated mineral wool wall tiles, heavy pleated velvet stage curtains, floor carpeting, and cushioned upholstered chairs.

Question 1.1: What is reverberation, and why was the speech in the school auditorium unintelligible?

Answer: Reverberation is the prolonged persistence of sound in an enclosed space due to repeated, successive reflections from surrounding hard surfaces. Because the walls, ceiling, and polished floor were bare and hard, sound reflected multiple times with negligible absorption. With an excessive reverberation time of 4.2 s, the reflections of one spoken word overlapped with the subsequent word, transforming distinct speech into an unintelligible, muddy roar.

Question 1.2: How do porous materials like mineral wool tiles and velvet drapes reduce reverberation physically?

Answer: Porous materials contain millions of interconnected microscopic air pockets and fibrous pores. When an acoustic compression wave enters these pores, the vibrating air molecules rub against the microscopic fibers. Viscous friction and drag convert the acoustic mechanical kinetic energy into tiny amounts of thermal energy (heat), absorbing the wave and stopping specular reflection.

Question 1.3: Why did the engineer suggest replacing the flat ceiling with a curved ceiling?

Answer: A flat ceiling reflects sound only to specific localized patches, causing hot spots and dead zones. A curved concave parabolic ceiling reflects incident sound waves across divergent angles, distributing reflected sound evenly across all rows, from the front row to the very back of the auditorium.

Question 1.4: Why do upholstered cushioned chairs help maintain consistent acoustics whether the hall is full or half empty?

Answer: Human clothing and bodies are natural sound absorbers. Bare wooden chairs reflect sound when empty, drastically changing the hall's acoustics between rehearsals (empty) and full assemblies. Fabric-upholstered cushioned seats have an acoustic absorption coefficient nearly identical to a seated human body, ensuring that the reverberation time remains constant at ∼1.1 s regardless of attendance!

Case Study 2: Marine Echolocation & Biomedical Ultrasound

Ultrasound waves (frequencies exceeding 20 kHz) have revolutionized modern marine navigation and medical diagnostics. A marine research vessel operating in the Bay of Bengal uses a SONAR fathometer operating at 40 kHz to map underwater topography and detect submerged shipwrecks. Concurrently, in a coastal medical hospital, an obstetrician uses a 3.5 MHz ultrasound transducer to image a developing 20-week human fetus, while a cardiologist performs an echocardiogram (ECHO) to assess the pumping action of a patient's mitral heart valve. Both systems rely on high-frequency piezoelectric crystal transducers that emit acoustic pulses and measure the precise time delay and amplitude of returning echoes.

Question 2.1: State two fundamental physical properties of ultrasound that make it superior to audible sound for both SONAR and medical diagnostics.

Answer:
1. Extremely short wavelength: Enables ultrasound to travel in sharp, highly directed, pencil-thin beams without spreading or diffracting around small obstacles.
2. High penetration power: Transmits effectively through dense media (seawater and human soft tissues) and reflects cleanly from acoustic impedance boundaries.

Question 2.2: If the ship's 40 kHz SONAR signal reflects from a submerged shipwreck and returns in 0.8 seconds, calculate the depth of the wreck. (Speed of sound in seawater = 1530 m/s).

Calculation:
2d = v × t  ⇒  d = (v × t) / 2.
d = (1530 × 0.8) / 2 = 1224 / 2 = 612 metres.
The shipwreck is at a depth of 612 metres.

Question 2.3: Why is ultrasonography (USG) preferred over X-ray radiography for monitoring prenatal fetal development?

Answer: X-rays are high-energy ionizing electromagnetic radiation that can cause DNA mutations, chromosomal breaks, and tissue damage in rapidly dividing fetal cells. Ultrasound consists of harmless mechanical acoustic pressure waves that produce zero ionizing radiation, making it completely safe for both mother and fetus.

Question 2.4: What is echocardiography (ECHO), and which physiological organ does it assess?

Answer: Echocardiography is a medical diagnostic technique that uses reflected ultrasonic waves to construct dynamic, real-time moving images of the human heart. It assesses heart chamber volumes, wall motion, myocardial thickness, and the opening and closing mechanics of cardiac valves.

PYQ 3 (CBSE 2022 Term-2): State the principle of Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) using ultrasound. Explain how an internal air crack in a heavy steel axle is detected without damaging it.
Point 1: Underlying Principle

Ultrasound waves pass through homogeneous metals in straight paths. However, when an ultrasonic wave strikes a boundary between two media of differing acoustic densities (such as steel and an air crack), a significant portion of the wave is reflected back.

Point 2: Testing Setup & Flawless vs Flawed Region

An ultrasonic transmitter sends pulses into one side of the steel axle, while a receiver array sits on the opposite side.
• In homogeneous solid sections, ultrasound travels straight through, registering full signal intensity on the opposing detector.
• When the beam encounters an internal air crack or void, the wave reflects from the crack boundary. The opposing detector receives zero or diminished signal, while a pulse-echo detector at the transmitter end records an early reflection pulse.

Point 3: Commercial Benefit

The axle is inspected thoroughly without cutting, sawing, or damaging the expensive industrial component.

Case (b): When Amplitude is Halved

Loudness decreases to one-fourth (1/4th) of its original value!

Pitch remains completely unchanged because pitch depends solely on frequency, not amplitude.

PYQ 5 (CBSE SQP): Establish why sound travels faster through humid (moist) air than through dry air at the exact same temperature and atmospheric pressure.
Point 1: Speed-Density Relation

The speed of sound in a gaseous medium is inversely proportional to the square root of its density:
v = √(γP / ρ)  ⇒  v ∝ 1 / √ρ.

Point 2: Molecular Mass Comparison

Dry air is a mixture of nitrogen (N&sub2;, molecular mass 28 u) and oxygen (O&sub2;, molecular mass 32 u), having an effective average molar mass of approximately 28.8 g/mol.
Water vapor consists of H&sub2;O molecules with a molar mass of only 18 g/mol.

Point 3: Density Reduction

When air becomes humid, lighter water vapor molecules displace heavier nitrogen and oxygen molecules at the same pressure and temperature. Therefore, humid air is less dense than dry air (ρhumid < ρdry).

Point 4: Final Deductive Conclusion

Because density is lower, sound waves propagate with less inertia and greater speed through humid air (vhumid > vdry).

6.1 Five Fatal Exam Hall Traps & How to Avoid Them

TRAP 1: The "Half-Distance" Disaster in Echo & SONAR Calculations

The Blunder: When given total time t for an echo or SONAR pulse to return, students frequently write Distance = Speed × Time (d = v × t), forgetting that sound has made a two-way round trip! This erroneously doubles the calculated distance and loses all marks.
The Correction: Always write the explicit round-trip identity first:
Total Distance Traveled = 2d  ⇒  2d = v × t  ⇒  d = (v × t) / 2.

TRAP 2: Believing Air Molecules Travel Across the Room with Sound

The Blunder: Thinking that when a speaker talks on a stage, the air molecules from their mouth fly bodily across the auditorium and enter the listener's ear canal.
The Correction: In any wave motion, particles of the medium never travel bodily from source to receiver! Each particle merely oscillates back and forth around its own fixed mean equilibrium position, passing energy to its neighbor. Only the disturbance (energy and momentum) propagates across space!

TRAP 3: Conflating Loudness with Pitch

The Blunder: Believing that a high-pitch sound is automatically a loud sound, or stating that a car horn has a higher pitch because it is louder.
The Correction: Loudness and pitch are completely independent acoustic properties!
• Loudness depends strictly on AMPLITUDE (Loudness ∝ A²).
• Pitch depends strictly on FREQUENCY.
A mosquito's whine has high pitch (high frequency) but low loudness (tiny amplitude). A lion's roar has low pitch (low frequency) but huge loudness (huge amplitude).

TRAP 4: Assuming High Frequency Means High Speed in the Same Medium

The Blunder: Reasoning that because v = ν × λ, increasing the frequency ν will cause the wave speed v to increase.
The Correction: In a given medium at constant temperature, the speed of sound v is a constant physical property of the medium. When you double the frequency (2ν), the wavelength automatically halves (λ / 2), keeping speed v completely constant!

TRAP 5: The "Stone in the Well / Tower" Single-Time Trap

The Blunder: When asked "When is the splash heard after dropping a stone into a deep well or tower pond?", calculating only the time taken for the stone to fall (t = √(2h / g)).
The Correction: Total time elapsed consists of two distinct physical phases:
1. Time for the stone to accelerate downward under gravity: t₁ = √(2h / g).
2. Time for the acoustic splash wave to travel upward at uniform speed: t₂ = h / vsound.
Total Time = t₁ + t₂. Never omit the sound return time!
