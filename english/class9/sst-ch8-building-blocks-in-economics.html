Section 2 Exhaustive Topic-by-Topic Notes

1. Etymology and Definition of Economics

The English word Economics is derived from the ancient Greek word ‘Oikonomia’, which is a compound of two words:

  • ‘Oikos’: Meaning ‘house’ or ‘household’.
  • ‘Nemein’: Meaning ‘to manage’ or ‘distribute’.

Thus, economics originally meant ‘household management’. Just as a family budget must decide whether to allocate limited monthly income toward nutritious food, school fees, rent, or a weekend holiday, an entire nation must decide how to distribute its limited national resources (land, minerals, manpower, capital, and tax revenue) among competing societal needs like national defence, highway infrastructure, government hospitals, and free schools.

📖 Formal Definition of Economics

Economics is a social science that studies how individuals, businesses, governments, and societies make choices to optimize the allocation of scarce resources that have alternative uses, in order to satisfy unlimited human needs and wants.

2. Needs vs Wants: The Engine of Unlimited Desires

Human psychology distinguishes sharply between basic biological survival requirements and expanding cultural desires:

  • Needs: Basic material essentials necessary for survival and human dignity, including potable drinking water, balanced food, basic clothing, safe shelter, healthcare, and primary education. Needs are finite and satiable.
  • Wants: Desires for goods and services that improve personal comfort, social prestige, entertainment, or luxury (e.g., smartphones, branded sneakers, luxury vacations, motorbikes).

The Nature of Human Wants: Human wants are unlimited and recurring. As soon as one desire is satisfied, new and higher aspirations emerge. For instance, a person who walks to work aspires to buy a bicycle; once they acquire a bicycle, they dream of a motorcycle; once they own a motorcycle, they desire an air-conditioned car. Because human desires are infinite while the physical planet is finite, no economy in world history can satisfy all human wants simultaneously.

3. The Scarcity Principle & Alternative Uses of Resources

Scarcity is the fundamental economic condition where available resources are insufficient to satisfy all human desires. Resources fall into two major categories:

  1. Natural Resources: Arable soil, fresh water, mineral ores, forests, fossil fuels, and marine life.
  2. Human-Made Resources: Industrial factories, electrical grids, transport networks, precision tools, machinery, and software technology.

Crucially, resources are not only scarce; they can also be put to competing alternative uses. For example, consider the metal steel:

  • A metric ton of steel can be used to fabricate surgical equipment and hospital beds.
  • Alternatively, it can be used to manufacture home refrigerators and kitchen utensils.
  • Or it can be deployed to build commercial aircraft and railway tracks.
  • Or it can be forged into military tanks and artillery shells.

Because the same piece of steel cannot be in two places at once, choosing to build more tanks inevitably means giving up hospital equipment or civilian trains.

4. Opportunity Cost and the Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

🎯 Core Concept: Opportunity Cost

Opportunity Cost is the value or benefit of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. In economics, there is no free lunch — every decision to consume, produce, or invest carries a hidden cost representing what you had to sacrifice.

The Production Possibility Curve (PPC) Decoded:

The Production Possibility Curve (PPC) (or Production Possibility Frontier) is a graphical curve that illustrates all possible maximum combinations of two goods that an economy (or farmer) can produce using all available resources and technology fully and efficiently.

6. The Three Central Questions Faced by Every Economy

Every nation on Earth — whether wealthy or developing, democratic or communist — must answer three fundamental questions:

Question 1: What to Produce (and in what quantities)?

An economy must determine which goods and services to create and how much of each. For example:

  • Consumer Goods vs Capital Goods: Should factories produce bread, clothes, and television sets (for immediate satisfaction), or industrial machinery, heavy tractors, and cranes (to expand future manufacturing capacity)?
  • Cash Crops vs Sustainable Grains: Should farmers cultivate water-intensive sugarcane and commercial paddy (generating fast cash profits but depleting groundwater tables), or drought-resistant, climate-resilient millets and pulses (which conserve water, replenish nitrogen in the soil, and bolster nutritional security)? Here, the opportunity cost of chasing short-term sugarcane profit is the permanent degradation of water tables and soil fertility.

Question 2: How to Produce?

This question deals with the choice of production technique — selecting the most cost-effective and productive combination of factors of production:

  • Labour-Intensive Technique (LIT): Uses a high proportion of human labour and relatively simple machinery. Highly suitable in developing nations with abundant, youthful labour (e.g., handicrafts, handloom textiles, seasonal harvest). It generates widespread employment.
  • Capital-Intensive Technique (CIT): Relies heavily on automated assembly lines, precision robotics, and advanced software, employing very few manual workers. Highly suitable where capital is abundant and labour is scarce or expensive (e.g., automobile assembly, semiconductor fabrication, petrochemical refining).

Question 3: For Whom to Produce?

Because goods cannot be produced in limitless quantities, an economy must decide how national income and products are distributed among citizens. In a market system, goods are distributed according to purchasing power (those with high incomes receive premium goods, while the poor may go without). For example, shoe manufacturers segment their production:

  • School Shoes: Simple, durable, affordable, targeted at students of all income classes.
  • Formal Office Shoes: Polished leather, comfortable padding, targeted at working professionals.
  • Sports/Athletic Shoes: Engineered shock-absorbing soles, targeted at athletes and runners.
  • Casual Rubber Slippers: Mass-produced, highly affordable, for daily wear across lower-income families.

8. Public Goods and the Evolution of the Indian Economy

🛡 What Are Public Goods?

Public Goods are commodities or services provided to all members of society that possess two non-negotiable economic characteristics:

  • Non-Excludable: No citizen can be practically excluded from benefiting from them, even if they pay no direct taxes (e.g., national border defence, street lighting, clean air).
  • Non-Rivalrous: One citizen’s consumption of the good does not diminish its availability to others (e.g., when you look at a streetlight or stroll through a public park, you do not reduce the light or open air available to other pedestrians).

Because private profit-seeking businesses cannot easily charge individual users for public goods, private markets fail to supply them in adequate quantities. Therefore, the government must step in to finance and build public goods using tax revenue.

Profit Motive: Drives technological innovation • Consumer Choice: Diverse goods, clothing, tech • Competition: Lowers costs, elevates quality • Efficiency: Rapid capital allocation Risk: Ignores the poor who lack purchasing power. The Historical Evolution of the Indian Economy:

  1. Post-Independence Decades (1950–1990):

    Following colonial exploitation, India adopted a state-led development strategy. Heavy industries, dams, steel mills, and railway networks were controlled by the public sector, while private firms were subjected to strict licensing and quota restrictions (popularly called the ‘License Raj’).

  2. The 1991 Economic Reforms (LPG):

    By 1991, India encountered a balance of payments crisis. In response, Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh unveiled sweeping economic reforms centered on Liberalisation, Privatisation, and Globalisation (LPG). These reforms dismantled industrial licensing, welcomed foreign direct investment, privatized inefficient state units, and encouraged private entrepreneurship — decisively transforming India into a vibrant, globally integrated mixed economy.

  3. The Economic Survey of India:

    Prepared annually by the Chief Economic Adviser and the Ministry of Finance, the Economic Survey is tabled in Parliament immediately before the Union Budget. It analyses GDP trajectory, agricultural harvests, industrial output, inflation rates, and employment trends, providing the empirical data required for sound economic decisions.

Answer:

    • Electricity bill and water supply payment (₹1,200): Need — Basic utility required for daily living and study.

  2. Problems Created by Excessive Wants:

    Yes, chasing too many wants creates severe economic and environmental crises:

    • Individual Level: Leads to debt traps, financial stress, and exhaustion of emergency savings.
    • Societal & Ecological Level: Rampant consumerism accelerates the over-extraction of finite natural resources (deforestation, excessive mining, plastic pollution, carbon emissions), causing climate change and ecological collapse.
Page 4: Let’s Explore — Everyday Purchase Choices & Is Time a Scarce Resource?

Answer:

  • Everyday Family Trade-Offs: When parents decide to spend money on enrolling a child in an advanced computer coding class, the opportunity cost is postponing the purchase of a new refrigerator or canceling a family weekend resort trip.
  • Is Time a Scarce Resource?

    Yes, time is the ultimate scarce resource. Every human being has exactly 24 hours per day. Time spent watching social media videos carries the direct opportunity cost of forgone study time, athletic practice, or restful sleep. Unlike money, lost time cannot be regenerated or earned back.

Page 7: Think About It — Budget Allocation: Healthcare & Education vs Defence & Space

Answer:

Governments face a classic ‘Guns vs Butter’ economic dilemma:

  1. The Case for Healthcare and Education (Human Capital): Investing in government schools, universities, clinics, and hospitals directly elevates human productivity, eradicates poverty, reduces maternal mortality, and drives long-term economic growth. An uneducated, unhealthy workforce cannot sustain a modern nation.
  2. The Case for Defence and Space: A country located in a hostile geopolitical neighborhood must invest in military deterrence to safeguard national sovereignty and protect citizens’ peaceful existence. Furthermore, satellite space programmes yield vital civilian applications — weather forecasting for farmers, disaster management, and telecommunications.
  3. Conclusion: A balanced compromise is necessary. A nation must maintain sufficient defence deterrence while prioritizing human development as the foundation of enduring national security.
Page 9: Let’s Explore — Should Government Completely Stay Out of Enterprise Decisions?

Answer:

No, the government should not stay completely out of enterprise decisions.

  • Why Total Laissez-Faire Fails: Without government oversight, private monopolies exploit consumers through price-gouging, exploit workers with unsafe sweatshop conditions, and dump hazardous chemical pollutants into public rivers.
  • Example Where Government Action Helped: Government subsidies and production-linked incentives (PLI schemes) helped India develop a world-class domestic mobile phone assembly and generic pharmaceutical manufacturing industry.
  • Example Where Excessive Regulation Harmed: Pre-1991 License Raj quotas, where a company had to wait months for bureaucratic permits just to import a spare machine part, stifled industrial efficiency and caused shortages.

Part B: End-of-Chapter Exercises & Activities (Questions 1 to 10)

Question 1: Why do people’s wants keep changing? How does this affect production? Why cannot all wants be satisfied?

Answer:

  1. Why Wants Change Over Time:
    • Technological Innovations: The invention of smartphones created entirely new desires that did not exist 20 years ago.
    • Income Growth & Lifestyles: As personal incomes rise, individuals upgrade from basic sustenance goods to comfort and luxury items.
    • Social Influences & Advertising: Media, fashion trends, and peer habits constantly generate new desires.
  2. Effect on Production:

    Producers must continuously reallocate factors of production away from obsolete items (e.g., landline telephones, cassette tapes) toward emerging consumer demands (e.g., electric vehicles, solar panels, cloud software).

  3. Why All Wants Cannot Be Satisfied:

    All production requires physical factors of production (land, fresh water, minerals, skilled labour, capital). Because Earth’s resources are strictly finite while human imagination and desires are boundless, universal satisfaction of all wants is a physical impossibility.

Question 2: ‘Human wants are unlimited and keep changing.’ How does this pressure the environment? Can satisfaction of wants and resource extraction be balanced?

Answer:

  1. Environmental Pressure from Relentless Consumption:
    • Resource Depletion: Over-mining fossil fuels, minerals, and over-extracting groundwater tables beyond natural recharge rates.
    • Habitat Destruction: Clear-cutting ancient forests to cultivate commercial cash crops, destroying wildlife biodiversity.
    • Pollution Crisis: Manufacturing millions of short-lived consumer goods generates mountains of electronic and plastic waste, greenhouse gases, and industrial toxins.
  2. Balancing Satisfaction and Conservation (Sustainable Development):

    Yes, a sustainable balance can be achieved through deliberate structural transitions:

    • Circular Economy Model: Shifting from a ‘take-make-dispose’ culture to rigorous recycling, remanufacturing, and designing long-lasting durable products.
    • Renewable Energy Transition: Replacing coal and petroleum with solar, wind, and green hydrogen power.
    • Conscious Consumerism: Promoting mindful living (e.g., Mission LiFE — Lifestyle for Environment), choosing public transport, reducing food waste, and distinguishing genuine needs from frivolous desires.
Question 3: Can you think of a resource in your region that is scarce but used wastefully? How could it be managed better?

Answer:

Identified Scarce Resource: Fresh Groundwater

  • Nature of Wastage:
    1. Flood Irrigation in Agriculture: Farmers pumping unrestricted groundwater using free electricity to cultivate water-thirsty crops like paddy and sugarcane in semi-arid zones, losing 60% of water to evaporation.
    2. Urban Negligence: Washing cars with running hosepipes, unaddressed leaking municipal pipes, and leaving overhead water tanks overflowing.
  • Better Economic Management Strategies:
    1. Micro-Irrigation Adoption: Incentivizing precision drip and sprinkler irrigation that delivers water directly to plant roots, saving 50% water.
    2. Crop Diversification: Shifting incentives toward drought-hardy millets, pulses, and oilseeds.
    3. Mandatory Rainwater Harvesting: Enforcing rooftop rainwater collection systems in all schools, municipal buildings, and residential complexes to recharge aquifers.
Question 4: Which economic system gives people the most freedom? Which is best suited for promoting innovation? Why?

Answer:

  1. System Providing the Most Individual Freedom: The Market Economy
    • Consumer Sovereignty: Individuals have absolute freedom to spend their income on whatever products they desire.
    • Freedom of Enterprise: Any entrepreneur can start, expand, or close a business, choose their profession, and buy or sell private property without needing central bureaucratic permits.
  2. System Best Suited for Promoting Innovation: The Market Economy (supported by Mixed Economy rules)
    • The Profit Incentive: Private entrepreneurs invest millions in research and development (R&D) because inventing a superior, cheaper product yields substantial financial rewards.
    • Competitive Pressure: In a competitive market, firms that fail to innovate go bankrupt (e.g., digital cameras replacing film cameras). Fear of obsolescence forces continuous technological upgrades.
Question 5: Critically examine why pure economic systems rarely exist in reality. Assess limitations and justify why a mixed economy is most practical.

Answer:

Limitations of Pure Theoretical Systems:

  1. Failure of Pure Command / Planned Economies:

    A central planning commission cannot accurately forecast millions of changing daily consumer demands. Complete state ownership eliminates individual initiative, fosters massive corruption, creates chronic shortages, and results in poor-quality goods (as seen in the economic collapse of the USSR).

  2. Failure of Pure Free Market / Laissez-Faire:

    Pure capitalism ignores equity. Private markets produce luxury sports cars for millionaires while failing to build affordable housing, clean drinking water, or hospitals for the poor because the poor lack purchasing power. Furthermore, pure markets fail to provide Public Goods and neglect environmental pollution.

Why the Mixed Economy is the Pragmatic Real-World Choice:

  • Best of Both Worlds: A mixed economy harnesses the entrepreneurial vitality, competitive efficiency, and innovation of the private market, while empowering the democratic state to regulate monopolies, build infrastructure, protect workers, and distribute subsidized food/healthcare to the needy.
  • Global Preponderance: Virtually every modern country — from India and the UK to Japan and even the United States — operates as a mixed economy with varying degrees of state intervention.

Question: A student has ₹100 and must choose between buying a notebook or saving the money for buying a tennis racket later. Which economic concept best explains this situation?

a. Demand
b. Opportunity cost
c. Production
d. Inflation

Correct Answer: b. Opportunity cost

Explanation: The student faces a choice under limited financial resources (₹100). Choosing to spend the ₹100 on the notebook means giving up the benefit of saving toward the tennis racket; that sacrificed alternative represents the opportunity cost of buying the notebook.

Question 7: How does understanding opportunity cost improve the quality of economic decision-making?

Answer:

  1. Reveals Hidden Sacrifices: Most people only look at the financial price tag. Understanding opportunity cost forces individuals and governments to ask: “What valuable alternative am I giving up by choosing this?”
  2. Prevents Impulsive Wastage: Encourages consumers to evaluate whether short-term instant gratification (eating junk food) is worth sacrificing long-term goals (saving for college education).
  3. Optimizes National Resource Allocation: Enables policymakers to weigh competing public investments — for example, evaluating whether ₹5,000 crore is better spent building a grandiose statue or upgrading 500 rural primary healthcare centers.
Question 8: Can effective economic decisions be made without reliable data? Support your answer with an example.

Answer:

No, effective economic decisions cannot be made without reliable, timely empirical data. Making economic policy without data is like flying an airplane blindfolded through a storm.

  • Illustrative Example — Food Security Management:

    Every year, the government must decide how much wheat and rice the Food Corporation of India (FCI) should procure and store in buffer stocks. If the government lacks reliable data on monsoon rainfall forecasts, crop acreage, and domestic population numbers, it could either procure too little grain (leading to catastrophic food shortages, hoarding, and soaring inflation) or procure excess grain that rots in open warehouses while farmers suffer distress sales. As shown by the Economic Survey of India, data-driven planning is indispensable for economic stability.

Question 9: Analyse how a country’s present economic choices shape its long-term future. Why is considering future consequences vital?

Answer:

  1. Human Capital Investment vs Instant Consumption:

    If a nation allocates its budget today toward high-quality free education, scientific research, and healthcare, its future citizens become skilled engineers, scientists, and entrepreneurs, transforming the nation into a high-income knowledge economy.

  2. Energy Infrastructure Choices:

    Building coal-fired thermal power plants today provides cheap immediate electricity but locks the country into decades of air pollution, respiratory disease, and costly international carbon penalties. Choosing renewable solar grids today ensures clean air and energy independence tomorrow.

  3. Intergenerational Equity:

    Present generations are custodians of the Earth. Recklessly exhausting fossil fuels, mining hills, and polluting rivers leaves future generations impoverished. Economic decisions must prioritize sustainable development — meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own.

LA 1: Explain the Three Central Questions Faced by Every Economy 5 Marks

Marking Breakdown:

  • Root cause (Scarcity & Choice) [1 Mark]
  • What to produce & quantity choice [1.5 Marks]
  • How to produce & technique selection [1.5 Marks]
  • For whom to produce & income distribution [1 Mark]

Comprehensive Answer:

  1. Origin in Scarcity: Because resources are scarce and have alternative uses, every society must address three central questions:
  2. 1. What to Produce and in What Quantities?

    Deciding which goods to manufacture and in what amounts — balancing immediate consumer necessities (food, clothing) against long-term capital assets (factories, dams), or commercial cash crops against drought-resistant food grains.

  3. 2. How to Produce?

    Selecting the optimal production technique: Labour-Intensive (employing more workers relative to capital, generating jobs) vs Capital-Intensive (using more machinery and automation, maximizing output speed and precision).

  4. 3. For Whom to Produce?

    Determining how total national output is distributed among citizens based on income, purchasing power, and social welfare needs (e.g., mass public transportation vs luxury personal cars).

LA 3: Detail the Production Possibility Curve (PPC) and its Economic Meaning 5 Marks

Marking Breakdown:

  • Definition of PPC [1 Mark]
  • Explanation of Downward Concave Slope (Increasing Opportunity Cost) [1.5 Marks]
  • Significance of points ON, INSIDE, and OUTSIDE the curve [1.5 Marks]
  • Practical application for national planning [1 Mark]

Comprehensive Answer:

  1. Definition: The Production Possibility Curve (PPC) shows all combinations of two goods that an economy can produce using its limited resources and current technology fully and efficiently.
  2. Why It Slopes Downward: Resources are scarce. To produce more units of Good X (e.g., Barley), the economy must transfer land and labor away from Good Y (e.g., Wheat). The value of forgone Wheat is the opportunity cost.
  3. Interpretation of Production Points:
    • Points on the Curve (A, B, C, D, E): Maximum productive efficiency — zero resource wastage.
    • Point Inside the Curve (Point U): Inefficiency, unemployment, or underutilization of resources.
    • Point Outside the Curve (Point X): Unattainable with current resources and technology; can only be reached through economic growth or technological breakthroughs.
  4. Planning Utility: Enables governments to visualize national choices (e.g., civilian goods vs military defense) and optimize resource allocation.

E. Competency-Based Case Studies (2 Real-World Scenarios)

Case Study 1: The Drought Dilemma in Shaktinagar 4 Marks

Case Narrative: In the semi-arid district of Shaktinagar, farmers traditionally planted cash-crop sugarcane because sugar mills offered guaranteed purchase contracts. However, five consecutive years of severe groundwater depletion caused borewells to run dry. The state agriculture department introduced a mission to incentivize drought-resistant pearl millet (Bajra) and pulses, offering seed subsidies and soil health kits. While sugarcane yielded ₹70,000 gross cash per acre, it required 2,000 mm of water; bajra required only 350 mm of water, improved soil nitrogen, and ensured household nutrition.

Questions & Step-by-Step Solutions:

1. Identify the fundamental ‘What to Produce’ trade-off in this case. (1 Mark)
Answer: The trade-off is between producing high-cash, water-intensive sugarcane (short-term monetary gain) versus cultivating drought-resilient millets and pulses (ecological sustainability and long-term water conservation).

2. What was the opportunity cost of continuing sugarcane cultivation? (1.5 Marks)
Answer: The opportunity cost included: (i) the lost harvest of nutritious millets and pulses; (ii) irreversible depletion of local groundwater aquifers; and (iii) long-term degradation of fertile soil health.

3. How did government policy intervene to align private choice with public welfare? (1.5 Marks)
Answer: By offering seed subsidies, soil testing kits, and promoting millet farming, the government corrected market failure, encouraging farmers to adopt sustainable production techniques without facing financial ruin.

Case Study 2: Technology Choice in Greenwear Garments 4 Marks

Case Narrative: Greenwear Apparel, a garment exporter in Tiruppur, received an order for 2,00,000 standardized cotton T-shirts for an international retailer. The factory manager had to decide whether to hire 150 temporary stitching workers using manual sewing tables or install 10 automated robotic laser-cutting and stitching machines imported from Germany. Manual labor would cost ₹350 per worker per day but had a 5% stitching defect rate. The automated robotic unit cost ₹1.2 crore upfront but operated 24/7 with a defect rate below 0.1%.

Questions & Step-by-Step Solutions:

1. Which central economic question is illustrated in this case? (1 Mark)
Answer: The central question is ‘How to Produce?’ — deciding between labour-intensive manual stitching and capital-intensive automated robotics.

2. State two factors that Greenwear Apparel must evaluate before deciding. (1.5 Marks)
Answer:

  1. Upfront Capital Cost vs Operational Labor Expenses: Comparing the ₹1.2 crore machinery investment against ongoing daily wage payments.
  2. Product Standardization & Quality Tolerance: High precision requirements (defect rate below 0.1%) of international buyers strongly favour automation.

3. What would be the broader societal impact if all garment firms in India shifted to robotics? (1.5 Marks)
Answer: While manufacturing productivity and global export competitiveness would rise, widespread displacement of unskilled manual labor could exacerbate unemployment in a labor-abundant country like India.

Section 6 Exam Hall Common Mistakes & 15 Crisp Revision Points

Top 5 Conceptual Pitfalls to Avoid

⚠ Mistake 1: Believing Opportunity Cost Is Just Money Spent

The Trap: Calculating opportunity cost solely as the cash expenditure of an item.
The Correction: Opportunity cost is NOT the money paid; it is the value of the next best alternative sacrificed (e.g., the opportunity cost of attending college is the full-time salary you could have earned by working).

⚠ Mistake 2: Thinking Points Outside the PPC Are ‘Inefficient’

The Trap: Marking points outside the PPC as inefficient production.
The Correction: Points inside the curve represent inefficiency and wastage. Points outside the curve are unattainable with current resources and technology.

⚠ Mistake 3: Confusing Capital with Just Paper Currency

The Trap: Treating ‘Capital’ in economics purely as currency notes.
The Correction: In production economics, Real Capital refers to man-made physical tools, factories, industrial machinery, and infrastructure used to produce goods, not just cash.

⚠ Mistake 4: Believing Market Economies Have No Government at All

The Trap: Stating that governments do not exist in market economies.
The Correction: Even in market economies, the government acts as an essential referee — enforcing property laws, police safety, contract enforcement, and national defence.

⚠ Mistake 5: Confusing Public Goods with Government-Owned Businesses

The Trap: Calling Air India or a state hotel a ‘public good’.
The Correction: Public goods must be non-excludable and non-rivalrous (e.g., streetlights, clean air, national borders). Airline seats and hotel rooms are private goods because non-paying users are easily excluded.

15 Ultra-Crisp Revision Points (Read Before Entering Exam Hall)

  1. Etymology: Economics stems from Greek Oikonomia — household resource management.
  2. The Core Problem: Scarcity arises because limited resources clash with unlimited human wants.
  3. Needs vs Wants: Needs are biological survival essentials; wants are expandable comfort/luxury desires.
  4. Alternative Uses: Resources like steel, land, and water have multiple competing uses, forcing trade-offs.
  5. Opportunity Cost: The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
  6. PPC Curve: Graphical curve plotting combinations of two goods produced at full resource efficiency.
  7. PPC Points: On curve = full efficiency; inside = underutilization/wastage; outside = unattainable.
  8. Factors of Production: Land (Rent), Labour (Wages), Capital (Interest), Technology/Enterprise (Profit).
  9. Three Central Questions: What to produce, How to produce, and For Whom to produce.
  10. Technique Selection: Labour-intensive (creates jobs) vs Capital-intensive (maximizes automated precision).
  11. Distribution Rule: ‘For whom to produce’ addresses how national income and products are distributed.
  12. Planned Economy: Central government planners decide all production and prices (e.g., former USSR).
  13. Market Economy: Private ownership; prices determined by demand and supply forces (e.g., USA).
  14. Mixed Economy: Private market engine + Government welfare safety net & public goods (India).
  15. 1991 LPG Reforms: Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation shifted India toward dynamic market orientation.
