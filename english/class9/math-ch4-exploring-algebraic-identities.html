2.1 The Grand Distinction: Algebraic Identity vs. Conditional Equation

Before we jump into formulas, let us settle the most fundamental question in secondary algebra: What is the difference between an equation and an identity? Many students confuse the two because both contain an equality symbol ("="). However, their inner mathematical meaning is completely different!

Definition: Conditional Algebraic Equation

An equation is a mathematical statement of equality involving one or more variables that is true only for specific, selected values of the variables. These specific values are called the solutions or roots of the equation.

Example: 3x + 5 = 17
• If x = 4: 3(4) + 5 = 12 + 5 = 17 (True! x = 4 is the unique solution)
• If x = 2: 3(2) + 5 = 6 + 5 = 11 ≠ 17 (False!)
Conclusion: This statement is NOT universally true; it is conditioned upon x taking the exact value 4.
Definition: Universal Algebraic Identity

An algebraic identity is an equality that holds true for ALL possible values of the variables chosen from their domain. It is an immutable, universal law of arithmetic structure disguised in algebraic symbols.

Example: (x + 1)² = x² + 2x + 1
• Test x = 0: LHS = (0 + 1)² = 1; RHS = 0² + 2(0) + 1 = 1 (True!)
• Test x = 5: LHS = (5 + 1)² = 6² = 36; RHS = 5² + 2(5) + 1 = 25 + 10 + 1 = 36 (True!)
• Test x = -3: LHS = (-3 + 1)² = (-2)² = 4; RHS = (-3)² + 2(-3) + 1 = 9 - 6 + 1 = 4 (True!)
• Test x = 1/2: LHS = (1/2 + 1)² = (3/2)² = 9/4; RHS = (1/2)² + 2(1/2) + 1 = 1/4 + 1 + 1 = 9/4 (True!)
Conclusion: No matter what real number you plug in for x, the Left-Hand Side (LHS) will always match the Right-Hand Side (RHS).
Feature / Characteristic Conditional Equation Universal Algebraic Identity
Scope of Validity True only for a finite number of specific roots (e.g., degree n polynomial equation has at most n roots). True for infinitely many values (every possible number in the real number system).
Primary Purpose To find the unknown value (solve for x, y, or z). To transform algebraic expressions (expand products or factorise polynomials).
Geometric Meaning The intersection point(s) of two curves or lines. Two different ways of calculating the exact same geometric area or volume.
Example x² - 5x + 6 = 0 (True only when x = 2 or x = 3). (x - 2)(x - 3) = x² - 5x + 6 (True for EVERY real number x).

2.2 Historical Roots: Bharatiya Bījagaṇita and Geometric Algebra

Algebraic identities were not invented as dry abstract symbols in modern textbooks. For thousands of years, ancient mathematicians explored these relationships as geometric dissections—physically cutting, measuring, and reassembling squares and rectangles on the ground!

1. The Vedic Sulba Sūtras (c. 800 – 500 BCE)

In ancient India, the master geometers Baudhāyana, Āpastamba, and Mānava composed the Sulba Sūtras ("Aphorisms of the Cord"). They constructed elaborate sacrificial fire altars (such as the Śyenaciti or falcon-shaped altar) having precise area requirements. To alter altar shapes while preserving exact areas, they formulated geometric equivalents of identities:

  • Converting a large square into two smaller squares or transforming a rectangle into an equivalent square.
  • Geometric proof that removing a square of side b from a square of side a leaves an L-shaped region (the gnomon) whose area equals a rectangle of length (a + b) and width (a - b)—the exact geometric origin of a² - b² = (a - b)(a + b)!
2. Brahmagupta and the Laws of Signs (628 CE)

In his magnum opus Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta, Acharya Brahmagupta established the first rigorous mathematical treatment of positive numbers (Dhana, fortune), negative numbers (Ṛṇa, debt), and zero (Kha / Śūnya). He proved the universal sign multiplication laws:

• "The product of two debts is a fortune" ⇒ (-a) × (-b) = +ab
• "The product of a debt and a fortune is a debt" ⇒ (-a) × (+b) = -ab
• Brahmagupta's Famous Biquadratic Identity:
(a² + b²)(c² + d²) = (ac - bd)² + (ad + bc)² = (ac + bd)² + (ad - bc)²

This profound identity later enabled Bhāskarācārya II to formulate the Chakravala method for solving indeterminate quadratic equations, centuries before European mathematicians rediscovered it.

3. Bhāskara II's Līlāvatī & Bījagaṇita (1150 CE)

Bhāskara II clearly distinguished between Vyakta-gaṇita (arithmetic of manifest, known numbers) and Avyakta-gaṇita or Bījagaṇita (algebra of unmanifest, unknown seeds). He provided poetic rules for computing squares (varga) and cubes (ghana) of sums:

"The square of the sum of two quantities equals the sum of their individual squares increased by twice their product." — Bhāskara II (Līlāvatī, Vargaparikarma)

Across the ancient world, Greek geometer Euclid (Elements Book II, c. 300 BCE) and Persian scholar Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (820 CE, Kitab al-Jabr wa-l-Muqabala) used geometric figures to visually validate these algebraic laws.

2.3 Identity I: Square of a Binomial Sum — (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²

Identity I describes what happens when a quantity made of two added parts (a + b) is multiplied by itself.

Algebraic Proof (The Distributive Law):
(a + b)² = (a + b)(a + b)
Applying the distributive property of multiplication over addition:
= a(a + b) + b(a + b)
= (a × a) + (a × b) + (b × a) + (b × b)
= a² + ab + ba + b²
Since multiplication is commutative for all real numbers (ab = ba):
= a² + ab + ab + b²
= a² + 2ab + b² (Hence Proved!)
a² Two Green Rects = 2ab Gold Square = b² Total Area of Large Square: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²

Mental Arithmetic Application of Identity I

How do mental math champions square numbers like 105 or 42 in their heads in 2 seconds? They split the number into a convenient round base plus a small remainder:

1. Calculate (105)² without long multiplication:
Write 105 as (100 + 5). Here a = 100, b = 5.
(105)² = (100 + 5)² = 100² + 2(100)(5) + 5²
= 10,000 + 1,000 + 25 = 11,025.

2. Calculate (42)²:
Write 42 as (40 + 2). Here a = 40, b = 2.
(42)² = 40² + 2(40)(2) + 2² = 1,600 + 160 + 4 = 1,764.

2.4 Identity II: Square of a Binomial Difference — (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b²

What happens when we square the difference of two terms? Let us prove it both algebraically and geometrically using the famous principle of Inclusion-Exclusion.

Method 1 (Distributive Law):
(a - b)² = (a - b)(a - b) = a(a - b) - b(a - b)
= a² - ab - ba + (-b)(-b)
Since (-b)(-b) = +b² and ab = ba:
= a² - 2ab + b²

Method 2 (Substitution in Identity I):
Replace 'b' with '(-b)' in (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²:
(a + (-b))² = a² + 2a(-b) + (-b)²
(a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b² (Notice that the square of (-b) is still positive +b²!)

Mental Arithmetic Application of Identity II
1. Calculate (97)² without direct multiplication:
97 = (100 - 3). Here a = 100, b = 3.
(97)² = 100² - 2(100)(3) + 3²
= 10,000 - 600 + 9 = 9,400 + 9 = 9,409.

2. Calculate (49)²:
49 = (50 - 1). Here a = 50, b = 1.
(49)² = 50² - 2(50)(1) + 1²
= 2,500 - 100 + 1 = 2,401.

2.5 Identity III: Difference of Two Squares — a² - b² = (a - b)(a + b)

Identity III is without doubt the most frequently tested and most powerful identity in high school mathematics. It links a subtraction of two squared terms directly to the product of their difference and their sum!

Algebraic Proof:
RHS = (a - b)(a + b) = a(a + b) - b(a + b)
= a² + ab - ba - b²
Since ab = ba, the cross-terms (+ab and -ba) cancel each other out completely:
= a² + 0 - b²
= a² - b² = LHS (Hence Proved!)

Numerical Speed Calculations & Chained Factorisation

1. Evaluating products of numbers symmetric around a round number:

• 104 × 96 = (100 + 4)(100 - 4) = 100² - 4² = 10,000 - 16 = 9,984
• 53 × 47 = (50 + 3)(50 - 3) = 50² - 3² = 2,500 - 9 = 2,491
• 10.5 × 9.5 = (10 + 0.5)(10 - 0.5) = 10² - (0.5)² = 100 - 0.25 = 99.75

2. Higher Power Chained Factorisation:

x⁴ - y⁴ = (x²)² - (y²)² = (x² - y²)(x² + y²)
Applying Identity III again to the first bracket (x² - y²):
= (x - y)(x + y)(x² + y²)

Similarly for degree 8:
x⁸ - 1 = (x⁴ - 1)(x⁴ + 1) = (x² - 1)(x² + 1)(x⁴ + 1) = (x - 1)(x + 1)(x² + 1)(x⁴ + 1)

2.6 Identity IV: Product of Two Binomials with Common Variable — (x + a)(x + b)

What happens when the first term in both binomials is identical (x), but the second terms are different constants (a and b)?

Algebraic Proof:
(x + a)(x + b) = x(x + b) + a(x + b)
= x² + bx + ax + ab
Factoring out x from the middle two terms (bx + ax = (b + a)x = (a + b)x):
(x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab
Sign Combination Standard Formula Form Concrete Algebraic Example
Both Positive (+, +) (x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab (x + 4)(x + 7) = x² + (4 + 7)x + (4)(7) = x² + 11x + 28
First Positive, Second Negative (+, -) (x + a)(x - b) = x² + (a - b)x - ab (x + 8)(x - 3) = x² + (8 - 3)x + (8)(-3) = x² + 5x - 24
First Negative, Second Positive (-, +) (x - a)(x + b) = x² - (a - b)x - ab (x - 9)(x + 4) = x² + (-9 + 4)x + (-9)(4) = x² - 5x - 36
Both Negative (-, -) (x - a)(x - b) = x² - (a + b)x + ab (x - 5)(x - 6) = x² + (-5 - 6)x + (-5)(-6) = x² - 11x + 30
The Splitting-the-Middle-Term Golden Bridge

Identity IV is the exact engine behind splitting the middle term to factorise quadratic trinomials x² + Bx + C:

  • We seek two numbers a and b such that their sum equals B (a + b = B) and their product equals C (ab = C).
  • Once found, the quadratic trinomial immediately factorises into (x + a)(x + b)!

2.7 Identity V: Square of a Trinomial — (a + b + c)²

When an expression consists of three distinct terms (a trinomial), squaring it creates 3 pure square terms and 3 pairwise product cross-terms!

Algebraic Derivation (Grouping Method):
Treat the first two terms (a + b) as a single composite variable u. Let u = a + b.
Then (a + b + c)² = (u + c)²
Using Identity I on (u + c)²:
= u² + 2uc + c²
Now substitute back u = (a + b):
= (a + b)² + 2(a + b)c + c²
Expand (a + b)² using Identity I, and expand 2(a + b)c using distributive law:
= (a² + 2ab + b²) + (2ac + 2bc) + c²
Rearranging in alphabetical, symmetric order:
(a + b + c)² = a² + b² + c² + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca

Master Rule for Handling Negative Signs in Trinomials

When some terms in the trinomial are negative, students often make errors with minus signs. Follow this infallible 2-step method:

1. Expanding (Forward Direction): Rewrite using plus signs with negative components inside parentheses:

Example: Expand (2x - 3y - 4z)²
Step 1: Rewrite as [(2x) + (-3y) + (-4z)]²
Step 2: Here a = 2x, b = -3y, c = -4z
= (2x)² + (-3y)² + (-4z)² + 2(2x)(-3y) + 2(-3y)(-4z) + 2(-4z)(2x)
= 4x² + 9y² + 16z² - 12xy + 24yz - 16zx
(Notice: Squares are ALWAYS positive! Minus × Minus in 2(-3y)(-4z) produces +24yz!)

2. Factorising (Reverse Direction): Given 4x² + 9y² + 16z² - 12xy + 24yz - 16zx, which variable is negative?

Look at the two NEGATIVE cross terms: -12xy and -16zx.
What variable is present in BOTH negative terms? It is x!
Therefore, the x-term must carry the negative sign (or both y and z carry negative signs):
= [-(2x) + 3y + 4z]² or [2x - 3y - 4z]²

2.8 Identity VI: Cube of a Binomial Sum — (a + b)³

Moving from 2D plane geometry to 3D solid geometry, what happens when we calculate the volume of a cube whose edge length is (a + b)?

Algebraic Proof:
(a + b)³ = (a + b)(a + b)²
Substitute Identity I for (a + b)²:
= (a + b)(a² + 2ab + b²)
Distribute (a + b) across the quadratic terms:
= a(a² + 2ab + b²) + b(a² + 2ab + b²)
= (a³ + 2a²b + ab²) + (a²b + 2ab² + b³)
Collect like terms (2a²b + a²b = 3a²b; ab² + 2ab² = 3ab²):
(a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³

Factoring out common 3ab from the two middle terms gives the Compact Factorisation Form:
(a + b)³ = a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b)

2.9 Identity VII: Cube of a Binomial Difference — (a - b)³

Just as Identity II was derived from Identity I by replacing b with (-b), Identity VII is derived directly from Identity VI:

Algebraic Derivation:
Replace 'b' with '(-b)' in (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³:
(a + (-b))³ = a³ + 3a²(-b) + 3a(-b)² + (-b)³
Since (-b)² = +b² and (-b)³ = -b³:
(a - b)³ = a³ - 3a²b + 3ab² - b³

Compact Factorisation Form (Factoring -3ab from middle terms):
(a - b)³ = a³ - b³ - 3ab(a - b)
The Alternating Signs Memory Key

Notice the rhythm of signs in the expanded polynomial form:

(a - b)³ = +a³ - 3a²b + 3ab² - b³
Signs alternate strictly: Positive (+), Negative (-), Positive (+), Negative (-)!
In the compact form, EVERY minus sign stays minus: (a - b)³ = a³ - b³ - 3ab(a - b).
Mental Evaluation of Large Cubes
1. Evaluate (99)³ without multiplying 99 × 99 × 99:
Write 99 as (100 - 1). Here a = 100, b = 1.
(99)³ = (100 - 1)³ = 100³ - 3(100)²(1) + 3(100)(1)² - 1³
= 1,000,000 - 3(10,000) + 300 - 1
= 1,000,000 - 30,000 + 300 - 1
= 970,000 + 299 = 970,299.

2. Evaluate (102)³:
102 = (100 + 2). Here a = 100, b = 2.
(102)³ = 100³ + 3(100)²(2) + 3(100)(2)² + 2³
= 1,000,000 + 60,000 + 1,200 + 8 = 1,061,208.

2.10 Factorisation of Sum and Difference of Two Cubes: a³ + b³ and a³ - b³

A frequent source of student confusion is mixing up (a + b)³ (the cube of a sum) with a³ + b³ (the sum of two cubes). Let us derive the precise factorisation of a³ + b³ and a³ - b³:

1. Derivation of Sum of Two Cubes (a³ + b³):
We know from Identity VI: (a + b)³ = a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b)
Transposing 3ab(a + b) to the LHS:
a³ + b³ = (a + b)³ - 3ab(a + b)
Factor out common factor (a + b):
a³ + b³ = (a + b) [(a + b)² - 3ab]
Expand (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² inside the bracket:
a³ + b³ = (a + b) [a² + 2ab + b² - 3ab]
Combine like terms (+2ab - 3ab = -ab):
a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² - ab + b²)

2. Derivation of Difference of Two Cubes (a³ - b³):
From Identity VII: (a - b)³ = a³ - b³ - 3ab(a - b)
Transposing -3ab(a - b) to LHS:
a³ - b³ = (a - b)³ + 3ab(a - b)
Factor out common factor (a - b):
a³ - b³ = (a - b) [(a - b)² + 3ab]
Expand (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b²:
a³ - b³ = (a - b) [a² - 2ab + b² + 3ab]
Combine like terms (-2ab + 3ab = +ab):
a³ - b³ = (a - b)(a² + ab + b²)
The "SOAP" Memory Rule for Signs

To never misplace a sign in cubic factorisations, remember the acronym SOAP:

  • S = Same sign: The first binomial factor takes the same sign as the original expression (a³ + b³ starts with (a + b); a³ - b³ starts with (a - b)).
  • O = Opposite sign: The middle cross-term in the quadratic trinomial has the opposite sign (for a³ + b³, middle term is -ab; for a³ - b³, middle term is +ab).
  • AP = Always Positive: The last term (+b²) is always positive!

2.11 Identity VIII: The Grand Three-Variable Cubic & The Half-Sum Formulation

Identity VIII is the pinnacle of secondary algebra. It links the sum of three cubes minus thrice their product to a product of a linear factor and a quadratic factor:

Identity VIII:
a³ + b³ + c³ - 3abc = (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² - ab - bc - ca)

Step-by-Step Rigorous Proof:
LHS = a³ + b³ + c³ - 3abc
Group the first two cubes: (a³ + b³) = (a + b)³ - 3ab(a + b)
So, LHS = [(a + b)³ + c³] - 3ab(a + b) - 3abc
Now treat (a + b) as X, so we have [X³ + c³]:
Using X³ + c³ = (X + c)(X² - Xc + c²):
= [(a + b) + c] [(a + b)² - (a + b)c + c²] - 3ab(a + b + c)
= (a + b + c) [a² + 2ab + b² - ac - bc + c²] - 3ab(a + b + c)
Notice that (a + b + c) is now a common factor in both terms! Factor it out:
= (a + b + c) [a² + 2ab + b² - ac - bc + c² - 3ab]
Combine like terms (+2ab - 3ab = -ab):
= (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² - ab - bc - ca) (Hence Proved!)
The Symmetric Half-Sum Formulation

Multiply and divide the quadratic factor (a² + b² + c² - ab - bc - ca) by 2:

a² + b² + c² - ab - bc - ca = 1/2 [2a² + 2b² + 2c² - 2ab - 2bc - 2ca]
Rearrange the terms inside the square brackets:
= 1/2 [(a² - 2ab + b²) + (b² - 2bc + c²) + (c² - 2ca + a²)]
Each grouped term is a perfect square!
= 1/2 [(a - b)² + (b - c)² + (c - a)²]

Therefore, Identity VIII can be written symmetrically as:
a³ + b³ + c³ - 3abc = 1/2 (a + b + c) [(a - b)² + (b - c)² + (c - a)²]

Profound Mathematical Insight: Since the square of any real number is non-negative ((a - b)² ≥ 0, (b - c)² ≥ 0, (c - a)² ≥ 0), the term [(a - b)² + (b - c)² + (c - a)²] is always ≥ 0! It equals zero if and only if a = b = c!

2.12 The Zero-Sum Conditional Identity: If a + b + c = 0, then a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc

This single theorem is one of the most frequently asked questions in CBSE Class 9 examinations and mathematics olympiads.

The Zero-Sum Theorem

Statement: If the sum of three numbers is zero, i.e., a + b + c = 0, then the sum of their cubes is strictly equal to thrice their product:

If a + b + c = 0  ⇒  a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc

Proof:

From Identity VIII:
a³ + b³ + c³ - 3abc = (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² - ab - bc - ca)
Given that a + b + c = 0:
a³ + b³ + c³ - 3abc = (0) × (a² + b² + c² - ab - bc - ca) = 0
Transposing -3abc to the RHS:
a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc (Hence Proved!)
Instant Evaluation Without Calculating Cubes
Problem 1: Evaluate (-12)³ + 7³ + 5³ without calculating cubes.
Let a = -12, b = 7, c = 5.
Check sum: a + b + c = -12 + 7 + 5 = -12 + 12 = 0.
Since a + b + c = 0, we can apply a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc directly!
Sum = 3(-12)(7)(5) = 3 × (-12) × 35 = -36 × 35 = -1,260.

Problem 2: Evaluate 28³ + (-15)³ + (-13)³.
Let a = 28, b = -15, c = -13.
Check sum: a + b + c = 28 + (-15) + (-13) = 28 - 28 = 0.
Sum = 3(28)(-15)(-13) = 84 × (+195) = 16,380.

2.14 Modern Scientific & Real-World Applications of Algebraic Identities

Algebraic identities are not just abstract paper exercises—they drive breakthrough engineering, aerospace design, high-frequency finance, and supercomputer algorithms:

1. Computer Science: The Karatsuba Multiplication Algorithm (1960)

When computers multiply two huge 1,000-digit integers, grade-school long multiplication takes 4 multiplications of n/2 digits: (ax + b)(cx + d) = ac·x² + (ad + bc)x + bd. In 1960, Soviet mathematician Anatoly Karatsuba used an algebraic identity to compute (ad + bc) using only one multiplication:

(ad + bc) = (a + b)(c + d) - ac - bd

Because ac and bd are already computed, Karatsuba reduced 4 multiplications to 3! This recursive speedup runs inside every computer processor today for RSA encryption, digital security, and blockchain verification.

2. Aerospace & Classical Physics: Kinetic Energy Differential

When a rocket accelerates from velocity v₁ to v₂, the mechanical work done by its thrusters equals the difference in kinetic energy:

ΔK = 1/2 m v₂² - 1/2 m v₁² = 1/2 m (v₂² - v₁²)
Using Identity III: ΔK = 1/2 m (v₂ - v₁)(v₂ + v₁) = [m(v₂ - v₁)] × [(v₂ + v₁)/2] = Impulse × Average Velocity!

Identity III enables rocket telemetry software to compute energy expenditure instantaneously from radar doppler measurements.

Applying Identity IV: (x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab with a = 5, b = 3:
= x² + (5 + 3)x + (5)(3) = x² + 8x + 15 m².

Sub-question (ii): If the effective usable ceiling area after cutting the skylight is (x² + 8x + 11) m², express the area removed using an algebraic identity.

Skylight Area = (Gross Area) - (Effective Area) = (x² + 8x + 15) - (x² + 8x + 11) = 4 m².
Since the skylight is a square of side 2 m, its area is 2² = 4 m².
Difference in Area = Gross Area - 2² = (x² + 8x + 15) - 4 = x² + 8x + 11 m².

Sub-question (iii): If x = 7 meters, calculate the exact usable floor area and cost of Italian marble tiling at ₹250 per square meter.

For x = 7 m:
Usable Area = 7² + 8(7) + 11 = 49 + 56 + 11 = 116 m².
Total Cost = 116 m² × ₹250/m² = ₹29,000.

Case Study 2: ISRO Rocket Fuel Tank Volumetric Engineering
Aerospace Engineering Context

ISRO engineers are modeling cryogenic propellant tanks for a new launch vehicle. The liquid hydrogen tank has a volume represented by the polynomial V(x) = 8x³ + 36x² + 54x + 27 cubic meters. The engineers need to find the physical dimensions of this tank and determine its storage capacity under cryogenic pressure.

Sub-question (i): Factorise V(x) to determine if the propellant tank is a perfect cube, and find its side length expression.

Look at the terms: 8x³ = (2x)³ and 27 = 3³.
Check middle terms:
• 3(2x)²(3) = 3(4x²)(3) = 36x² (matches!)
• 3(2x)(3)² = 3(2x)(9) = 54x (matches!)
Using Identity VI: a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³ = (a + b)³.
V(x) = (2x + 3)³.
Conclusion: Yes, the tank is a perfect cube of side length (2x + 3) meters.

Sub-question (ii): If manufacturing tolerances require side length to decrease by 2 meters to (2x + 1), find the new volume expression.

New Volume = (2x + 1)³ = (2x)³ + 3(2x)²(1) + 3(2x)(1)² + 1³ = 8x³ + 12x² + 6x + 1 m³.

Sub-question (iii): Find the exact reduction in fuel tank volume if x = 2 meters.

Initial Side = 2(2) + 3 = 7 m ⇒ Initial Volume = 7³ = 343 m³.
Reduced Side = 2(2) + 1 = 5 m ⇒ Reduced Volume = 5³ = 125 m³.
Volume Difference = 343 - 125 = 218 m³.

PYQ 5 (CBSE 2019): Prove that (a + b + c)³ - a³ - b³ - c³ = 3(a + b)(b + c)(c + a)
Step 1: Regroup (a + b + c)³ as [(a + b) + c]³

[(a + b) + c]³ = (a + b)³ + c³ + 3(a + b)c[(a + b) + c]
Now substitute this into LHS:
LHS = (a + b)³ + c³ + 3(a + b)c(a + b + c) - a³ - b³ - c³.

Step 2: Expand (a + b)³ in Terms of a³ + b³

(a + b)³ = a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b).
Substitute back:
LHS = [a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b)] + c³ + 3c(a + b)(a + b + c) - a³ - b³ - c³
The terms a³, b³, c³ cancel with -a³, -b³, -c³!
LHS = 3ab(a + b) + 3c(a + b)(a + b + c).

= 3(a + b) [ab + c(a + b + c)]
= 3(a + b) [ab + ca + cb + c²]
= 3(a + b) [a(b + c) + c(b + c)]
= 3(a + b)(b + c)(c + a) = RHS. (Hence Proved!)

5 Fatal Exam Hall Blunders (Trap Alerts)

Blunder 1: Distributing the Exponent Over Addition: (a + b)² ≠ a² + b²

The Rookie Mistake: Writing (x + 3)² = x² + 9.
Why It Is Wrong: You completely forgot the middle cross term 2ab! Geometrically, you forgot the two rectangles of area 3x each (total 6x)!
The Right Way: (x + 3)² = x² + 2(x)(3) + 3² = x² + 6x + 9.
Teacher's Tip: Never distribute powers over '+' or '-' signs! Powers distribute over multiplication and division only: (ab)² = a²b², but (a + b)² ≠ a² + b²!

Blunder 2: Writing Negative Square Terms: (-3y)² ≠ -9y²

The Rookie Mistake: In expanding (2x - 3y + 4z)², writing the second term as -9y².
Why It Is Wrong: The square of any real number (positive or negative) is always non-negative: (-3y)² = (-3y) × (-3y) = +9y²!
The Right Way: In any trinomial expansion (a + b + c)², the three squared terms a², b², c² are ALWAYS POSITIVE. Only the cross terms can be negative.

Blunder 3: Confusing (a + b)³ with (a³ + b³)

The Rookie Mistake: Using the formula for (a + b)³ when asked to factorise (a³ + b³).
Why It Is Wrong: (a + b)³ is the cube of a binomial sum (which equals a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³). Whereas a³ + b³ is the sum of two cubes, which factorises into (a + b)(a² - ab + b²)!
Teacher's Tip: Use the "SOAP" rule for a³ + b³: Same sign (a + b), Opposite middle (-ab), Always Positive (+b²).

Blunder 4: Applying Zero-Sum Identity Without Verifying a + b + c = 0

The Rookie Mistake: Evaluating 10³ + 20³ + 30³ as 3(10)(20)(30) = 18,000.
Why It Is Wrong: 10 + 20 + 30 = 60 ≠ 0! The identity a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc is a conditional identity that holds true if and only if a + b + c = 0.
The Right Way: Always show the check: "Since a + b + c = 0..." before writing a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc.

Blunder 5: Missing Common Monomial Factor Before Identity Matching

The Rookie Mistake: Trying to factorise 12x² - 27y² directly without factoring out 3.
Why It Is Wrong: Neither 12 nor 27 is a perfect square! Students get stuck thinking it is impossible.
The Right Way: Always extract the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) first: 12x² - 27y² = 3(4x² - 9y²) = 3(2x - 3y)(2x + 3y).

15 Golden High-Yield Revision Points

  1. An algebraic identity is an equation valid for all real values of the variables, unlike a conditional equation which holds only for specific roots.
  2. Ancient Indian geometers in the Sulba Sūtras (Baudhāyana, Āpastamba) proved identities via geometric dissections of ropes and brick altars.
  3. Acharya Brahmagupta (628 CE) established the sign rules for Dhana (positive) and Ṛṇa (negative) and formulated the Brahmagupta product identity.
  4. Identity I: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² represents a square partitioned into two squares (a², b²) and two identical rectangles (2ab).
  5. Identity II: (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b² uses the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion, adding back the double-subtracted corner b².
  6. Identity III: a² - b² = (a - b)(a + b) transforms an L-shaped region of area a² - b² into a single rectangle of length (a + b) and width (a - b).
  7. Identity IV: (x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab is the foundation of the middle-term splitting method for factoring quadratic trinomials.
  8. Identity V: (a + b + c)² = a² + b² + c² + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca produces 3 pure squares (always positive) and 3 pairwise cross terms.
  9. To find the negative variable in a 6-term trinomial square, find the variable common to both negative cross terms.
  10. Identity VI: (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³ decomposes a 3D cube into 8 constituent polyhedra (1 a³ cube, 1 b³ cube, 3 a²b slabs, 3 ab² rods).
  11. Identity VII: (a - b)³ = a³ - 3a²b + 3ab² - b³ features alternating signs: +, -, +, -.
  12. Sum & Diff of Cubes: a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² - ab + b²) and a³ - b³ = (a - b)(a² + ab + b²) follow the SOAP sign rule.
  13. Identity VIII: a³ + b³ + c³ - 3abc = (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² - ab - bc - ca) = 1/2(a + b + c)[(a - b)² + (b - c)² + (c - a)²].
  14. Zero-Sum Condition: If a + b + c = 0, then a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc. Always check this first when cubes of three numbers are added!
  15. The algebraic expression (a² + b² + c² - ab - bc - ca) is always ≥ 0 for all real numbers, and equals 0 if and only if a = b = c.
