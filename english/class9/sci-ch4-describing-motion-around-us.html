2.1 The Nature of Motion & Relativity of Rest

Look around you right now. The wall in front of you, the study table, and the books appear completely stationary. But are they truly at rest? If an astronaut looking through a telescope on the Moon observes you right now, you, your chair, and your entire house are hurtling through space at roughly 30 kilometres every single second because Earth revolves around the Sun!

Core Scientific Law: Rest and Motion are Relative Terms An object is never in an absolute state of rest or absolute state of motion. An object is said to be in motion if its position changes continuously with respect to a stationary observer or fixed reference point as time passes. If its position does not change, it is said to be at rest.

The Reference Point (Origin)

To describe the location of an object, we must first choose a fixed reference point, known as the Origin (often labeled as point O). For example, if we say: "The high school is 2 km north of the railway station," the railway station is our chosen reference point. Without specifying a reference point, describing an object's position or movement is physically meaningless.

Everyday Classroom Example: The Passenger in a Moving Bus Imagine you are sitting inside a bus moving smoothly at 50 km/h:
  • With respect to your friend sitting right next to you: You are completely at rest because the distance between you and your friend does not change over time.
  • With respect to a person standing by the roadside: You, your friend, and the bus are in rapid motion forward at 50 km/h.
  • With respect to you inside the bus: The roadside electric poles, houses, and trees appear to be rushing backwards!
Hence, the state of rest or motion depends entirely upon the chosen frame of reference!

2.2 Motion Along a Straight Line: Distance vs Displacement

The simplest type of motion is motion along a straight line, known in physics as rectilinear motion (1-dimensional motion). When an object moves along a straight line, say along the X-axis from the origin O, we define two fundamental physical quantities to describe how far it travels: Distance and Displacement.

Distance (ଦୂରତା)

  • Definition: The total length of the actual path covered by an object during its journey, irrespective of the direction in which it travels.
  • Nature: Scalar quantity (has only magnitude, no direction).
  • Sign: Can only be positive or zero (for stationary bodies). It can never be negative.
  • Zero Value: If a body has moved, its distance can never be zero!
  • SI Unit: Metre (m).

Displacement (ବିସ୍ଥାପନ)

  • Definition: The shortest straight-line distance directed from the initial position to the final position of an object.
  • Nature: Vector quantity (has both numerical magnitude AND a specific direction).
  • Sign: Can be positive, negative, or zero depending on direction relative to origin.
  • Zero Value: Can be zero even if the body travels a huge distance, provided it returns to its starting point!
  • SI Unit: Metre (m).

Figure 4.2: Visual proof showing why Distance is path-dependent, whereas Displacement depends only on initial and final spatial coordinates. In a closed loop, Displacement is identically ZERO.

Golden Rule: Magnitude of Displacement vs Distance The magnitude of displacement is always less than or equal to the distance travelled:
|Displacement| ≤ Distance
Displacement equals distance if and only if the object moves along a straight line in a constant, unchanging direction without turning back! The moment it turns or curves, distance exceeds displacement.

2.3 Uniform Motion and Non-Uniform Motion

Imagine two vehicles travelling on a highway. Car A covers exactly 10 metres in the 1st second, 10 metres in the 2nd second, 10 metres in the 3rd second, and 10 metres in every subsequent second. On the other hand, Car B covers 5 metres in the 1st second, gets stuck behind a truck and covers only 2 metres in the 2nd second, then speeds up on an open stretch to cover 18 metres in the 3rd second. How do physicists classify these two patterns?

Uniform Motion (ସମଗତି)

Definition: When an object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, no matter how small these time intervals may be, the object is said to be in uniform motion.

  • Speed: Remains constant throughout the entire motion.
  • Acceleration: Exactly zero (a = 0 m/s²).
  • Example: A car cruising on cruise control on an empty straight expressway; hands of a mechanical clock; Earth rotating on its axis.

Non-Uniform Motion (ଅସମଗତି)

Definition: When an object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time, the object is said to be in non-uniform motion.

  • Speed: Changes continuously (either increases or decreases).
  • Acceleration: Non-zero (a ≠ 0 m/s²); motion is accelerated.
  • Example: A car driving through crowded city traffic; a runner in a 100m sprint speeding up from starting blocks; a train pulling into a station.

2.4 Measuring the Rate of Motion: Speed

Different objects take different amounts of time to cover a given distance. A cheetah runs faster than a tortoise; a bullet train moves faster than a bicycle. To quantify how fast or slow an object moves, we define the physical quantity called Speed.

Definition of Speed (ଦ୍ରୁତି / ବେଗ) The speed of an object is defined as the distance travelled by the object per unit of time.
Speed (v) = Distance (s) ÷ Time (t) ⇒ v = s / t
  • Nature: Scalar quantity (it has magnitude only; no directional specification).
  • SI Unit: Metre per second (m/s or m s⁻¹). Other everyday units include cm/s and km/h.
  • Always Non-Negative: Speed can be zero or positive. It can never be negative.

Average Speed vs Instantaneous Speed

In real life, moving bodies rarely maintain a single steady speed. When you travel from Cuttack to Bhubaneswar by bus (a distance of ~30 km) in 1 hour, your bus stops at red lights, slows down in bazaars, and speeds up on bridges. Its speed varies every second. Hence, we calculate the Average Speed for the entire journey:

Average Speed (v_avg) = Total Distance Travelled ÷ Total Time Taken = s_total / t_total

Instantaneous Speed: The speed of a vehicle at an exact, specific fraction of a second. This is precisely what the speedometer on your motorcycle or car dashboard shows!

Topper's Shortcut: The 5/18 Speed Unit Conversion Secret In 90% of exam numericals, speed is given in km/h (kilometres per hour), but SI formulas strictly require m/s. Never do long multiplications in the exam hall! Use this derivation:
1 km / 1 h = 1000 metres ÷ 3600 seconds = 10 / 36 = 5 / 18 m/s
  • To convert km/h → m/s: Multiply by 5/18 (e.g., 72 km/h × 5/18 = 4 × 5 = 20 m/s; 36 km/h = 10 m/s; 54 km/h = 15 m/s; 90 km/h = 25 m/s).
  • To convert m/s → km/h: Multiply by 18/5 (e.g., 25 m/s × 18/5 = 5 × 18 = 90 km/h).

2.5 Speed with Direction: Velocity

Suppose weather forecasters announce: "A cyclonic storm is moving at 120 km/h." This information is incomplete! Will the storm hit your coastal city, or will it move away towards the deep sea? To answer this, meteorologists must specify the direction of motion (e.g., "120 km/h North-Northwest towards Puri coast"). Speed combined with a direction is called Velocity.

Definition of Velocity (ପରିବେଗ) Velocity is defined as the rate of change of displacement with time, or simply the speed of an object in a specified direction.
Velocity (v⃗) = Displacement (s⃗) ÷ Time taken (t)
  • Nature: Vector quantity (possesses both numerical magnitude and spatial direction).
  • SI Unit: Metre per second (m/s or m s⁻¹).
  • Sign: Can be positive, negative, or zero.
  • Ways to Change Velocity: Velocity changes if:
    1. The speed of the object changes (speeds up or slows down).
    2. The direction of motion changes (even if speed remains strictly constant!).
    3. Both speed and direction change simultaneously.

Two Key Formulas for Average Velocity

Depending on the nature of the problem, CBSE students must choose between two distinct formulas for Average Velocity:

Case 1 (General Case): When an object moves with variable speed along arbitrary paths:

Average Velocity = Net Total Displacement ÷ Total Time Taken

Case 2 (Uniform Rate of Velocity Change): When an object moves along a straight line and its velocity changes at a uniform rate (constant acceleration), the average velocity is simply the arithmetic mean of the initial velocity (u) and final velocity (v):

Average Velocity (v_avg) = (Initial Velocity + Final Velocity) ÷ 2 = (u + v) / 2

2.6 Rate of Change of Velocity: Acceleration

In uniform motion along a straight line, velocity remains completely constant with time; the change in velocity during any time interval is zero. But in non-uniform motion, velocity varies from moment to moment. A vehicle accelerates when you press the throttle and decelerates when you hit the brakes. How do we quantify this rate of change?

Definition of Acceleration (ତ୍ୱରଣ) Acceleration is defined as the measure of the change in velocity of an object per unit time.
Acceleration (a) = (Final Velocity - Initial Velocity) ÷ Time taken ⇒ a = (v - u) / t
  • Symbol: a (initial velocity = u, final velocity = v, elapsed time = t).
  • Nature: Vector quantity. Direction is taken as positive if it is in the direction of velocity, and negative if it opposes velocity.
  • SI Unit: Metre per second squared (m/s² or m s⁻²).

Positive Acceleration (ତ୍ୱରଣ)

Occurs when velocity increases over time (v > u). The acceleration vector points in the same direction as the motion of the body. Example: A train departing a platform or a ball rolling down a steep ramp.

Negative Acceleration / Retardation / Deceleration (ମନ୍ଦନ)

Occurs when velocity decreases over time (v < u). Here, a is negative and acts opposite to the direction of motion. Example: Applying brakes to stop a car, or throwing a stone vertically upwards against gravity.

Uniform vs Non-Uniform Acceleration

  • Uniform Acceleration: If an object travels in a straight line and its velocity increases or decreases by equal amounts in equal intervals of time, the acceleration is said to be uniform. Classic Example: The motion of a freely falling body dropped near Earth's surface (g ≈ 9.8 m/s²).
  • Non-Uniform Acceleration: If the velocity changes by unequal amounts in equal intervals of time. Classic Example: A car navigating city streets with varying throttle inputs, speed breakers, and traffic lights.

2.7 Graphical Representation of Motion: Calculus in Geometry

Graphs provide a powerful visual method to present basic information about various kinds of motion. To study the motion of an object along a straight line, two types of coordinate graphs are essential in Class 9 Science:

1. Distance-Time Graphs (s-t Graphs)

Time is always plotted along the horizontal X-axis (independent variable), and distance is plotted along the vertical Y-axis (dependent variable).

  • Physical Meaning of Slope: Consider two points A(t₁, s₁) and B(t₂, s₂) on a straight-line s-t graph.
    Slope = (Vertical Change) ÷ (Horizontal Change) = (s₂ - s₁) / (t₂ - t₁) = Distance / Time = Speed (v)
  • Steepness Rule: The steeper the line (higher slope angle θ), the greater is the speed of the body! If Graph Line A is steeper than Graph Line B, Car A is travelling faster than Car B.

2. Velocity-Time Graphs (v-t Graphs)

Time is represented along the X-axis, and velocity is plotted along the Y-axis. Velocity-time graphs unlock two tremendous kinematic secrets:

Secret 1: Slope = Acceleration

The slope of a velocity-time graph gives the acceleration of the body:

Slope = (v₂ - v₁) / (t₂ - t₁) = Δv / Δt = Acceleration (a)
  • If slope is horizontal (0°), a = 0 m/s² (uniform velocity).
  • If slope slants upward, acceleration is positive (speeding up).
  • If slope slants downward, acceleration is negative (retardation).

Secret 2: Area = Displacement

The total area bounded by the velocity-time curve and the time axis between any two time limits represents the displacement (distance) travelled by the object:

Area under v-t Graph = Velocity × Time = Distance (s)

For uniform velocity, Area = Rectangle = Height × Base = v × t.

For uniform acceleration, Area = Trapezium = Rectangle + Triangle.

2.8 Graphical Derivation of the Three Equations of Motion

When an object moves along a straight line with uniform acceleration, its velocity, acceleration, elapsed time, and distance covered are connected by three mathematical equations called the Equations of Motion. In CBSE Class 9 examinations, deriving these three equations by the graphical method carries 5 full marks!

Figure 4.5: Master Velocity-Time Construction for deriving all three equations of motion. A represents initial state (t = 0, v = u), B represents final state (t = t, v = v).

Derivation 1: Equation for Velocity-Time Relation (v = u + at)

Step-by-Step Graphical Derivation: Let an object have an initial velocity u at time t = 0 (represented by point A on the graph). It accelerates uniformly with acceleration a for time t and attains a final velocity v at time t (represented by point B).

From the graph, we draw perpendiculars:
  • BC perpendicular to time axis, so OC = t and BC = v.
  • AD parallel to OC, so AD = OC = t and DC = OA = u.
Now, notice that the total line segment BC consists of:
BC = BD + DC
Substituting BC = v and DC = u:
v = BD + u ⇒ BD = v - u --- (Equation 1)
By definition, acceleration (a) is the slope of the velocity-time line AB:
a = Slope of line AB = Change in velocity ÷ Time taken = BD / AD
Since AD = OC = t:
a = BD / t ⇒ BD = at --- (Equation 2)
Equating Equation 1 and Equation 2:
v - u = at ⇒ v = u + at
Hence Derived! This is the First Equation of Motion.

Derivation 2: Equation for Position-Time Relation (s = ut + ½ at²)

Step-by-Step Graphical Derivation: Let the distance travelled by the object in time t be s.
As proven earlier, the distance travelled s is given by the total area enclosed under the velocity-time graph line AB within the time interval 0 to t.

Looking at Figure 4.5, the total area is the area of the trapezium OABC:
s = Area of trapezium OABC = (Area of rectangle OADC) + (Area of triangle ABD)
Now let us calculate each geometric area separately:
  1. Area of rectangle OADC:
    Area = OA × OC = u × t = ut
  2. Area of triangle ABD:
    Area = ½ × Base × Height = ½ × AD × BD
    Since AD = t, and from our first derivation, BD = at:
    Area = ½ × t × at = ½ at²
Adding both areas together:
s = ut + ½ at²
Hence Derived! This is the Second Equation of Motion.

Derivation 3: Equation for Position-Velocity Relation (2as = v² - u²)

Step-by-Step Graphical Derivation: The distance s travelled by the object in time t is the area of trapezium OABC.
Recall the standard mathematical formula for the area of a trapezium:
Area of Trapezium = ½ × (Sum of parallel sides) × (Perpendicular distance between them)
In trapezium OABC:
  • The two parallel vertical sides are OA and BC.
  • The perpendicular distance between them is OC.
Therefore:
s = ½ × (OA + BC) × OC
Substitute OA = u, BC = v, and OC = t:
s = ½ × (u + v) × t = [(u + v) t] / 2 --- (Equation A)
Now, eliminate the time variable t using the first equation of motion (v = u + at):
v = u + at ⇒ at = v - u ⇒ t = (v - u) / a
Substitute this value of t into Equation A:
s = [(v + u) / 2] × [(v - u) / a]
Recall the algebraic identity: (v + u)(v - u) = v² - u²:
s = (v² - u²) / (2a)
Cross-multiplying 2a to the left side:
2as = v² - u² (or v² = u² + 2as)
Hence Derived! This is the Third Equation of Motion.

2.9 Motion Under Gravity: The Mechanics of Free Fall

When an object is dropped from a height or thrown vertically upwards, it moves under the influence of Earth's gravitational attraction alone (neglecting air resistance). This motion is a classic real-world example of uniform acceleration, known as Free Fall.

  • Acceleration Due to Gravity (g): Every object falling freely experiences a constant acceleration directed vertically downwards towards Earth's centre:
    g ≈ 9.8 m/s² (often approximated as 10 m/s² in school exam numericals)
  • Independence of Mass: Galileo Galilei famously demonstrated (from the Leaning Tower of Pisa) that in vacuum, all objects—whether a heavy iron cannonball or a light bird's feather—fall with the exact same acceleration g!

Case 1: Body Dropped Downwards

  • Initial Velocity: Dropped from rest ⇒ u = 0.
  • Acceleration: Velocity increases downwards ⇒ a = +g.
  • Modified Equations:
    v = gt
    h = ½ gt²
    v² = 2gh

Case 2: Body Thrown Vertically Upwards

  • Velocity at Top: Object momentarily stops ⇒ v = 0 at highest point h_max.
  • Acceleration: Gravity opposes motion ⇒ a = -g.
  • Modified Equations:
    0 = u - gt ⇒ t_ascent = u / g
    h_max = u² / (2g)
  • Time Symmetry: Time of ascent = Time of descent! Total flight time = 2u / g.

2.10 Uniform Circular Motion: Why Constant Speed Accelerates!

When an athlete runs around an octagonal track, she must change her direction of running 8 times in one complete lap. If the track is a dodecagon (12-sided polygon), she changes direction 12 times. What happens if the number of sides increases indefinitely? The polygonal track becomes a perfect circle!

On a circular track, the runner must continuously change her direction of motion at every single infinitesimal point of the path!

Definition of Uniform Circular Motion When an object moves along a circular path with constant speed, its motion is called Uniform Circular Motion.

Why is it an ACCELERATED Motion? Even though the numerical value of speed remains constant, the direction of motion changes continuously at every point along the tangent to the circle. Because velocity is a vector quantity (magnitude + direction), a continuous change in direction means velocity is continuously changing. A changing velocity produces an acceleration!
Speed in Circular Path (v) = Circumference ÷ Time = (2 × π × r) / t
Where:
  • r = radius of the circular orbit / track (in metres).
  • t = time taken for one complete revolution (in seconds).
  • π ≈ 22/7 ≈ 3.1416.

Activity 4.1: The Moving Bus and Relative Observers
Step 1: Core Concept

Motion and rest are not absolute properties; they are relative to the chosen frame of reference / observer.

Step 2: Observations Recorded

(i) For a passenger inside a moving bus, roadside trees, lamp posts, and houses appear to be rushing backwards.
(ii) For an observer standing on the pavement, the bus along with all its passengers is moving forward.
(iii) To a passenger sitting in the bus, the fellow passenger sitting beside him appears to be at complete rest.

Step 3: Scientific Reasoning

An object is at rest if its spatial coordinates do not change with respect to the observer over time. Inside the bus, the distance between two seated passengers remains constant (zero relative velocity). However, relative to the ground observer, both the bus and passengers change coordinates continuously.

Step 4: Real-World Analogy

Earth rotates eastward at ~1670 km/h at the equator, yet we feel stationary because we, our houses, and the surrounding atmosphere all move together at the exact same velocity.

Step 5: Final Conclusion

Conclusion: Rest and motion are relative terms. To describe any motion accurately, the reference frame must always be explicitly stated.

Activity 4.2: Illusion of Motion in Adjacent Trains & Day/Night Cycle
Step 1: Core Concept

Optical illusion of self-motion (vection) occurs when our reference frame moves smoothly relative to an adjacent object without mechanical jolts.

Step 2: Activity Inquiries

(a) Have you ever experienced that the train in which you are sitting appears to move while it is actually at rest?
(b) Why do we experience the cycle of day and night without directly feeling Earth's rotation?

Step 3: Scientific Explanation

(a) When sitting in a stationary train and the train on the parallel track begins to move slowly, our eyes detect relative motion between the two trains. Without looking at the fixed ground or station platform, our brain mistakenly attributes the relative velocity to our own train.
(b) Earth rotates uniformly from West to East. Because everything on Earth (including the atmosphere) shares this uniform angular motion, there is no relative motion between us and Earth. We observe the Sun, Moon, and stars appearing to rise in the East and set in the West.

Step 4: Diagnostic Rule

Always verify motion against an immovable, external reference point (like the railway platform or distant hills) rather than an adjacent moving vehicle.

Step 5: Final Takeaway

Apparent celestial motion (the Sun moving across the sky) is a direct consequence of Earth rotating beneath a celestial reference frame.

Relation between uniform speed, elapsed time, and distance: Distance (s) = Speed (v) × Time (t).

Formula: Distance s = v × t.

Step 4: Step-by-Step Calculation

In kilometres: s = 4 km/h × 0.2 h = 0.8 km.
In SI metres: s = 0.8 × 1000 m = 800 metres.

Step 5: Final Statement

The estimated distance between your home and the bus stop is 800 metres (0.8 km).

Activity 4.4: Thunder & Lightning: Estimating Storm Distance
Step 1: Core Concept

Speed of light in air (c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s) is vastly greater than the speed of sound in air (v_sound ≈ 343 m/s at 20°C). Hence, the flash of lightning reaches our eyes practically instantaneously (t ≈ 0), whereas the sound of thunder takes noticeable seconds to arrive.

Step 2: Experimental Procedure

On a cloudy stormy day, start a stopwatch the instant you see a lightning flash. Stop the watch when you hear the thunder clap. Suppose the measured time gap Δt = 5.0 seconds.

Step 3: Formula

Distance of thundercloud d = Speed of sound (v) × Time delay (Δt).

Step 4: Calculation

d = 343 m/s × 5.0 s = 1,715 metres = 1.715 km.

Step 5: Final Conclusion

The lightning strike occurred approximately 1.72 km away from the observer. Handy rule of thumb: Every 3 seconds of delay represents roughly 1 km of distance!

Activity 4.7: Stone Whirled in a Circle and Released Along Tangent
Step 1: Core Concept

At any instant during circular motion, the direction of linear velocity is directed along the geometric tangent to the circular path at that precise position.

Step 2: Experimental Steps

Tie a small stone to one end of a piece of thread. Hold the other end and whirl it around to describe a horizontal circular path at constant speed. Suddenly let go of the thread at different positions around the circle.

Step 3: Scientific Observation

The moment the thread is released, the centripetal tension vanishes instantly. The stone does NOT fly out radially away from the centre! Instead, it flies off straight along the tangent to the circular path at the exact point where it was released.

Step 4: Newton's First Law Connection

In accordance with the law of inertia, once the inward centripetal force is removed, the stone continues to move in a straight line in the direction it was moving at that very moment.

Step 5: Final Takeaway

This experimentally demonstrates that the direction of velocity in circular motion changes continuously and is always tangential to the circle at every point.

In-Text Questions Set 1 (Page 74 / After Distance & Displacement)

Q1: Can an object have zero displacement after moving through a distance? Support with an example.
Step 1: Core Concept

Displacement is the vector difference between the final position and the initial position (s⃗ = x_f - x_i), whereas distance is the total scalar path length.

Step 2: Direct Answer

YES, an object that has moved through a non-zero distance can definitely have zero displacement, provided its final position coincides with its initial position.

Step 3: Concrete Examples

Example 1 (Round Trip): A student walks from home to school (distance = 2 km) in the morning and returns back home in the afternoon. The total distance covered is 2 km + 2 km = 4 km. However, because the initial and final positions are identical (home), the net displacement is exactly 0 km.

Example 2 (Circular Track): An athlete runs one complete lap around a circular athletic track of radius 50 m. The distance covered is the circumference = 2πr = 314.16 m, but the displacement is 0 m.

Step 4: Vector Validation

Mathematically: s⃗ = x_final - x_initial = 0.

Step 5: Final Summary

Whenever a moving object returns to its starting point, its distance > 0, while its displacement = 0.

Q2 (Famous Farmer Numerical): Farmer moves along boundary of square field (side 10 m) in 40 s. Find displacement at 2 min 20 s.
Step 1: Core Concept

Displacement in 2D geometry is the shortest straight line connecting initial point A to final point C, calculated using Pythagoras' Theorem across the square diagonal.

Step 2: Given Data & SI Conversion

• Side of square field (a) = 10 m.
• Perimeter of square field = 4 × a = 4 × 10 m = 40 m.
• Time taken for 1 complete round = 40 s.
• Total elapsed time (t) = 2 minutes 20 seconds = (2 × 60) + 20 = 120 + 20 = 140 seconds.

Step 3: Calculating Rounds Completed

Number of rounds = Total time ÷ Time for 1 round = 140 s ÷ 40 s = 3.5 rounds.
• In 3 complete rounds, the farmer returns to the starting corner (say corner A), so displacement from 3 rounds is 0.
• In the remaining 0.5 (half) round, the farmer covers 0.5 × 40 m = 20 m along two sides (from A to B to C), reaching the diagonally opposite corner C!

Step 4: Calculating Diagonal Displacement (AC)

In right-angled triangle ABC (∠B = 90°):
AC² = AB² + BC²
AC² = (10)² + (10)² = 100 + 100 = 200
AC = √200 = √(100 × 2) = 10√2 metres.
Using √2 ≈ 1.414:
AC = 10 × 1.414 = 14.14 metres.

Step 5: Final Statement

The magnitude of displacement of the farmer from his initial position is 10√2 m (or 14.14 m) along the diagonal opposite corner.

Q3: Which of the following is true for displacement? (a) It cannot be zero. (b) Its magnitude is greater than the distance travelled.
Step 1: Core Concept

Fundamental property of displacement: |Displacement| ≤ Distance.

Step 2: Evaluation of Statement (a)

Statement (a) is FALSE. As proven in Q1, displacement CAN be zero whenever a moving object returns to its starting point or remains at rest.

Step 3: Evaluation of Statement (b)

Statement (b) is FALSE. The magnitude of displacement can NEVER be greater than the distance travelled. The straight line is the shortest path between two points. Hence, magnitude of displacement is either equal to distance (in straight unidirectional motion) or strictly less than distance.

Step 4: Golden Summary Inequality

0 ≤ |Displacement| ≤ Distance.

Step 5: Final Conclusion

Neither statement is true. Both statements (a) and (b) are physically false.

In-Text Questions Set 3 (Page 77 / After Acceleration)

Q1: When will you say a body is in (i) uniform acceleration? (ii) non-uniform acceleration?
Step 1: Core Concept

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = Δv / Δt.

Step 2: Uniform Acceleration Definition

A body is said to be in uniform acceleration if it travels in a straight line and its velocity increases or decreases by equal amounts in equal intervals of time, however small these intervals may be.
Example: A ball dropped freely from a high cliff falling under gravity (g = 9.8 m/s²), or a ball rolling down a smooth inclined plane.

Step 3: Non-Uniform Acceleration Definition

A body is said to be in non-uniform acceleration if its velocity changes by unequal amounts in equal intervals of time.
Example: A bus driving through a busy crowded marketplace, frequently accelerating, braking, and stopping.

Step 4: Graphical Representation

In a v-t graph, uniform acceleration is a straight inclined line; non-uniform acceleration is a curved or zigzag line.

Step 5: Final Summary

Uniform: Equal velocity changes in equal times. Non-uniform: Unequal velocity changes in equal times.

Q2 (Bus Braking Numerical): A bus decreases its speed from 80 km/h to 60 km/h in 5 s. Find acceleration.
Step 1: Core Concept

Acceleration formula: a = (v - u) / t. When velocity decreases, acceleration is negative (retardation).

Step 2: Given Data & Conversion to SI Units

• Initial speed (u) = 80 km/h = 80 × (5/18) = 400 / 18 = 22.22 m/s.
• Final speed (v) = 60 km/h = 60 × (5/18) = 300 / 18 = 16.67 m/s.
• Time taken (t) = 5 s.

Step 3: Formula

a = (v - u) / t.

Step 4: Calculation

Change in velocity: (v - u) = (300/18) - (400/18) = -100 / 18 = -5.56 m/s.
a = (-100 / 18) ÷ 5 = -100 / (18 × 5) = -100 / 90 = -10 / 9 = -1.11 m/s².

Step 5: Final Statement

The acceleration of the bus is -1.11 m/s² (or its retardation is 1.11 m/s²). The negative sign indicates that the bus is slowing down.

Q3 (Train Departing Station): Train starting from station attains 40 km/h in 10 minutes. Find acceleration.
Step 1: Core Concept

Whenever a body starts from a station or from rest, its initial velocity is zero (u = 0).

Step 2: Given Data & SI Conversion

• Initial velocity (u) = 0 m/s (starts from rest).
• Final velocity (v) = 40 km/h = 40 × (5/18) = 200 / 18 = 11.11 m/s.
• Time taken (t) = 10 minutes = 10 × 60 = 600 seconds.

Step 3: Formula

a = (v - u) / t.

Step 4: Calculation

a = (11.11 - 0) / 600 = (200 / 18) ÷ 600 = 200 / (18 × 600) = 2 / 108 = 1 / 54 ≈ 0.0185 m/s².

Step 5: Final Statement

The uniform acceleration of the train is 1/54 m/s² ≈ 0.0185 m/s².

Q1: What is the nature of distance-time graphs for uniform and non-uniform motion?
Step 1: Core Concept

The slope of a distance-time graph represents speed.

Step 2: For Uniform Motion

The distance-time graph is a straight line inclined to the time axis (passing through the origin if s = 0 at t = 0). A constant slope signifies constant speed.

Step 3: For Non-Uniform Motion

The distance-time graph is a curved line (non-linear). If the body is speeding up, the curve bends upwards; if slowing down, it bends downwards.

Step 4: Key Diagnostic

Straight line = Uniform speed. Curved line = Non-uniform speed.

Step 5: Final Summary

Uniform motion gives a straight inclined line; non-uniform motion gives a curve.

Slope of s-t graph = Speed = Δs / Δt.

Step 2: Geometric Deduction

A line parallel to the horizontal time axis has a vertical change of zero (Δs = 0). Therefore, its slope is exactly zero.

Step 3: Physical Interpretation

As time elapses, the distance of the object from the origin remains completely unchanged. The object is not moving at all!

Step 4: Mathematical Form

v = 0 m/s.

Step 5: Final Statement

The object is at rest (stationary).

Step 3: Physical Interpretation

The object is travelling with uniform speed (or uniform velocity) with zero acceleration.

Step 4: Common Confusion Warning

Do not confuse this with Q2! In an s-t graph, a horizontal line means at rest. In a v-t graph, a horizontal line means moving with constant speed!

Step 5: Final Statement

The object is moving with constant (uniform) speed / zero acceleration.

The area occupied below the velocity-time graph represents the magnitude of displacement (or distance) travelled by the object.

Step 4: Sign Sensitivity

For motion with reversing direction, area above the time axis is positive displacement, and area below the time axis is negative displacement.

The quantity measured is Displacement (or Distance travelled).

Exercise Q1 (Athlete on Circular Track): Athlete completes circular track (diameter 200 m) in 40 s. Find distance and displacement at 2 min 20 s.
Step 1: Core Concept

Distance is the total circular path length covered: Distance = (Number of rounds) × (Circumference 2πr). Displacement is the straight line from start to finish across the diameter.

Step 2: Given Data & SI Conversion

• Diameter of track (d) = 200 m ⇒ Radius (r) = d / 2 = 100 m.
• Circumference of circular track = 2πr = π × d = (22/7) × 200 = 4400 / 7 m ≈ 628.57 m.
• Time for 1 complete round = 40 s.
• Total time (t) = 2 min 20 s = (2 × 60) + 20 = 120 + 20 = 140 seconds.

Step 3: Calculating Rounds Completed

Number of rounds completed = Total time ÷ Time per round = 140 s ÷ 40 s = 3.5 rounds.
• In 3 complete rounds, the athlete returns to the initial point A. Displacement from 3 rounds = 0.
• In the remaining 0.5 (half) round, the athlete reaches the diametrically opposite point B on the circular track!

Step 4: Calculations

1. Distance Covered:
Distance = 3.5 × Circumference = 3.5 × (4400 / 7) = (7 / 2) × (4400 / 7) = 4400 / 2 = 2,200 metres.

2. Magnitude of Displacement:
Since the athlete is at the diametrically opposite point B after 3.5 rounds, the shortest distance from starting point A to point B is the straight line diameter:
Displacement = Diameter = 200 metres.

Step 5: Final Statement

At the end of 2 minutes 20 seconds, the athlete covers a distance of 2,200 metres (2.2 km), and has a displacement of 200 metres.

Exercise Q2 (Joseph Jogging A to B to C): Straight road 300 m in 2 min 30 s, then turns back 100 m to C in 1 min. Find average speed & velocity for (a) A to B, (b) A to C.
Step 1: Core Concept

Average Speed = Total Distance ÷ Total Time. Average Velocity = Net Displacement ÷ Total Time.

Step 2: Given Data & Time Conversions

• B to C (reverse direction): Distance = 100 m, Time t₂ = 1 min = 60 s.
• Total time from A to C: t_total = 150 s + 60 s = 210 s.
• Total distance from A to C: s_total = 300 m + 100 m = 400 m.
• Net displacement from A to C: s⃗ = 300 m - 100 m = 200 m (directed from A to C).

Step 3: Part (a) - From A to B

• Average Speed = Distance ÷ Time = 300 m ÷ 150 s = 2.0 m/s.
• Average Velocity = Displacement ÷ Time = 300 m ÷ 150 s = 2.0 m/s (directed along AB).
(Notice: In straight unidirectional motion, Average Speed = Average Velocity!)

Step 4: Part (b) - From A to C

• Average Speed = Total Distance ÷ Total Time = 400 m ÷ 210 s = 1.90 m/s.
• Average Velocity = Net Displacement ÷ Total Time = 200 m ÷ 210 s = 0.952 m/s (directed from A towards C).

Step 5: Final Statement

(a) From A to B: Average speed = 2.0 m/s, Average velocity = 2.0 m/s.
(b) From A to C: Average speed = 1.90 m/s, Average velocity = 0.95 m/s.

Step 1: Core Concept & Exam Hall Trap Warning

DANGER TRAP: Never do (20 + 30) ÷ 2 = 25 km/h! This is completely WRONG because Abdul spends different amounts of time travelling at 20 km/h and 30 km/h. Average speed MUST be calculated as Total Distance ÷ Total Time.

Let the one-way distance between home and school be x km.

• Speed for return trip (v₂) = 30 km/h.

Average Speed = Total Distance ÷ Total Time

The average speed for Abdul's entire trip is 24 km/h.

Exercise Q9 (Physical Possibility Scenarios): Are these 3 motion situations possible? Give clear real-world examples.
Step 1: Scenario (a) - Constant acceleration with zero velocity

YES, perfectly possible!
Example: When a stone is thrown vertically upwards, at its highest peak point, the stone momentarily stops: its velocity v = 0. However, throughout this instant, Earth's gravity continues to act on it with an unchanging acceleration due to gravity a = -9.8 m/s² (constant non-zero acceleration)!

Step 2: Scenario (b) - Moving with acceleration but with uniform speed

YES, perfectly possible!
Example: Uniform Circular Motion. An artificial satellite orbiting Earth, or an athlete running on a circular track with constant speed. The magnitude of speed is strictly constant, but because the direction of motion is changing every millisecond, the velocity changes, producing constant inward centripetal acceleration!

Step 3: Scenario (c) - Acceleration in direction perpendicular to motion

YES, perfectly possible!
Example 1: Again, Uniform Circular Motion! The linear velocity is tangential to the circle, while the centripetal acceleration is directed radially inward towards the centre. The angle between tangent and radius is exactly 90° (perpendicular)!
Example 2: An airplane flying horizontally while gravity pulls vertically downwards.

Step 4: Vector Orthogonality Note

When acceleration is perpendicular to velocity (a ⊥ v), it changes only the direction of motion, leaving the speed completely unaltered.

Step 5: Final Summary

All three scenarios (a), (b), and (c) are completely physically possible and occur regularly in classical mechanics.

1 A car moves along a circular path of radius R with constant speed v. When it completes one full circle, what are its distance and displacement respectively?
Teacher's Trap & Pedagogical Breakdown

Correct Answer: (c) 2πR and 0

Trap Alert: Confused students often invert distance and displacement or select (a). Distance is the actual perimeter of the track = circumference = 2πR. Displacement is the straight line distance between initial and final position. Because the car returns to its starting point on completing one revolution, initial and final coordinates coincide, making displacement strictly 0!

2 The slope of a velocity-time (v-t) graph gives which physical quantity?
Teacher's Trap & Pedagogical Breakdown

Correct Answer: (b) Acceleration

Trap Alert: Students frequently mix up slope and area under the curve! In a v-t graph: Slope = Δv/Δt = Acceleration. The area under the curve represents Displacement. In an s-t graph, the slope represents Speed.

3 An object is thrown vertically upwards with velocity u. The maximum height h to which it will rise is:
Teacher's Trap & Pedagogical Breakdown

Correct Answer: (b) u² / (2g)

Trap Alert: (a) u/g is the time taken to reach the peak, not the height! Using the third equation of motion: 2as = v² - u². Here v = 0 at the peak, and a = -g. Thus: 2(-g)h = 0 - u² ⇒ -2gh = -u² ⇒ h = u² / (2g).

4 A body travelling with an initial speed of 36 km/h comes to rest in 5 seconds after applying brakes. Its deceleration is:
Teacher's Trap & Pedagogical Breakdown

Correct Answer: (d) 2.0 m/s²

Trap Alert: Students who forget to convert km/h to m/s do (0 - 36)/5 = -7.2, picking option (a)! The correct approach is: u = 36 km/h × (5/18) = 10 m/s. Then a = (v - u)/t = (0 - 10)/5 = -2.0 m/s². The word "deceleration" already implies the negative sign, so the deceleration magnitude is 2.0 m/s².

Teacher's Trap & Pedagogical Breakdown

Correct Answer: (a) Speed

5 Assertion & Reasoning Questions

Question 6 (AR 1): Assertion: Displacement can be zero even when distance is not zero. Reason: Displacement is the shortest straight-line distance between initial and final positions.

Correct Option: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Detailed Explanation: When a moving body returns to its starting point (e.g., after completing a round trip or circular lap), its initial and final position vectors are identical (x_final = x_initial). Because displacement is defined as the shortest vector from start to finish, the net displacement is zero. Meanwhile, the actual scalar path length (distance) travelled is positive and non-zero. Reason R correctly defines displacement and explains why Assertion A occurs.

Question 7 (AR 2): Assertion: Uniform circular motion is an accelerated motion. Reason: The speed of an object in uniform circular motion remains constant.

Correct Option: (b) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.

Detailed Explanation: Assertion A is true: Uniform circular motion is accelerated because the direction of the velocity vector changes continuously at every point. Reason R is also true: By definition of "uniform", the numerical value of speed remains constant. However, constant speed does NOT cause acceleration; rather, it is the continuous change in direction that causes acceleration. Thus, R does not explain A.

Question 8 (AR 3): Assertion: The slope of a distance-time graph of a moving body can never be negative. Reason: Distance travelled by a moving body can never decrease with time.

Correct Option: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Detailed Explanation: Slope of a distance-time graph = Δs / Δt = Speed. Because distance is a cumulative scalar quantity that either increases (for a moving body) or stays flat (for a body at rest), Δs is never negative as time Δt advances. Consequently, the slope is always ≥ 0 and can never slope downwards. Reason R is the exact foundational cause for Assertion A.

Question 9 (AR 4): Assertion: A body can have zero instantaneous velocity and still possess non-zero acceleration. Reason: When a stone is at the highest peak of its vertical upward flight, its velocity is zero while acceleration due to gravity is 9.8 m/s² downwards.

Correct Option: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Detailed Explanation: Velocity is the rate of change of position, whereas acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. At the apex of a vertical throw, the object momentarily halts (v = 0) as it reverses direction. However, Earth's gravitational pull does not switch off; it continues to accelerate the object downwards at a constant g = 9.8 m/s². Reason R provides the exact physical proof.

Question 10 (AR 5): Assertion: The three equations of motion (v = u + at, etc.) can be used for any moving object in any trajectory. Reason: Acceleration in circular motion is directed towards the centre.

Correct Option: (d) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) is true.

Detailed Explanation: Assertion A is completely false! The three kinematic equations are strictly valid ONLY when the object moves along a straight line with UNIFORM (constant) acceleration. They fail completely if acceleration varies with time or if motion is non-rectilinear. Reason R is a true scientific statement (centripetal acceleration is directed radially inward towards the centre).

Question 11 (2 Marks): Can a body have a constant speed and a varying velocity? Support your answer with a concrete example.

Answer:

  • Yes, a body can certainly possess a constant speed while its velocity varies continuously.
  • Reason: Speed is a scalar quantity (magnitude only), whereas velocity is a vector quantity having both magnitude and direction. If the direction of motion changes while the speed remains unchanged, the velocity changes.
  • Example: An athlete running around a circular track at a steady speed of 5 m/s. Her speed is constant (5 m/s), but her velocity vector is continuously altering its direction along the tangent to the track at every single millisecond.
Question 12 (2 Marks): Derive the relationship between average speed and magnitude of average velocity for straight-line motion. When are they equal?

Answer:

  • Definitions:
    Average Speed = Total Distance / Total Time
    |Average Velocity| = |Total Displacement| / Total Time
  • Relationship: Since straight line is the shortest distance between two points, |Displacement| ≤ Distance. Dividing both sides by total elapsed time:
    |Average Velocity| ≤ Average Speed
  • Condition for Equality: The magnitude of average velocity is strictly equal to average speed if and only if the body moves along a straight line in a fixed, single direction without reversing!

Question 14 (3 Marks): Explain what physical quantities are derived from the slope and area of a velocity-time graph. Show dimensional derivation.

Answer:

  1. Slope of v-t Graph represents Acceleration (a):

Question 18 (5 Marks): Comprehensive Train Journey Numerical: Train starts from rest, accelerates at 0.2 m/s² for 5 min, cruises for 20 min, brakes to rest in 2 min. Find: (a) max speed, (b) total distance, (c) average speed.
Step 1: Phase 1 - Acceleration (0 to 5 min = 300 s)

• Initial velocity u = 0, a₁ = 0.2 m/s², t₁ = 5 × 60 = 300 s.
• Max speed attained (v_max): v = u + a₁t₁ = 0 + (0.2 × 300) = 60 m/s (216 km/h).
• Distance s₁: s₁ = ut₁ + ½ a₁t₁² = 0 + ½ × 0.2 × (300)² = 0.1 × 90,000 = 9,000 metres (9 km).

Step 2: Phase 2 - Cruising at Constant Speed (20 min = 1200 s)

• Speed v = 60 m/s (constant, a₂ = 0), t₂ = 20 × 60 = 1200 s.
• Distance s₂: s₂ = v × t₂ = 60 m/s × 1200 s = 72,000 metres (72 km).

Step 3: Phase 3 - Retardation to Rest (2 min = 120 s)

• Initial speed u₃ = 60 m/s, final speed v₃ = 0, t₃ = 2 × 60 = 120 s.
• Retardation a₃ = (0 - 60) / 120 = -0.5 m/s².
• Distance s₃: s₃ = [(u₃ + v₃) / 2] × t₃ = [(60 + 0) / 2] × 120 = 30 × 120 = 3,600 metres (3.6 km).

Step 4: Calculating Total Journey Metrics

• Total Distance: s_total = s₁ + s₂ + s₃ = 9,000 + 72,000 + 3,600 = 84,600 metres (84.6 km).
• Total Time: t_total = 300 + 1200 + 120 = 1620 seconds (27 minutes).
• Average Speed: v_avg = s_total / t_total = 84,600 / 1620 ≈ 52.22 m/s (188 km/h).

Step 5: Final Statement

(a) Maximum speed attained = 60 m/s (216 km/h).
(b) Total distance travelled = 84.6 kilometres.
(c) Average speed for entire journey = 52.22 m/s (188 km/h).

Question 19 (5 Marks): (a) What is free fall? (b) Two Stones Crossing Problem: Stone 1 dropped from 100 m tower; Stone 2 thrown upward at 25 m/s. When and where will they meet?
Step 1: Part (a) - Concept of Free Fall (2 Marks)

When an object falls towards Earth under the influence of the gravitational force alone, with no other forces (like air drag) acting on it, its motion is called Free Fall. In vacuum, all bodies fall with the exact same acceleration due to gravity (g = 9.8 m/s²), independent of their mass, shape, or density.

Step 2: Part (b) - Setting Equations for Both Stones

Let the two stones meet after time t seconds at a height h above the ground.
• For Stone 1 (dropped from top of 100 m tower):
It falls a downward distance = (100 - h).
Initial velocity u₁ = 0, a = +g = +9.8 m/s².
Using s = ut + ½ gt²:
100 - h = 0 + ½ gt² ⇒ 100 - h = 4.9 t² ⇒ h = 100 - 4.9 t² --- (Equation 1).

• For Stone 2 (thrown vertically upward from ground):
Travels upward to height h.
Initial velocity u₂ = 25 m/s, a = -g = -9.8 m/s².
Using s = ut - ½ gt²:
h = 25 t - 4.9 t² --- (Equation 2).

Step 3: Solving for Time (t)

Equating Equation 1 and Equation 2:
100 - 4.9 t² = 25 t - 4.9 t²
Notice that the 4.9 t² terms cancel out completely!
100 = 25 t
t = 100 / 25 = 4 seconds!

Step 4: Solving for Position (h)

Substitute t = 4 s into Equation 2:
h = 25(4) - 4.9(4)² = 100 - 4.9(16) = 100 - 78.4 = 21.6 metres from the ground.
(Or from the top of the tower: 100 - 21.6 = 78.4 metres below the top).

Step 5: Final Statement

The two stones will cross each other after exactly 4.0 seconds, at a height of 21.6 metres above the ground.

Question 20 (5 Marks): (a) Define Uniform Circular Motion & derive speed formula. (b) A cyclist completes 3 rounds of circular track (radius 140 m) in 2 minutes. Find her speed.
Step 1: Part (a) - Definition & Derivation

• Definition: When a particle traverses a circular perimeter at constant speed, it is in Uniform Circular Motion.
• Speed Derivation: In one complete revolution, the distance covered equals the geometric circumference of the circle = 2πr. If time taken is t, then:

Speed (v) = Distance ÷ Time = (2 × π × r) / t

Step 2: Part (b) - Given Data

• Radius of circular track (r) = 140 m.
• Number of rounds (N) = 3.
• Total time (t) = 2 minutes = 2 × 60 s = 120 seconds.

Step 3: Calculating Total Distance

Circumference of 1 round = 2 × (22 / 7) × 140 m = 2 × 22 × 20 = 880 metres.
Total distance for 3 rounds = 3 × 880 m = 2,640 metres.

Step 4: Calculating Speed

Speed (v) = Total Distance ÷ Total Time = 2640 m ÷ 120 s = 22 m/s.
In km/h: 22 × (18 / 5) = 396 / 5 = 79.2 km/h.

Step 5: Final Statement

The cyclist's speed is 22 m/s (79.2 km/h).

Case Study 1: Automatic Train Protection (Kavach System) & Kinematics of Emergency Braking Indian Railways is deploying the indigenous 'Kavach' Automatic Train Protection (ATP) system. Consider a Vande Bharat Express travelling at a high cruising speed of 144 km/h (40 m/s) on a straight track. The locomotive driver suddenly detects an obstruction on the tracks 500 metres ahead. The electronic emergency braking system initiates after a sensor delay of 0.5 seconds, providing a uniform deceleration of 2.0 m/s² until the train halts.
Question 21 (Case 1 Sub-questions): Solve all 4 sub-questions based on the Kavach braking scenario.

Sub-question 1: What distance does the train travel during the 0.5-second system sensor delay?
During the sensor reaction delay (t = 0.5 s), brakes have not yet engaged, so the train moves with uniform speed:
s_reaction = v × t_reaction = 40 m/s × 0.5 s = 20 metres.

Sub-question 2: How long does it take for the brakes to bring the train to a complete standstill?
Using 1st equation: v = u + at ⇒ 0 = 40 + (-2.0) × t_braking
2.0 × t_braking = 40 ⇒ t_braking = 40 / 2.0 = 20 seconds.

Sub-question 3: What is the braking distance required by the train to stop after brakes engage?
Using 3rd equation: 2as = v² - u²
2 × (-2.0) × s_braking = 0² - (40)²
-4.0 × s_braking = -1600 ⇒ s_braking = 1600 / 4.0 = 400 metres.

Sub-question 4: Does the train avert the collision with the obstruction located 500 m ahead?
Total stopping distance = s_reaction + s_braking = 20 m + 400 m = 420 metres.
Since 420 m < 500 m, the train comes to a complete halt 80 metres before the obstacle. The collision is safely averted!

Case Study 2: ISRO Chandrayaan Lunar Orbit Kinematics During ISRO's Chandrayaan mission, the spacecraft entered a circular polar orbit around the Moon at an altitude of 100 km above the lunar surface. The radius of the Moon is approximately 1,740 km, so the orbital radius is R_orbit = 1,740 + 100 = 1,840 km. The spacecraft completes one circular revolution around the Moon in approximately 2 hours (120 minutes).
Question 22 (Case 2 Sub-questions): Solve all 4 sub-questions based on Chandrayaan's lunar circular trajectory.

Sub-question 1: Calculate the total distance covered by the spacecraft in one single orbit.
Circumference = 2 × π × R_orbit = 2 × (22 / 7) × 1840 km = 11,565.7 km (1.157 × 10⁷ m).

Sub-question 2: What is the orbital speed of the Chandrayaan spacecraft in km/s?
Time t = 2 hours = 7,200 seconds.
v = Distance ÷ Time = 11,565.7 km ÷ 7,200 s ≈ 1.606 km/s ≈ 1.61 km/s (5,783 km/h).

Sub-question 3: What is the displacement of the spacecraft after completing: (a) 1.5 orbits, (b) 2 complete orbits?

Because its direction of motion changes continuously along the circular orbit, its velocity vector continuously alters direction, giving rise to an inward centripetal acceleration produced by the Moon's gravitational attraction.

5 Common Student Blunders (Exam Hall Trap Alerts)

Trap 1: The Average Speed Arithmetic Mean Trap

The Mistake: When asked to find the average speed of a journey with two legs at speeds v₁ and v₂, students blindly calculate (v₁ + v₂) / 2. For example, in Abdul's school trip (20 km/h and 30 km/h), writing (20 + 30)/2 = 25 km/h results in 0 marks!

The Correct Rule: Average speed is ALWAYS defined as: Average Speed = Total Distance ÷ Total Time. Because less time is spent at 30 km/h than at 20 km/h, the true average is the harmonic mean = 24 km/h.

Trap 2: The Unit Inconsistency Blunder (km/h vs m/s)

The Mistake: Inserting speed in km/h directly into equations where time is in seconds or acceleration is in m/s² (e.g., calculating a = (0 - 54)/4 = -13.5 instead of converting 54 km/h to 15 m/s first).

The Correct Rule: ALWAYS convert km/h to SI units (m/s) before applying kinematic equations by multiplying by 5/18.

Trap 3: Forgetting that Displacement is ZERO in Closed Loops

The Mistake: An athlete runs 5 laps around a 400 m track. Students often report displacement as 5 × 400 = 2000 m.

The Correct Rule: Distance is 2000 m, but because the athlete finishes where she started, Displacement is exactly 0 m! Similarly, average velocity for the entire run is 0 m/s.

Trap 4: Forgetting the Negative Sign of 'g' in Upward Motion

The Mistake: Writing v² = u² + 2gh when a stone is thrown vertically upwards, leading to square roots of negative numbers or physically impossible velocities.

The Correct Rule: When a projectile rises, gravity pulls downwards opposing the upward velocity: acceleration a = -g (or -9.8 m/s²). At the highest point, final velocity v = 0.

Trap 5: The "Constant Speed Means Zero Acceleration" Myth

The Mistake: Assuming that because an object in uniform circular motion moves at constant speed, its acceleration must be zero.

The Correct Rule: Velocity is a vector. Even if the speed (magnitude) never changes, the continuous alteration in the direction of motion produces a constant inward centripetal acceleration! Uniform circular motion is ALWAYS an accelerated motion.

15 Fast-Track High-Yield Revision Points

  1. Relativity of Motion: An object is in motion if its position changes relative to a chosen reference point (Origin) over time. Motion and rest are relative terms.
  2. Distance vs Displacement: Distance is the total actual scalar path length (always ≥ 0). Displacement is the shortest straight-line vector between initial and final points.
  3. Fundamental Inequality: The magnitude of displacement is always less than or equal to distance: |s⃗| ≤ s. They are equal only in unidirectional straight-line motion.
  4. Speed: Scalar rate of distance coverage (v = s/t). SI unit is m/s (or m s⁻¹).
  5. Velocity: Vector rate of displacement (v⃗ = s⃗/t). Velocity changes if speed changes, direction changes, or both change.
  6. Average Speed: Total Distance ÷ Total Time. Never take the arithmetic mean unless time intervals are identical!
  7. Average Velocity: Total Displacement ÷ Total Time. For uniform acceleration along a straight line, it equals (u + v) / 2.
  8. Acceleration: Rate of change of velocity: a = (v - u) / t. SI unit is m/s² (or m s⁻²).
  9. Retardation: Negative acceleration occurring when a body slows down (opposing velocity).
  10. Slope of s-t Graph: Represents the Speed of the body. Steeper slope = higher speed. Horizontal line = body at rest.
  11. Slope of v-t Graph: Represents the Acceleration of the body. Horizontal line = constant velocity (a = 0).
  12. Area Under v-t Graph: Represents the Displacement (Distance) travelled by the object.
  13. The Three Equations of Motion: (1) v = u + at, (2) s = ut + ½ at², (3) 2as = v² - u². Valid ONLY for uniform acceleration along a straight line!
  14. Free Fall: Downward motion under gravity alone with uniform downward acceleration g ≈ 9.8 m/s², completely independent of the object's mass.
  15. Uniform Circular Motion: Motion along a circular path with constant speed (v = 2πr/t). It is an accelerated motion with centripetal acceleration pointing permanently towards the circle's centre.
