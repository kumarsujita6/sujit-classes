2.1 The Core Problem: How to Locate an Object in Space?

In our daily life, we constantly need to describe where things are. But have you ever wondered how many pieces of information are needed to describe a location? The answer depends entirely on the dimension of the space!

One-Dimensional (1D) Location vs. Two-Dimensional (2D) Location

1. In One Dimension (1D - Along a Line): Exactly ONE number is sufficient to describe the exact position of a point relative to a fixed starting point (the reference zero).

  • Kilometer Stones on a Highway: On National Highway 16, saying "Milestone 42 km from Bhubaneswar towards Cuttack" pinpoints the exact spot on that line.
  • Ruler or Scale: A mark at "7.5 cm" tells you the exact position along the edge of the ruler.
  • Thermometer: A mercury thread at "37°C" locates the temperature on a single graduated column.
  • Number Line: Any real number (such as +5 or -3) is represented by a unique single point on a 1D line.

2. In Two Dimensions (2D - On a Flat Surface or Plane): Exactly TWO independent numbers are strictly required to pinpoint a location. A single number is never enough!

Why is one number not enough in 2D? Imagine you are in a large empty classroom and the teacher says: "Find the box that is 3 meters away from the teacher's table." You would be confused! Why? Because there are infinitely many points that are 3 meters away—they form a complete circle of radius 3 meters around the table! To find the exact box, you need two pieces of information: for instance, "Walk 3 meters towards the east wall, and then 2 meters towards the blackboard."

Real-World Everyday Examples of 2D Coordinate Systems:

Classroom Seating

Desks are arranged in Rows (running horizontally from front to back) and Columns (running vertically from left to right). To find Priya, the teacher says: "Row 3, Column 4".

Chessboard Coordinates

A chessboard has 64 squares organized into 8 vertical Files (labeled 'a' through 'h') and 8 horizontal Ranks (labeled 1 to 8). The king's pawn opens on square e4.

Cinema Hall Ticket

Your movie ticket does not just say "Seat 18". It says Row G, Seat 18. The letter specifies the distance from the screen, and the number specifies the position along that row.

Digital Screen Pixels

A computer or mobile screen has millions of tiny picture elements called pixels. A Full HD screen has a grid of 1920 × 1080 pixels. Every single pixel has an (x, y) address.

2.2 Historical Roots & Bharatiya Ganita Heritage

Many textbooks claim that coordinate geometry was invented solely by René Descartes in 1637. However, the foundational concepts of orthogonal grid planning, perpendicular reference lines, astronomical coordinate meridians, zero, and negative numbers have profound civilisational roots in ancient Bharat:

1. Sindhu-Sarasvatī Civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE): Urban Grid Planning

More than 4,500 years ago, cities of the Sindhu-Sarasvatī Civilisation (such as Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi, and Dholavira) were planned with breathtaking mathematical precision.

  • Orthogonal Street Layout: Broad major boulevards were aligned strictly along the cardinal North–South and East–West axes, intersecting at perfect right angles (90°).
  • Regular Modular Spacing: Secondary streets and residential lanes were spaced at regular modular intervals (approximately 10 metres apart).
  • Early Practical Coordinate System: This rectangular grid enabled city administrators, emergency drain sweepers, and merchants to locate any warehouse or granary simply by counting units from the central city junction—the earliest known urban coordinate addressing framework in human history!
2. Baudhāyana Sulba Sūtras (c. 800 BCE): Sacred Geometry & Reference Axes

In the Vedic period, sage Baudhāyana composed the Śulba Sūtras (treatises on cord geometry used to design sacred fire altars such as the falcon-shaped Śyena-citi).

  • Perpendicular Reference Lines: Baudhāyana established the central East–West line called the Prācī-Pratīcī axis using the shadow cast by a vertical gnomon (Śaṅku) at sunrise and sunset. He then constructed the perpendicular North–South line called the Udīcī-Dakṣiṇa axis using intersecting cord arcs (Rajju).
  • The Baudhāyana-Pythagoras Theorem: Centered on this perpendicular coordinate frame, Baudhāyana formulated the universal geometric theorem centuries before Pythagoras:
    दीर्घस्याक्ष्णया रज्जुः पार्श्वमानी तिर्यङ् मानी च ।
    यत् पृथग् भूते कुरुतस्तदुभयं करोति ॥
    "The rope stretched across the diagonal of a rectangle produces by itself both the areas which the length (pārśvamānī) and the breadth (tiryaṅmānī) produce separately."
    Modern Form: Diagonal² = Length² + Breadth² (d² = x² + y²)
    This very theorem is the exact mathematical parent of the modern Cartesian Distance Formula!
3. The Prime Meridian of Ujjayinī (Madhya-rekhā)

Just as the modern world uses Greenwich (London) as the 0° longitude Prime Meridian, ancient Indian astronomers (including Āryabhaṭa in 499 CE, Varāhamihira in 505 CE, Brahmagupta in 628 CE, and Bhāskarācārya II in 1150 CE) established the global reference meridian passing through Ujjayinī (modern Ujjain, Madhya Pradesh).

The Madhya-rekhā was chosen because Ujjayinī sits directly at the intersection of the Tropic of Cancer and a great longitudinal circle connecting Mount Meru (North Pole) through Kurukṣetra, Rohitaka (Rohtak), Ujjayinī, and Laṅkā on the equator. All terrestrial longitudes (deśāntara) and astronomical time corrections were computed east or west from this reference line!

4. Brahmagupta (628 CE): Unlocking the Four Quadrants

In his masterpiece Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE), the great mathematician Brahmagupta became the first person in human history to establish formal arithmetic rules for Zero (Śūnya) and Negative Numbers (Ṛṇa or Kṣaya):

  • Zero is not just "nothing"; it is a distinct number that separates positive assets (Dhana) from negative debts (Ṛṇa).
  • He gave rules such as: Negative × Negative = Positive, and Subtracting a negative number is equivalent to adding a positive number.
  • Why this is crucial for Coordinate Geometry: Without negative numbers, you can only plot points in one single positive corner (Quadrant I). Brahmagupta’s algebra of signed numbers is the mandatory foundation that enables the Cartesian plane to extend in all four directions into all four quadrants!
5. René Descartes (1637 CE) & The Cartesian Plane

In 1637 CE, French philosopher and mathematician René Descartes published an appendix titled La Géométrie (Geometry).

A popular story recounts that Descartes was resting in bed while unwell. As he watched a small fly crawl along the ceiling tiles, he wondered how to describe the fly’s exact position at any second. He realised that by measuring the perpendicular distance of the fly from the two adjacent walls of the room, he could uniquely fix its position with an ordered pair of numbers (x, y)!

Descartes called this system the Cartesian Coordinate System (named after his Latinized name, Cartesius). His historic breakthrough was uniting Algebra and Geometry into a single discipline: Analytical Geometry.

2.3 The Cartesian Coordinate Plane: Core Anatomy

To construct a Cartesian coordinate system, we take two real number lines and place them perpendicular to each other on a flat plane such that their zero marks coincide:

The Horizontal Axis: x-axis

• Denoted as line X'OX.
• The point O represents zero.
• The ray OX to the right of zero represents positive real numbers (+1, +2, +3, ...).
• The ray OX' to the left of zero represents negative real numbers (-1, -2, -3, ...).

The Vertical Axis: y-axis

• Denoted as line Y'OY.
• Placed perpendicular to the x-axis at the point O (∠XOY = 90°).
• The ray OY going upwards represents positive real numbers (+1, +2, +3, ...).
• The ray OY' going downwards represents negative real numbers (-1, -2, -3, ...).

The Origin: Point O

• The point where the two axes intersect is called the Origin (letter O).
• It is the starting reference benchmark for all measurements.
• Its coordinates are strictly (0, 0).
• It belongs simultaneously to both the x-axis and the y-axis.

The Coordinate Plane

• The infinite two-dimensional flat surface containing both coordinate axes is called the Coordinate Plane, or Cartesian Plane, or simply the xy-plane.
• The coordinate axes divide this entire plane into four disjoint regions called quadrants.

2.4 Coordinates of a Point & The Concept of Ordered Pairs

How do we name any point P lying in the coordinate plane? We drop perpendicular lines from point P onto both axes:

Definitions: Abscissa and Ordinate
  1. The x-coordinate (or Abscissa):
    • It is the perpendicular directed distance of point P from the vertical y-axis.
    • It is measured horizontally along or parallel to the x-axis.
    • It is positive if P lies to the right of the y-axis, negative if to the left, and zero if P lies on the y-axis.
  2. The y-coordinate (or Ordinate):
    • It is the perpendicular directed distance of point P from the horizontal x-axis.
    • It is measured vertically along or parallel to the y-axis.
    • It is positive if P lies above the x-axis, negative if below, and zero if P lies on the x-axis.
  3. The Ordered Pair (x, y):
    • The position of point P is uniquely written inside round parentheses as an ordered pair:
    P(x, y) = (Abscissa, Ordinate)
CRITICAL CONCEPT: Why "Order" Matters in an Ordered Pair!

The word "ordered" means the sequence of numbers is strictly fixed: the x-coordinate ALWAYS comes first, followed by a comma, and then the y-coordinate comes second!

Therefore, in general:
(x, y) ≠ (y, x)   unless   x = y

Concrete Example:
• Consider point A(2, 5): Its abscissa is 2 and ordinate is 5. To plot it, start at the origin O(0, 0), move 2 units right, and then 5 units up.
• Now consider point B(5, 2): Its abscissa is 5 and ordinate is 2. Start at origin, move 5 units right, and then 2 units up.
• Look at Figure 1.1: Points A(2, 5) and B(5, 2) are completely different locations! Confusing them is one of the most common exam blunders.

2.5 The Four Quadrants & Points on Coordinate Axes

The two intersecting axes X'OX and Y'OY slice the infinite Cartesian plane into four open regions called Quadrants. They are numbered anticlockwise (counter-clockwise) starting from the top-right:

Quadrant Name Region Sign of x (Abscissa) Sign of y (Ordinate) Coordinate Form Representative Example
Quadrant I (1st) Upper Right (XOY) Positive (x > 0) Positive (y > 0) (+, +) (3, 7), (1.5, 4), (6, 2)
Quadrant II (2nd) Upper Left (X'OY) Negative (x < 0) Positive (y > 0) (–, +) (-4, 5), (-2, 8), (-0.5, 3)
Quadrant III (3rd) Lower Left (X'OY') Negative (x < 0) Negative (y < 0) (–, –) (-5, -6), (-3, -1), (-7, -2.5)
Quadrant IV (4th) Lower Right (XOY') Positive (x > 0) Negative (y < 0) (+, –) (4, -3), (2, -7), (8, -1.2)
GOLDEN EXAM RULE: Points on the Axes Do NOT Belong to Any Quadrant!

The coordinate axes form the boundaries that separate the quadrants. Therefore:

  • Points on the x-axis: Have their vertical distance (ordinate) equal to zero. Every point on the x-axis is of the form (x, 0).
    Examples: (5, 0) lies on the positive x-axis; (-4, 0) lies on the negative x-axis. Neither lies in any quadrant!
  • Points on the y-axis: Have their horizontal distance (abscissa) equal to zero. Every point on the y-axis is of the form (0, y).
    Examples: (0, 6) lies on the positive y-axis; (0, -8) lies on the negative y-axis. Neither lies in any quadrant!
  • The Origin O(0, 0): Lies on both axes at their point of intersection. It does NOT belong to any quadrant!

2.6 Distance Between Points Along Lines Parallel to Axes

Before tackling the general distance formula, let us understand the two simplest cases where calculating distances requires only basic subtraction:

Case 1: Horizontal Line Segment (Parallel to x-axis)

If two points A(x₁, y) and B(x₂, y) have the exact same y-coordinate, the line segment AB joining them is completely horizontal (parallel to the x-axis).

The distance AB is simply the absolute difference between their x-coordinates:
Distance AB = |x₂ - x₁| = |x₁ - x₂|

Example: Distance between P(-3, 4) and Q(5, 4):
Here, both y-coordinates are 4.
Distance PQ = |5 - (-3)| = |5 + 3| = |8| = 8 units.

Case 2: Vertical Line Segment (Parallel to y-axis)

If two points C(x, y₁) and D(x, y₂) have the exact same x-coordinate, the line segment CD joining them is completely vertical (parallel to the y-axis).

The distance CD is simply the absolute difference between their y-coordinates:
Distance CD = |y₂ - y₁| = |y₁ - y₂|

Example: Distance between R(2, -5) and S(2, 7):
Here, both x-coordinates are 2.
Distance RS = |7 - (-5)| = |7 + 5| = |12| = 12 units.

2.7 Derivation of the Distance Formula (Via Baudhāyana-Pythagoras)

What if two points are neither horizontally nor vertically aligned? How do we find the straight-line Euclidean distance between any two arbitrary points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂)?

Step-by-Step Geometric Derivation
  1. Construction of a Right-Angled Triangle:
    Let P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂) be any two points in the Cartesian plane.
    From point P, draw a line segment PR parallel to the x-axis.
    From point Q, draw a line segment QR parallel to the y-axis.
    These two lines intersect at point R.
  2. Coordinates of the Intersection Point R:
    Because PR is horizontal, R has the same y-coordinate as P (y₁).
    Because QR is vertical, R has the same x-coordinate as Q (x₂).
    Thus, the coordinates of R are strictly R(x₂, y₁).
  3. Lengths of the Perpendicular Legs:
    • Base leg PR (horizontal segment): PR = |x₂ - x₁|
    • Altitude leg QR (vertical segment): QR = |y₂ - y₁|
    • Because the x-axis and y-axis are perpendicular, the horizontal segment PR and vertical segment QR meet at a 90° right angle: ∠PRQ = 90°.
  4. Applying the Baudhāyana-Pythagoras Theorem in △PQR:
    In the right-angled triangle △PQR, PQ is the hypotenuse:
    PQ² = PR² + QR²
    Substituting the lengths of PR and QR:
    PQ² = (|x₂ - x₁|)² + (|y₂ - y₁|)² = (x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²
  5. Taking the Non-Negative Square Root:
    Since physical distance is always positive (or zero):
    PQ = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²]
Special Case: Distance of Any Point P(x, y) from the Origin O(0, 0)

When one of the points is the Origin O(0, 0), we substitute x₁ = 0 and y₁ = 0 into the general distance formula:

OP = √[(x - 0)² + (y - 0)²] = √(x² + y²)

Quick Check: What is the distance of point P(3, -4) from the origin?
OP = √[3² + (-4)²] = √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5 units. (Notice that (-4)² = +16, so distance is always positive!).

2.8 Midpoint of a Line Segment

The Midpoint of a line segment is the unique point that divides the segment into two equal halves. In coordinate geometry, finding the midpoint is as simple as taking the arithmetic mean (average) of the coordinates:

The Midpoint Formula

If M(x, y) is the midpoint of the line segment joining points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂), then:

M(x, y) = ( (x₁ + x₂) / 2 , (y₁ + y₂) / 2 )

Worked Example: Find the midpoint of the segment joining A(-4, 6) and B(8, -2):
• x-coordinate of M = [(-4) + 8] / 2 = 4 / 2 = 2
• y-coordinate of M = [6 + (-2)] / 2 = 4 / 2 = 2
• Therefore, the midpoint is strictly M(2, 2).

2.9 Coordinate Transformations: Reflections Across Axes & Origin

Imagine the coordinate axes as flat mirrors. When a point or shape is reflected across an axis, its coordinates transform according to strict algebraic rules:

1. Reflection in x-axis

• The x-coordinate remains completely unchanged.
• The y-coordinate flips its algebraic sign.
(x, y) → (x, -y)
Example: (4, 3) reflects to (4, -3).

2. Reflection in y-axis

• The y-coordinate remains completely unchanged.
• The x-coordinate flips its algebraic sign.
(x, y) → (-x, y)
Example: (4, 3) reflects to (-4, 3).

3. Reflection in Origin O(0, 0)

• Equivalent to reflecting across both axes consecutively.
• Both x and y flip their algebraic signs.
(x, y) → (-x, -y)
Example: (4, 3) reflects to (-4, -3).

4. Reflection in Line y = x

• The coordinates swap their positions.
• Abscissa becomes ordinate, and ordinate becomes abscissa.
(x, y) → (y, x)
Example: (4, 3) reflects to (3, 4).

What Changes and What Stays the Same? (Geometric Invariants)

When a geometric polygon (such as a triangle △ABC) is reflected across any coordinate axis:

  • Invariants (Unchanged Properties):
    • The lengths of all sides remain exactly equal (distance formula values are preserved).
    • The perimeter of the shape remains identical.
    • The measures of all interior angles remain identical.
    • The enclosed surface area remains identical.
  • Variant (Changed Property):
    • The Orientation (chirality or handedness) is reversed (a clockwise vertex naming A → B → C becomes counter-clockwise A' → B' → C').

2.10 Real-World Applications: Blueprints & Wheelchair Accessibility

Coordinate geometry is not just abstract theory—it is the universal operating language used by architects, civil engineers, drone software pilots, and hospital planners:

Universal Barrier-Free Design: Wheelchair Doorway Clearance

According to the National Building Code of India (NBC) and the Harmonised Guidelines and Standards for Universal Accessibility in India (2021) framed under the RPwD Act:

  • Minimum Doorway Clearance: Any entrance door or internal doorway must provide a clear unobstructed opening width of at least 900 mm (90 cm or 0.90 metres) so that standard manual and motorized wheelchairs (width 65–75 cm) can pass safely with hand clearance.
  • Turning Radius Clearance: A wheelchair requires a circular turning diameter of at least 1500 mm (1.5 metres / radius 0.75 m) to execute a 360-degree rotation inside a room or bathroom.
  • Coordinate Modeling: On an architectural CAD drawing, the doorway opening lies along a wall modeled as a coordinate axis line. If the left door jamb is at (x₁, y) and the right door jamb is at (x₂, y), the clear accessible opening width is:
    Width = |x₂ - x₁|
    If |x₂ - x₁| < 0.90 m, the doorway fails accessibility compliance!

In-Text Activity 1: Classroom Seating Plan Coordinate Mapping

Problem Prompt: In a classroom of 30 students, desks are arranged in 5 columns (running from left to right along the teacher's view) and 6 rows (running from front to back). If Sasmita sits at the intersection of the 4th column and the 3rd row, how can her position be written as a coordinate pair? If Rohan sits at Column 2, Row 5, can their locations be confused if we write them without establishing an agreed rule?

Step 1: Given Information & Goal

• Number of vertical columns (left to right) = 5.
• Number of horizontal rows (front to back) = 6.
• Sasmita sits at Column 4, Row 3.
• Rohan sits at Column 2, Row 5.
• Goal: Formulate coordinate ordered pairs and explain why an agreed convention (Column, Row) is necessary.

Step 2: Formula / Principle Applied

In any 2D coordinate system, we must establish a fixed reference origin (e.g., the front-left corner of the classroom) and agree on the sequence of an ordered pair: (Horizontal offset, Vertical offset) = (Column, Row).

Step 3: Step-by-Step Calculation

1. Choosing the front-left corner as Origin O(0, 0):
• Along the horizontal direction (x-axis): Columns 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.
• Along the vertical direction (y-axis): Rows 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
2. Sasmita is at Column 4 (x = 4) and Row 3 (y = 3). Her coordinate pair is strictly S(4, 3).
3. Rohan is at Column 2 (x = 2) and Row 5 (y = 5). His coordinate pair is strictly R(2, 5).
4. If no agreed rule exists, someone might write Sasmita as (3, 4) by putting Row first. But (3, 4) represents Column 3, Row 4—which is a completely different student's desk!

Step 4: Verification / Cross-Check

Check if (4, 3) = (3, 4): Since 4 ≠ 3, the ordered pair (4, 3) ≠ (3, 4). The rule that abscissa must always represent the horizontal column prevents ambiguity.

Step 5: Final Concluding Answer

Sasmita's position: (4, 3); Rohan's position: (2, 5). Without an established convention, coordinates cannot be uniquely identified, proving why Cartesian ordered pairs mandate the x-coordinate (horizontal) first.

Both y-coordinates are 0 (horizontal wall).

In-Text Activity 3: Think & Reflect — Coordinates on Coordinate Axes

Problem Prompt: (i) What is the x-coordinate of any point that lies on the y-axis? (ii) What is the y-coordinate of any point that lies on the x-axis? (iii) Does the Origin O(0, 0) belong to Quadrant I, Quadrant II, Quadrant III, or Quadrant IV? Justify mathematically.

Step 1: Given Information & Goal

• Concepts tested: Definition of coordinate axes, perpendicular distance from axes, and quadrant definitions.
• Goal: Formulate exact algebraic rules and explain quadrant membership.

Step 2: Formula / Principle Applied

• Abscissa = directed perpendicular distance from the y-axis.
• Ordinate = directed perpendicular distance from the x-axis.
• Quadrant definitions require strict inequalities: x > 0 or x < 0, and y > 0 or y < 0.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Calculation

1. Points on the y-axis: Since any point lying directly on the y-axis has a perpendicular distance of zero from the y-axis, its x-coordinate is identically zero. Every point on the y-axis has the form (0, y).
2. Points on the x-axis: Since any point lying directly on the x-axis has a perpendicular distance of zero from the x-axis, its y-coordinate is identically zero. Every point on the x-axis has the form (x, 0).
3. Origin O(0, 0): At the origin, x = 0 and y = 0. • Quadrant I requires x > 0 and y > 0. • Quadrant II requires x < 0 and y > 0. • Quadrant III requires x < 0 and y < 0. • Quadrant IV requires x > 0 and y < 0. Since 0 is neither positive nor negative (0 is not > 0 and not < 0), the Origin satisfies NONE of these conditions.

Step 4: Verification / Cross-Check

The coordinate axes are the boundaries dividing the plane; boundaries do not belong to the interior regions they separate.

Step 5: Final Concluding Answer

(i) The x-coordinate of any point on the y-axis is ALWAYS 0.
(ii) The y-coordinate of any point on the x-axis is ALWAYS 0.
(iii) The Origin O(0, 0) belongs to NO quadrant; it lies at the intersection of both axes.

In-Text Activity 4: Think & Reflect — Point Symmetry & Swapping Coordinates

Problem Prompt: Under what condition does the point Q(y, x) coincide with the point P(x, y)? What is the geometric interpretation when x ≠ y?

Step 1: Given Information & Goal

• Given two points P(x, y) and Q(y, x).
• Goal: Find the algebraic condition for coincidence and explain the geometric relationship when x ≠ y.

Step 2: Formula / Principle Applied

Two ordered pairs (a, b) and (c, d) are equal if and only if their corresponding components are equal: a = c and b = d.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Calculation

1. For P(x, y) and Q(y, x) to coincide: • Equating x-coordinates: x = y • Equating y-coordinates: y = x Thus, P and Q coincide if and only if x = y.
2. Geometric Meaning of x = y: These points lie on the straight line y = x (the 45° diagonal passing through the Origin in Quadrants I and III). Examples: (0, 0), (2, 2), (-4, -4).
3. Geometric Meaning when x ≠ y: Point Q(y, x) is the reflection of point P(x, y) across the line y = x. The segment PQ is perpendicular to the line y = x and is bisected by it.

Step 4: Verification / Cross-Check

Take P(1, 4). Then Q is (4, 1). Midpoint of PQ = ((1+4)/2, (4+1)/2) = (2.5, 2.5), which lies on the line y = x because 2.5 = 2.5. Distance from (2.5, 2.5) to P = √[(1 - 2.5)² + (4 - 2.5)²] = √[2.25 + 2.25] = √4.5. Distance to Q = √[(4 - 2.5)² + (1 - 2.5)²] = √4.5. Verified!

Step 5: Final Concluding Answer

P(x, y) and Q(y, x) coincide if and only if x = y (points on the line y = x). When x ≠ y, Q is the mirror reflection of P across the line y = x.

In-Text Activity 5: Think & Reflect — Reflection of a Triangle Across Axes

Problem Prompt: A triangle has vertices A(1, 2), B(4, 2), and C(1, 6). (i) Reflect △ABC across the x-axis to obtain △A'B'C'. (ii) Reflect △ABC across the y-axis to obtain △A''B''C''. (iii) Compare the lengths of the sides, interior angles, and areas of the original and reflected triangles. What changes, and what stays invariant?

Step 1: Given Information & Goal

• Original vertices: A(1, 2), B(4, 2), C(1, 6).
• Goal: Find coordinates of reflected vertices and prove invariance of distance, angle, and area under reflection.

Step 2: Formula / Principle Applied

• Reflection in x-axis: (x, y) → (x, -y).
• Reflection in y-axis: (x, y) → (-x, y).
• Distance formula: PQ = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²].
• Area of right-angled triangle = ½ × base × height.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Calculation

1. Original Triangle △ABC Dimensions:
• Side AB (horizontal, y = 2): AB = |4 - 1| = 3 units.
• Side AC (vertical, x = 1): AC = |6 - 2| = 4 units.
• Since AB is horizontal and AC is vertical, ∠A = 90°.
• Hypotenuse BC = √[3² + 4²] = √25 = 5 units.
• Area(△ABC) = ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 sq units.

2. Reflection in x-axis (△A'B'C'):
• A(1, 2) → A'(1, -2)
• B(4, 2) → B'(4, -2)
• C(1, 6) → C'(1, -6)
• Length A'B' = |4 - 1| = 3 units. Length A'C' = |-6 - (-2)| = 4 units. Length B'C' = √[(1 - 4)² + (-6 - (-2))²] = √[9 + 16] = 5 units.
• Area(△A'B'C') = ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 sq units.

3. Reflection in y-axis (△A''B''C''):
• A(1, 2) → A''(-1, 2)
• B(4, 2) → B''(-4, 2)
• C(1, 6) → C''(-1, 6)
• Length A''B'' = |-4 - (-1)| = 3 units. Length A''C'' = |6 - 2| = 4 units. Length B''C'' = 5 units.
• Area(△A''B''C'') = ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 sq units.

Step 4: Verification / Cross-Check

Notice that in △ABC, going from A → B → C is counter-clockwise. In △A'B'C', going from A' → B' → C' is clockwise. Thus, orientation flips, but all geometric metrics are conserved.

Step 5: Final Concluding Answer

Reflected in x-axis: A'(1, -2), B'(4, -2), C'(1, -6).
Reflected in y-axis: A''(-1, 2), B''(-4, 2), C''(-1, 6).
Invariants: Side lengths (3, 4, 5 units), angles (90°, 53.1°, 36.9°), and area (6 sq units) remain 100% identical. Only the orientation is reversed.

Since OA = OB = AB = 6 units, △OAB is strictly an Equilateral Triangle. [Award 2 Marks]

(ii) Coordinates of Centroid G (1.5 Marks):
• Centroid formula: G = ((x₁ + x₂ + x₃)/3, (y₁ + y₂ + y₃)/3).
• x_G = (0 + 6 + 3) / 3 = 9 / 3 = 3.
• y_G = (0 + 0 + 3√3) / 3 = 3√3 / 3 = √3.
• Therefore, G = (3, √3). [Award 1.5 Marks]

(iii) Equidistance of Centroid G from Vertices (1.5 Marks):
• Distance OG = √[(3 - 0)² + (√3 - 0)²] = √[9 + 3] = √12 = 2√3 units.
• Distance AG = √[(3 - 6)² + (√3 - 0)²] = √[(-3)² + 3] = √[9 + 3] = √12 = 2√3 units.
• Distance BG = √[(3 - 3)² + (√3 - 3√3)²] = √[0 + (-2√3)²] = √[4 × 3] = √12 = 2√3 units.
Since OG = AG = BG = 2√3 units, the centroid G is strictly equidistant from all three vertices (circumcenter coincides with centroid in an equilateral triangle). [Award 1.5 Marks]

Long Answer Question 4 (5 Marks): Civilisational Heritage & Modern Urban Planning

Question (5 Marks): Write a comprehensive analytical essay connecting the ancient heritage of coordinate thinking in Bharat with modern applications: (i) Explain the town planning principles of the Sindhu-Sarasvatī civilisation and how they foreshadowed Cartesian grids. (1.5 Marks) (ii) Describe the significance of the Ujjayinī Prime Meridian (Madhya-rekhā) in ancient Indian geography and astronomy. (1.5 Marks) (iii) Formulate two modern engineering applications (such as GPS satellite geodesy and hospital wheelchair barrier-free architecture) where coordinate systems are indispensable. (2 Marks)

Model Answer & Comprehensive Marking Rubric (5 Marks Total)

(i) Sindhu-Sarasvatī Town Planning (1.5 Marks):
Over 4,500 years ago, cities like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa implemented orthogonal grid systems where major thoroughfares ran strictly North–South and East–West, intersecting at 90° right angles. Secondary residential lanes were spaced at modular intervals of approximately 10 metres. This grid served as an ancient practical coordinate addressing matrix, enabling systematic location of warehouses, wells, and drainage junctions thousands of years before European formalization. [Award 1.5 Marks]

(ii) Significance of Ujjayinī Prime Meridian (1.5 Marks):
Ancient Indian astronomers (such as Āryabhaṭa, Varāhamihira, and Brahmagupta) selected Ujjayinī (modern Ujjain, 23.2°N) as the zero-degree reference longitude (Madhya-rekhā) because it was the junction of the Tropic of Cancer and a great geographical circle passing from Mount Meru to Laṅkā on the equator. All local times, solar eclipses, and planetary positions across Bharat were computed relative to this prime meridian, demonstrating an advanced 2D global terrestrial coordinate system. [Award 1.5 Marks]

(iii) Two Modern Engineering Applications (2 Marks):
• 1. GPS & Satellite Geodesy: Global Positioning Systems (and India’s NavIC satellite constellation) model Earth’s surface on a 3D Cartesian reference frame (WGS-84 coordinate system). Satellites calculate latitude, longitude, and altitude using trilateration, which is a 3D extension of the Cartesian distance formula.
• 2. Universal Barrier-Free Design: In architectural drafting, doorway widths and corridor circulation paths are modeled as coordinate segments. Applying the National Building Code rule |x₂ - x₁| ≥ 0.90 m ensures all doorways permit unobstructed wheelchair access, while coordinate circles with radius r ≥ 0.75 m guarantee mandatory turning radius clearances. [Award 2 Marks]

Case Study 1: Smart City Master Planning & Ancient Harappan Grid Heritage

Context & Background: The Bhubaneswar Smart City Development Corporation (BSCDCL) is designing a new sustainable green innovation township inspired by the ancient orthogonal grid planning of the Sindhu-Sarasvatī civilisation. The township master plan is plotted on a coordinate grid where 1 unit = 100 metres (0.1 km). The central administration building is located at the Origin O(0, 0). The locations of key public utility hubs are plotted as follows:
• Solar Energy Power Substation: S(–6, 8)
• Water Treatment Facility: W(6, 8)
• Emergency Fire & Disaster Response Station: F(–6, –2)
• Central Community Park: C(6, –2)

Sub-Question 1 (1 Mark): Quadrants & Geometry of Township Boundary

State the quadrant in which each facility lies, and identify the geometric shape formed by joining S → W → C → F in order.

Solution:
• S(-6, 8): x < 0, y > 0 → Quadrant II.
• W(6, 8): x > 0, y > 0 → Quadrant I.
• C(6, -2): x > 0, y < 0 → Quadrant IV.
• F(-6, -2): x < 0, y < 0 → Quadrant III.
• Side SW: y = 8, length = |6 - (-6)| = 12 units.
• Side WC: x = 6, length = |8 - (-2)| = 10 units.
• Side CF: y = -2, length = |-6 - 6| = 12 units.
• Side FS: x = -6, length = |8 - (-2)| = 10 units.
Since opposite sides are equal (12 units and 10 units) and adjacent sides are perpendicular (∠ = 90°), SWCF is a RECTANGLE.

Sub-Question 2 (1 Mark): Physical Pipeline Length in Kilometers

An underground primary water supply pipeline runs horizontally from Solar Substation S(-6, 8) to Water Facility W(6, 8). Calculate its physical length in kilometers.

Solution:
• Grid distance SW = |6 - (-6)| = 12 grid units.
• Scale: 1 unit = 100 metres = 0.1 km.
• Physical pipeline length = 12 × 100 m = 1200 metres = 1.20 km.

Sub-Question 3 (1 Mark): Central Communication Tower Location

A central 5G telecom tower is to be erected at the exact geometric midpoint of the diagonal joining Disaster Station F(-6, -2) and Water Facility W(6, 8). Find the coordinates of the tower T.

Solution:
• Midpoint formula: T = ((x_F + x_W)/2, (y_F + y_W)/2).
• x_T = [(-6) + 6] / 2 = 0 / 2 = 0.
• y_T = [(-2) + 8] / 2 = 6 / 2 = 3.
• Therefore, the tower coordinates are T(0, 3). Notice that T lies directly on the positive y-axis!

Sub-Question 4 (1 Mark): Perimeter and Area of Township Enclosure

Calculate the total perimeter (in km) and the total ground area (in square kilometers) enclosed by the rectangle SWCF.

Solution:
• Length = 1.2 km (1200 m); Breadth = 10 units × 0.1 km = 1.0 km (1000 m).
• Perimeter: P = 2 × (1.2 km + 1.0 km) = 2 × 2.2 km = 4.4 km.
• Enclosed Area: Area = 1.2 km × 1.0 km = 1.2 km² (1.2 square kilometers).

Case Study 2: Hospital Wheelchair Access & Autonomous Drone Delivery Grid

Context & Background: At the new Trauma & Emergency Wing of AIIMS, an architectural coordinate grid (where 1 unit = 1 metre) is used for accessibility compliance under the RPwD Act 2016 and for routing autonomous medicine delivery drones:
• The main corridor center-line runs along the x-axis (y = 0).
• ICU Entrance Door: Left jamb at A(2.10, 4.00), Right jamb at B(3.20, 4.00).
• Old Restroom Door: Left jamb at C(8.50, 4.00), Right jamb at D(9.25, 4.00).
• Pharmacy Dispatch Counter: P(–4, –3).
• Emergency Operating Theatre: E(8, 6).

Sub-Question 1 (1 Mark): Doorway Accessibility Audit

The National Building Code mandates that any door must provide a clear unobstructed opening width of at least 90 cm (0.90 m) for wheelchair transit. Determine which of the two doors complies with this standard.

Solution:
• ICU Entrance Door (AB): Width = |3.20 - 2.10| = 1.10 m (110 cm). Since 1.10 m > 0.90 m, the ICU door is COMPLIANT (Wheelchair Accessible ✓).
• Restroom Door (CD): Width = |9.25 - 8.50| = 0.75 m (75 cm). Since 0.75 m < 0.90 m, the Restroom door FAILS accessibility compliance (Barred ✗).

Sub-Question 2 (1 Mark): Drone Direct Flight Path

An automated drone delivers emergency blood units from Pharmacy Dispatch P(–4, –3) directly to Operating Theatre E(8, 6). Calculate the straight-line distance flown by the drone.

Solution:
• Distance PE = √[(8 - (-4))² + (6 - (-3))²] = √[(8 + 4)² + (6 + 3)²] = √[12² + 9²] = √[144 + 81] = √225 = 15 metres.

Sub-Question 3 (1 Mark): Drone Middle Sensor Beacon

A safety infrared beacon is positioned at the exact midpoint of the drone's flight path PE. Find the coordinates of this beacon B.

Solution:
• x_B = [(-4) + 8] / 2 = 4 / 2 = 2.
• y_B = [(-3) + 6] / 2 = 3 / 2 = 1.5.
• Beacon coordinates = B(2, 1.5) [Quadrant I].

Sub-Question 4 (1 Mark): Wheelchair 360° Turning Clearance

A circular wheelchair turning area requires a minimum diameter of 1.5 m (radius 0.75 m). If a turning circle is centered at (5, 2), does the circle cross into the negative x or negative y region?

Solution:
• Center: (5, 2); Radius r = 0.75 m.
• Minimum x-reach: 5 - 0.75 = 4.25 m > 0.
• Minimum y-reach: 2 - 0.75 = 1.25 m > 0.
• The turning circle stays completely inside Quadrant I (well clear of any negative coordinate boundaries).

PYQ 1: Distance of P(–6, 8) from Origin and from Horizontal Line y = 3

Question (CBSE 2023 / BSE Odisha Model): Find the distance of the point P(–6, 8) from: (i) the Origin O(0, 0), and (ii) the horizontal line y = 3.

Model Answer

1. Distance from Origin:
OP = √[(-6)² + 8²] = √[36 + 64] = √100 = 10 units.

2. Distance from line y = 3:
Line y = 3 is a horizontal line where all points have ordinate 3.
The perpendicular from P(-6, 8) hits line y = 3 at foot N(-6, 3).
Distance PN = |8 - 3| = 5 units.

PYQ 2: Finding Unknown Abscissa Given Distance = 10

Question (CBSE 2022 / Most Probable Exam Question): If the straight-line distance between the points A(x, 2) and B(3, –6) is exactly 10 units, find all possible real values of x.

Model Answer

1. Given AB = 10 ⇒ AB² = 100.
2. By distance formula: (3 - x)² + (-6 - 2)² = 100.
3. (3 - x)² + (-8)² = 100 ⇒ (3 - x)² + 64 = 100.
4. (3 - x)² = 100 - 64 = 36.
5. Taking square roots on both sides:
3 - x = ±6.
• Case 1: 3 - x = 6 ⇒ x = 3 - 6 = -3.
• Case 2: 3 - x = -6 ⇒ x = 3 - (-6) = 3 + 6 = 9.
6. Final Answer: x = –3 or x = 9. (Both points A₁(-3, 2) and A₂(9, 2) are exactly 10 units from B(3, -6)).

PYQ 3: Finding Endpoint of Circle Diameter

Question (CBSE 2021): The center of a circle is C(2, –3) and one endpoint of a diameter AB is A(5, 1). Find the coordinates of the other endpoint B.

Model Answer

1. The center of a circle C is the midpoint of its diameter AB.
2. Let the coordinates of B be (x, y).
3. Midpoint formula for x:
(5 + x) / 2 = 2 ⇒ 5 + x = 4 ⇒ x = 4 - 5 = -1.
4. Midpoint formula for y:
(1 + y) / 2 = -3 ⇒ 1 + y = -6 ⇒ y = -6 - 1 = -7.
5. Final Answer: The coordinates of endpoint B are (–1, –7) [Quadrant III].

PYQ 4: Collinearity & Division Ratio of A(2, 3), B(–1, –2), C(5, 8)

Question (CBSE 2020 / State Board Standard): Prove using distance formulas that the points A(2, 3), B(–1, –2), and C(5, 8) are collinear. Which point lies between the other two?

Model Answer

1. Distance AB = √[(-1 - 2)² + (-2 - 3)²] = √[(-3)² + (-5)²] = √[9 + 25] = √34 units.
2. Distance AC = √[(5 - 2)² + (8 - 3)²] = √[3² + 5²] = √[9 + 25] = √34 units.
3. Distance BC = √[(5 - (-1))² + (8 - (-2))²] = √[6² + 10²] = √[36 + 100] = √136 = √(4 × 34) = 2√34 units.
4. Notice that: AB + AC = √34 + √34 = 2√34 = BC.
5. Since AB + AC = BC, the points are strictly COLLINEAR, and point A lies directly between B and C (A is the midpoint of BC!).

PYQ 5: Finding 4th Vertex of Parallelogram Using Diagonals

Question (CBSE 2024 / High-Yield Probable): Three consecutive vertices of a parallelogram ABCD are A(–2, 3), B(6, 7), and C(8, 3). Find the coordinates of the fourth vertex D.

Model Answer

1. In any parallelogram, the diagonals bisect each other, meaning diagonal AC and diagonal BD have the exact same midpoint!
2. Calculate midpoint of diagonal AC:
M = ( (-2 + 8)/2 , (3 + 3)/2 ) = ( 6/2 , 6/2 ) = (3, 3).
3. Let vertex D have coordinates (x, y).
Then the midpoint of BD must also equal (3, 3):
• (6 + x) / 2 = 3 ⇒ 6 + x = 6 ⇒ x = 0.
• (7 + y) / 2 = 3 ⇒ 7 + y = 6 ⇒ y = 6 - 7 = -1.
4. Final Answer: The fourth vertex is D(0, –1), which lies on the negative y-axis.

5 Common Exam Hall Student Blunders

Avoid these 5 classic traps that cost students valuable marks in annual CBSE and BSE board examinations:

Blunder 1: The Abscissa-Ordinate Swapping Trap

What students do wrong: When asked to plot point P(3, 5), students move 3 units vertically up along the y-axis and 5 units horizontally to the right, mistakenly plotting (5, 3).
The correct rule: In any ordered pair (x, y), the first number is ALWAYS the horizontal displacement (Abscissa) along the x-axis, and the second number is ALWAYS the vertical displacement (Ordinate) along the y-axis. Remember the alphabetical memory hack: x comes before y in the alphabet, just as Abscissa comes before Ordinate (A before O)!

Blunder 2: The "Points on Axis Have a Quadrant" Trap

What students do wrong: When asked "Which quadrant does (4, 0) belong to?", students guess Quadrant I or Quadrant IV. When asked about (0, -7), they guess Quadrant III or IV.
The correct rule: The coordinate axes are the dividing boundaries that separate the quadrants. Points lying on the coordinate axes do NOT belong to any quadrant! State clearly: (4, 0) lies on the positive x-axis; (0, -7) lies on the negative y-axis; and (0, 0) is the Origin lying on both axes.

Blunder 3: The "Negative Distance" Sign Trap

What students do wrong: When asked: "Find the perpendicular distance of P(-8, 5) from the y-axis", students write "-8 units".
The correct rule: Distance is a scalar physical magnitude and can NEVER be negative! The abscissa is -8, but the distance from the y-axis is the absolute value |-8| = +8 units. Writing a negative distance loses full marks immediately!

Blunder 4: The Incomplete Reflection Trap

What students do wrong: When asked to reflect a point P(3, 4) in the horizontal x-axis, students mistakenly flip the sign of x, writing (-3, 4). Or when reflecting in the origin, they only flip one coordinate.
The correct rule:
• Reflection in x-axis: Keep x unchanged; flip y → (x, -y).
• Reflection in y-axis: Keep y unchanged; flip x → (-x, y).
• Reflection in Origin: Flip BOTH signs → (-x, -y).

Blunder 5: The Doorway Width Subtraction Trap

What students do wrong: In real-world architectural problems, when finding the width of a door between jambs at A(1.2, 0) and B(2.1, 0), students subtract in reverse and write width = -0.9 m, or try to apply the full distance formula across diagonal walls without noticing the segment is parallel to an axis.
The correct rule: Always take the absolute difference: Width = |x₂ - x₁| = |2.1 - 1.2| = 0.90 m (90 cm). Ensure your final answer is accompanied by units (cm or m) and verified against the National Building Code (NBC) 90 cm threshold.

15 High-Yield Rapid Revision Points

  1. 1D vs 2D Space: One coordinate fixes a point on a line (1D), but exactly TWO independent coordinates are required on a plane (2D).
  2. Sindhu-Sarasvatī Heritage (c. 2600–1900 BCE): Ancient Bharat designed the world's first urban orthogonal street grids (spaced ~10 m apart) running strictly N-S and E-W at right angles.
  3. Baudhāyana Sulba Sūtras (c. 800 BCE): Sage Baudhāyana introduced the perpendicular Prācī-Pratīcī (E-W) and Udīcī-Dakṣiṇa (N-S) cord reference lines and formulated the Baudhāyana-Pythagoras theorem (d² = x² + y²).
  4. Ujjayinī Prime Meridian (Madhya-rekhā): Indian astronomers (Āryabhaṭa, Brahmagupta) used the Ujjayinī meridian (23.2°N) as the prime 0° longitude reference line for planetary tables and time reckoning.
  5. Brahmagupta's Zero & Negative Numbers (628 CE): Formalized arithmetic of zero (śūnya) and negative numbers (ṛṇa), providing the mandatory foundation to expand coordinate systems into all four quadrants.
  6. René Descartes (1637 CE): United Algebra and Geometry into Analytical Geometry by introducing the Cartesian coordinate plane.
  7. Coordinate Axes: Horizontal line is x-axis (equation: y = 0); vertical line is y-axis (equation: x = 0). They intersect at 90° at Origin O(0, 0).
  8. Ordered Pair: Point P is represented as P(x, y) where x is the abscissa and y is the ordinate. In general, (x, y) ≠ (y, x) unless x = y.
  9. Perpendicular Distances: Distance of P(x, y) from y-axis = |x|; Distance from x-axis = |y|. Distance is always non-negative.
  10. Four Quadrants: Numbered counter-clockwise: Q-I (+, +), Q-II (–, +), Q-III (–, –), Q-IV (+, –). Points on axes belong to NO quadrant.
  11. Distance Along Axes: Horizontal segment (same y): d = |x₂ - x₁|; Vertical segment (same x): d = |y₂ - y₁|.
  12. General Distance Formula: Straight-line distance between P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂) is PQ = √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²].
  13. Distance from Origin: For any point P(x, y), its distance from O(0, 0) is OP = √(x² + y²).
  14. Midpoint Formula: Midpoint M of segment joining P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂) is M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2).
  15. Accessibility Standards (NBC/RPwD): Architectural doorways must provide a minimum clear opening width of |x₂ - x₁| ≥ 0.90 m (90 cm) and a circular turning clearance of diameter ≥ 1.5 m.
