1. Ancient Philosophical Conceptions of Matter

Around 500 BCE, thinkers across ancient India and Greece began pondering a fundamental philosophical question: "If we continuously divide a piece of matter into smaller and smaller pieces, will we ever reach a stage where no further division is possible?"

Indian Philosophy (Maharishi Kanad & Pakudha Katyayama)

The ancient Indian sage Maharishi Kanad postulated that if we go on dividing matter (called Padartha), we shall get smaller and smaller particles. Ultimately, a limit will be reached beyond which the particles cannot be divided any further. He named these smallest indivisible particles Parmanu (ପରମାଣୁ).

Another Indian philosopher, Pakudha Katyayama, elaborated this doctrine and stated that these particles normally exist in a combined form, which gives us various forms and states of matter (solids, liquids, and gases).

Greek Philosophy (Democritus & Leucippus)

Around the same era in ancient Greece, philosophers Democritus and his teacher Leucippus suggested that if we go on dividing matter, a stage will come when the resulting particles cannot be cut or divided further.

Democritus called these indivisible particles Atomos (from which the modern English word atom is derived), meaning "uncuttable" or "indivisible".

Crucial Historical Distinction: Philosophy vs. Modern Science All these ancient ideas were purely philosophical speculations! Neither Kanad nor Democritus performed controlled quantitative laboratory experiments with precision balances. Until the end of the eighteenth century, no experimental techniques existed to verify whether atoms truly existed or how they combined chemically.

2. The Laws of Chemical Combination

By the end of the 18th century, scientists began distinguishing between elements and compounds and grew eager to find out how and why elements combine and what happens when they react. French chemist Antoine Laurent Lavoisier (widely honored as the Father of Modern Chemistry) established the foundations of chemical science by laying down two fundamental quantitative laws of chemical combination.

2.1 Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier, 1774)

Formal Definition: Law of Conservation of Mass The Law of Conservation of Mass states that mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

Mathematically: In any chemical transformation occurring inside a closed system:
Total Mass of Reactants = Total Mass of Products

Before Lavoisier, early scientists were frequently confused because when wood burned, it turned into a tiny pinch of lightweight ash, leading them to believe that mass had been "destroyed" or vanished into nothingness. Similarly, when a piece of iron rusted, it became noticeably heavier, leading them to believe that new mass was spontaneously "created".

Lavoisier solved this mystery through groundbreaking quantitative rigor: he conducted all chemical reactions in strictly sealed, closed containers. When wood burned in a sealed glass vessel, the combined weight of the ashes, smoke, carbon dioxide gas, and water vapour produced was precisely identical to the initial mass of the wood and the oxygen gas consumed!

2.2 Law of Definite Proportions (Proust, 1799)

Formal Definition: Law of Definite Proportions (Constant Proportions) The Law of Definite Proportions (also known as the Law of Constant Composition), stated by French chemist Joseph Louis Proust, establishes that:

"In a pure chemical substance, the elements are always present in definite proportions by mass, regardless of the source from which it was obtained or who synthesized it."

Proust noticed that no matter where a chemical compound came from on Earth, its internal mass composition was always identical:

Case Study 1: Pure Water (H₂O)

Whether water is collected from the Mahanadi River in Odisha, the Pacific Ocean, melted Himalayan glacier ice, generated as rain, or synthesized inside a high-tech chemistry laboratory by burning hydrogen in oxygen:

Mass Ratio: Hydrogen and oxygen are always present in the ratio of 1 : 8 by mass.

Proof: If 9 grams of pure water are decomposed by electrolysis, exactly 1 gram of hydrogen gas and 8 grams of oxygen gas are liberated:
9 g H₂O → 1 g H₂ + 8 g O₂   (1 : 8 ratio)
Similarly, if 18 g of water are decomposed, exactly 2 g of hydrogen and 16 g of oxygen are produced (2 : 16 = 1 : 8).

Case Study 2: Ammonia Gas (NH₃)

In ammonia gas, nitrogen and hydrogen are always present in the fixed mass ratio of 14 : 3 by mass.

Proof: One atom of nitrogen has an atomic mass of 14 u, and three atoms of hydrogen contribute 3 × 1 = 3 u:
Mass Ratio = 14 : (3 × 1) = 14 : 3
Consequently, if 17 grams of pure ammonia decompose, exactly 14 grams of nitrogen and 3 grams of hydrogen are obtained, regardless of the industrial synthesis conditions.

Case Study 3: Carbon Dioxide Gas (CO₂)

Carbon dioxide can be formed in several distinct ways:

  • By burning pure carbon / charcoal in excess oxygen: C + O₂ → CO₂
  • By the thermal decomposition of limestone: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
  • By reacting baking soda or marble chips with dilute hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂
  • Through biological respiration exhaled by humans and animals.

In every single sample of pure CO₂, carbon and oxygen are combined in the constant mass ratio of 3 : 8:
Mass of C : Mass of O = 12 : (2 × 16) = 12 : 32 = 3 : 8

3. John Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

The problem confronting early nineteenth-century scientists was that although Lavoisier and Proust had established these experimental laws of chemical combination, no one could explain WHY elements behaved in this strange, orderly manner. Why did mass remain conserved? Why did elements combine in fixed whole-number proportions?

In 1808, an English schoolteacher and polymath named John Dalton provided the revolutionary breakthrough. Dalton picked up the ancient philosophical idea of "atoms" and gave it a precise, quantitative physical foundation. He proposed that the smallest unit of matter is the atom, and formulated his historic Dalton's Atomic Theory.

The Six Canonical Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory
  1. Postulate 1: All matter (whether an element, a compound, or a mixture) is composed of extremely tiny particles called atoms.
  2. Postulate 2: Atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  3. Postulate 3: Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties.
  4. Postulate 4: Atoms of different elements have different masses and different chemical properties.
  5. Postulate 5: Atoms combine in the ratio of small whole numbers to form chemical compounds.
  6. Postulate 6: The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound.

3.1 How Dalton's Postulates Explain the Quantitative Laws

Explaining Conservation of Mass

Postulate 2 explains the Law of Conservation of Mass:

Because atoms are indestructible and indivisible, a chemical reaction is simply a rearrangement or shuffling of preexisting atoms. No atoms disappear into nothingness, and no brand-new atoms are magically created. Since the number and types of atoms before and after the reaction remain identical, the total mass of the reactants must strictly equal the total mass of the products.

Explaining Constant Proportions

Postulate 6 (supported by Postulate 3 and 5) explains the Law of Definite Proportions:

Since every atom of a given element has a fixed, characteristic mass, and since atoms always combine in fixed small whole-number ratios (such as 2 atoms of H to 1 atom of O), the proportion by mass between the constituent elements in that compound must forever remain strictly constant.

3.2 Modern Critique: Modifications and Drawbacks of Dalton's Theory

While Dalton's theory was a monumental milestone that launched modern chemistry, modern 20th-century physics and subatomic discoveries required several key modifications:

Dalton's Original Postulate (1808) Modern Scientific Discovery Corrected Modern Scientific Reality
Atoms are indivisible: The atom is the ultimate indestructible particle of matter. Discovery of subatomic particles (electrons by Thomson, protons by Goldstein/Rutherford, neutrons by Chadwick). Modified Atoms are divisible! They are composed of subatomic particles (protons, neutrons, and electrons) and can undergo nuclear reactions (fission and fusion).
Atoms of the same element have identical masses: All atoms of carbon, chlorine, etc., weigh exactly the same. Discovery of Isotopes (Frederick Soddy, 1913). Modified Atoms of the same element can have slightly different masses! For example, Chlorine has two isotopes: ³⁵Cl (mass 35 u) and ³⁷Cl (mass 37 u); Carbon has ¹²C and ¹⁴C.
Atoms of different elements have different masses: No two different elements can share the same mass. Discovery of Isobars. Modified Atoms of different elements can have the identical mass number! For example, Argon (₁₈Ar⁴⁰) and Calcium (₂₀Ca⁴⁰) both have a mass of 40 u.
Atoms combine in simple small whole numbers: Only small ratios like 1:1, 1:2, 2:3 exist. Discovery of complex organic macromolecules and polymers. Refined While simple inorganic molecules follow small ratios, complex biochemical polymers (e.g., Cane sugar C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, cellulose, DNA, proteins) combine in large, intricate whole-number ratios.

4. What is an Atom? The Physical Scale of Matter

Have you ever watched a mason building a high brick wall? The bricks make up the wall, walls make up a room, and multiple rooms make up a giant mansion. In the exact same way, atoms are the universal building blocks of all matter around us.

How big is an atom? Atoms are unimaginably tiny! They are so small that millions of atoms stacked closely on top of each other would barely create a layer as thick as a single thin sheet of paper.

The Nanometre Scale (nm) Atomic radii are measured in nanometres (nm):
1 m = 10⁹ nm   ⇔   1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m
The smallest atom in the universe is the Hydrogen atom, whose atomic radius is approximately 10⁻¹⁰ m (or 0.1 nm).

4.2 Modern IUPAC Symbols of Elements

In the early days of chemistry, Dalton was the first scientist to use graphic symbols for elements in a specific sense. When he wrote a circle with a dot in the centre, he meant exactly one atom of hydrogen; a plain circle meant oxygen; a circle with a cross meant sulphur. However, drawing intricate geometric symbols became impossible as dozens of new elements were discovered!

Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius suggested a far simpler, universal system: represent elements using one or two letters derived from the element's name.

The IUPAC Capitalization Rules for Chemical Symbols Today, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) approves names and symbols:

• Rule 1: The first letter of an element's symbol is ALWAYS written as a capital (uppercase) letter.
• Rule 2: If the symbol has a second letter, it is ALWAYS written as a small (lowercase) letter.

Classic Exam Trap Warning:
Writing Co represents the element Cobalt (one atom).
Writing CO represents the compound Carbon Monoxide (one atom of Carbon bonded to one atom of Oxygen)!
Similarly, Aluminium is written as Al (never AL), Chlorine is Cl (never CL), and Magnesium is Mg (never MG).

Many element symbols are simply the first letter of their English name (e.g., Hydrogen = H, Carbon = C, Nitrogen = N, Oxygen = O, Fluorine = F, Phosphorus = P, Sulphur = S). When multiple elements start with the same letter, the first letter is combined with a second prominent letter (e.g., Calcium = Ca, Chlorine = Cl, Chromium = Cr, Cobalt = Co).

5. Atomic Mass & The Carbon-12 Reference Standard

The most remarkable concept in Dalton's atomic theory was that each element has a characteristic atomic mass. However, since individual atoms are inconceivably tiny, determining the absolute mass of a single atom by direct weighing on a mechanical scale is physically impossible! For instance, one atom of hydrogen weighs approximately 0.00000000000000000000000166 grams (1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g). Handling such awkward numbers in daily calculations would cause endless calculation errors.

Chemists realized that what matters most in chemical reactions is not the absolute microscopic weight in grams, but the relative mass of one atom compared to another.

5.1 The Fruit Seller Analogy (NCERT Visual Concept)

Imagine a fruit vendor in an Indian bazaar who has no standard metallic kilogram weights. He takes a large, fresh watermelon and decides: "This whole watermelon has a mass equal to 12 watermelon units!" He carefully slices the watermelon into 12 equal pieces. Each single piece now represents 1 watermelon mass unit. When a customer wants apples, he places apples in one pan and balances them against 2 watermelon slices; the apples weigh 2 watermelon mass units. If a pumpkin balances against 24 slices, it weighs 24 watermelon mass units!

5.2 The Carbon-12 Universal Standard (Adopted 1961)

In the nineteenth century, chemists originally experimented with Hydrogen (the lightest atom, set to 1) and later Oxygen (1/16th mass of natural oxygen, because oxygen reacts with almost all elements). However, the existence of isotopes caused small inconsistencies. Finally, in 1961, IUPAC universally agreed on the Carbon-12 isotope (¹²C) as the international reference standard for measuring all atomic masses.

Formal Definition: Unified Atomic Mass Unit (u) One atomic mass unit (symbol u, unified mass; formerly amu) is defined as a mass unit strictly equal to exactly 1/12th (one-twelfth) of the mass of one atom of carbon-12 isotope.

1 u = (Mass of one ¹²C atom) / 12 ≈ 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁴ g = 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg

Relative Atomic Mass of an Element: The average relative mass of an atom of the element as compared to 1/12th of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.

6. How Do Atoms Exist? Molecules and Atomicity

Atoms of most elements are not chemically stable enough to exist independently in nature with unbonded single atoms. Except for the noble gases (Helium, Neon, Argon, Krypton, Xenon), which have completely filled outer electron shells, atoms spontaneously link together with other atoms to achieve a stable electronic configuration.

Atoms generally exist in two fundamental forms: Molecules or Ions.

Formal Definition: What is a Molecule? A molecule is the smallest particle of an element or a compound that is capable of an independent existence under ordinary conditions and exhibits all the chemical properties of that substance.

A molecule consists of two or more atoms of the same or different elements that are chemically held together by attractive electrical forces called chemical bonds (such as covalent bonds).

6.1 Molecules of Elements & Concept of Atomicity

The molecules of an element are constituted by the same type of atoms. The number of atoms constituting a molecule is defined as its Atomicity (ପାରମାଣବିକତା).

7. What is an Ion? Cations, Anions, and Polyatomic Ions

Compounds composed of metals and non-metals contain charged species known as Ions (ଆୟନ). An ion is an electrically charged atom or group of covalently bonded atoms.

Cation (Positively Charged Ion)

A cation is formed when a neutral atom loses one or more valence electrons. Because protons outnumber electrons, the net electrical charge is positive.

Metals typically form cations:
• Na → Na⁺ + 1e⁻ (Sodium ion)
• Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ (Magnesium ion)
• Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ (Aluminium ion)

Anion (Negatively Charged Ion)

An anion is formed when a neutral atom gains one or more electrons into its valence shell. Because electrons outnumber protons, the net electrical charge is negative.

Non-metals typically form anions:
• Cl + 1e⁻ → Cl⁻ (Chloride ion)
• O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ (Oxide ion)
• N + 3e⁻ → N³⁻ (Nitride ion)

7.1 Polyatomic Ions

Formal Definition: Polyatomic Ion A polyatomic ion (ବହୁପାରମାଣବିକ ଆୟନ) is a cluster of tightly bonded atoms that behaves as a single collective unit and carries a definite net electric charge.

Examples include the Ammonium ion (NH₄⁺), Hydroxide ion (OH⁻), Nitrate ion (NO₃⁻), Carbonate ion (CO₃²⁻), Sulphate ion (SO₄²⁻), and Phosphate ion (PO₄³⁻).

8. Writing Chemical Formulae: The Criss-Cross Method

The chemical formula of a compound is a symbolic representation of its chemical composition. It indicates the exact elements present and the fixed number of atoms of each element in the simplest repeating unit.

The Three Golden Rules for Writing Chemical Formulae
  1. Rule 1 (Electrical Neutrality): The positive and negative valencies or charges of the combining ions must balance each other so that the net electrical charge of the finished compound is strictly zero.
  2. Rule 2 (Metal First): When a compound consists of a metal and a non-metal, the symbol and name of the metal are written first on the left, followed by the non-metal on the right. (e.g., Calcium oxide: CaO, Sodium chloride: NaCl, Iron sulphide: FeS).
  3. Rule 3 (The Bracket / Parenthesis Rule for Polyatomic Ions): If a formula contains two or more identical polyatomic ions, the entire formula of the polyatomic ion must be enclosed inside brackets (parentheses) before writing the subscript number outside (e.g., Ca(OH)₂, (NH₄)₂SO₄). If only one polyatomic ion is present, no brackets are needed (e.g., NaOH, KNO₃).

9. Molecular Mass and Formula Unit Mass

Now that we can write accurate chemical formulae, we can calculate how much an entire molecule or ionic unit weighs by summing the atomic masses of all its constituent atoms.

Molecular Mass (for Covalent Molecules)

The molecular mass of a substance is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in a single molecule of that substance. It is expressed in unified mass units (u).

Used for discrete molecular substances like H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, HNO₃, and C₆H₁₂O₆.

Formula Unit Mass (for Ionic Compounds)

The formula unit mass of a substance is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms present in a formula unit of a compound.

Used for substances whose constituent particles are ions arranged in continuous 3D crystalline arrays (such as NaCl, CaCl₂, ZnO, Na₂O), where separate isolated molecules do not exist!

Molecular Mass of Nitric Acid (HNO₃)

Atomic masses: H = 1 u, N = 14 u, O = 16 u

10. The Grand Mole Concept: Bridging the Micro and Macro Worlds

In a chemistry laboratory, chemists mix substances in beakers, test tubes, and conical flasks. But in a chemical equation like:

2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O Molecular Reaction Ratio: 2 molecules + 1 molecule → 2 molecules

This equation indicates that 2 molecules of hydrogen react with 1 molecule of oxygen to form 2 molecules of water. Notice that chemical reactions happen by counting individual particles, not by weighing random grams! Yet, we cannot count individual atoms or molecules directly because they are far too tiny to see or pick with tweezers. How do we count particles simply by weighing them on a balance? We use The Mole Concept!

10.1 The Counting Analogy

In our daily shopping routines, we regularly use counting collective nouns:

  • 1 Pair = Exactly 2 items (shoes, socks)
  • 1 Dozen = Exactly 12 items (bananas, eggs)
  • 1 Gross = Exactly 144 items (12 dozen pens)
  • 1 Ream = Exactly 500 sheets of paper

Similarly, for chemists dealing with atoms and molecules, 1 Mole is the chemist's counting dozen—except it is an enormous number because atoms are so exceedingly tiny!

Formal Definition: What is 1 Mole? One mole of any species (atoms, molecules, ions, or electrons) is that quantity in number having a mass equal to its atomic or molecular mass in grams.

The number of particles (atoms, molecules, or ions) present in 1 mole of any substance is fixed, with an internationally established value of:
N_A = 6.02214076 × 10²³ particles / mol ≈ 6.022 × 10²³

This universal constant is named the Avogadro Constant (or Avogadro's Number, symbol N_A or N₀) in honor of the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro. The term mole was introduced around 1896 by Wilhelm Ostwald from the Latin word moles, meaning a "heap" or "massive pile".

10.2 Molar Mass (M) and the Magic Numerical Link

Here is the most beautiful mathematical discovery in chemistry: The mass of 1 mole of a substance in grams is NUMERICALLY EQUAL to its atomic or molecular mass in unified atomic mass units (u)!

Microscopic Scale: Unified Mass (u)

• Mass of 1 single atom of Hydrogen = 1 u

• Mass of 1 single atom of Carbon = 12 u

• Mass of 1 single atom of Oxygen = 16 u

• Mass of 1 single molecule of Water = 18 u

Macroscopic Scale: Molar Mass (g/mol)

• Mass of 1 mole (6.022 × 10²³) H atoms = 1 g

• Mass of 1 mole (6.022 × 10²³) C atoms = 12 g

• Mass of 1 mole (6.022 × 10²³) O atoms = 16 g

• Mass of 1 mole (6.022 × 10²³) H₂O molecules = 18 g

Figure 2.5: The Master Mole Wheel. To convert between mass and particles, always travel through the central gateway of moles (n).

10.3 The Core Algebraic Formulas of the Mole Concept

1. Number of Moles from Mass

n = m / M m = mass in g, M = molar mass in g/mol

Used whenever the problem gives you a mass in grams and asks for moles.

2. Number of Moles from Particles

n = N / N_A N = particles count, N_A = 6.022 × 10²³

Used whenever the problem provides the count of atoms, molecules, or ions.

3. Total Particles from Given Mass

N = (m / M) × N_A Combined Master Equation

Calculates the actual physical count of atoms or molecules in any weighed laboratory sample.

4. Mass of a Single Atom or Molecule

Mass of 1 particle = M / N_A Unit: grams

Converts macroscopic molar mass into absolute microscopic mass of one particle in grams.

5. Percentage Composition of an Element in a Compound

% of Element = [ (Total Mass of Element in 1 mol) / (Molar Mass of Compound) ] × 100% Elemental Purity

For example, in water (H₂O, M = 18 g/mol):
• % of Hydrogen = (2 × 1 / 18) × 100% = 2/18 × 100% = 11.11%
• % of Oxygen = (16 / 18) × 100% = 88.89%

NCERT Activity 9.1: Verification of Law of Conservation of Mass in a Closed System
Step 1: Scientific Objective & Principle

To experimentally verify the Law of Conservation of Mass formulated by Antoine Lavoisier by reacting solutions X and Y inside a sealed conical flask with an ignition tube, and verifying whether any mass change occurs after precipitation.

Step 2: Experimental Setup & Materials

• Conical flask (150 mL / 250 mL) fitted with a tight rubber cork.
• Small ignition tube tied with a thin cotton thread.
• Chemical Pair Option 1: Copper sulphate solution (X, 5% w/v) in the ignition tube + Sodium carbonate solution (Y, 5% w/v) in the flask.
• Chemical Pair Option 2: Barium chloride solution (X) + Sodium sulphate solution (Y).
• High-precision digital weighing balance sensitive to 0.01 g.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Prepare 5% aqueous solutions of copper sulphate and sodium carbonate separately.
2. Pour 10 mL of sodium carbonate solution (Y) into the bottom of the clean conical flask.
3. Fill the small ignition tube with 4 mL of copper sulphate solution (X).
4. Carefully suspend the ignition tube inside the conical flask using a thread held firmly by the rubber cork at the mouth, ensuring that the two solutions do not mix at this stage.
5. Place the entire sealed apparatus on the digital balance and record the initial mass (m₁ = 152.40 g).
6. Now gently tilt and swirl the flask so that the blue copper sulphate solution in the ignition tube spills out and mixes thoroughly with the clear sodium carbonate solution in the flask.
7. Observe the immediate formation of a pale blue-green precipitate of copper carbonate.
8. Place the flask back on the balance and record the final mass (m₂ = 152.40 g).

Step 4: Observations & Chemical Equations

Visual Observation: An immediate double displacement reaction occurs. A blue-green precipitate of insoluble copper carbonate (CuCO₃) settles at the bottom of the flask, leaving aqueous sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄) in solution:
CuSO₄(aq) + Na₂CO₃(aq) → CuCO₃(s)↓ + Na₂SO₄(aq)
For Barium Chloride + Sodium Sulphate:
BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ (dense white precipitate) + 2NaCl(aq)
Mass Comparison: Initial Mass (m₁) = 152.40 g; Final Mass (m₂) = 152.40 g.

Step 5: Final Conclusion & Teacher's Practical Tip

Conclusion: Since m₁ = m₂, there is zero change in mass during the chemical reaction. The Law of Conservation of Mass is rigorously validated.
Teacher's Lab Note: A rubber cork is absolutely mandatory! If any gas escapes from an unsealed flask, the mass appears to decrease, leading to an incorrect experimental conclusion.

NCERT Activity 9.3: Deriving Ratio of Atoms by Number from Ratio of Atoms by Mass
Step 1: Core Mathematical Principle

To determine the chemical formula of a compound from experimental mass ratio data by dividing the mass proportion of each element by its atomic mass and finding the simplest whole-number ratio.

Step 2: Analytical Formula

Atomic Ratio = (Mass Ratio of Element) ÷ (Atomic Mass of Element)

Step 3: Tabular Derivation for Three Compounds

1. Water (H₂O):
- Mass ratio: H : O = 1 : 8
- Atomic masses: H = 1 u, O = 16 u
- Atomic ratio H = 1 / 1 = 1.0; Atomic ratio O = 8 / 16 = 0.5
- Simplest whole number ratio: H : O = (1.0 / 0.5) : (0.5 / 0.5) = 2 : 1 → Formula is H₂O.

2. Ammonia (NH₃):
- Mass ratio: N : H = 14 : 3
- Atomic masses: N = 14 u, H = 1 u
- Atomic ratio N = 14 / 14 = 1.0; Atomic ratio H = 3 / 1 = 3.0
- Simplest whole number ratio: N : H = 1 : 3 → Formula is NH₃.

3. Carbon Dioxide (CO₂):
- Mass ratio: C : O = 3 : 8
- Atomic masses: C = 12 u, O = 16 u
- Atomic ratio C = 3 / 12 = 0.25; Atomic ratio O = 8 / 16 = 0.50
- Simplest whole number ratio: C : O = (0.25 / 0.25) : (0.50 / 0.25) = 1 : 2 → Formula is CO₂.

Step 4: Verification

The resulting whole-number ratios (2:1 for H₂O, 1:3 for NH₃, 1:2 for CO₂) precisely match the molecular formulas established by modern spectroscopic and crystallographic analysis.

Step 5: Pedagogical Takeaway

This simple mathematical operation bridges macroscopic laboratory balance masses with the discrete atomic counting world predicted by John Dalton.

In-Text Q1.1: In a reaction, 5.3 g of sodium carbonate reacted with 6 g of ethanoic acid. The products were 2.2 g of carbon dioxide, 0.9 g of water, and 8.2 g of sodium ethanoate. Show that these observations agree with the Law of Conservation of Mass.
Step 1: Governing Principle

According to the Law of Conservation of Mass, in any chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants must strictly equal the total mass of the products (Σ Mass_reactants = Σ Mass_products).

Step 2: Given Data

• Mass of Sodium Carbonate (Reactant 1) = 5.3 g
• Mass of Ethanoic Acid (Reactant 2) = 6.0 g
• Mass of Carbon Dioxide (Product 1) = 2.2 g
• Mass of Water (Product 2) = 0.9 g
• Mass of Sodium Ethanoate (Product 3) = 8.2 g

Step 3: Word Equation of the Reaction

Sodium Carbonate + Ethanoic Acid → Sodium Ethanoate + Carbon Dioxide + Water

Step 4: Step-by-Step Calculation

Total Mass of Reactants:
Mass_reactants = Mass(Sodium Carbonate) + Mass(Ethanoic Acid)
= 5.3 g + 6.0 g = 11.3 g

Total Mass of Products:
Mass_products = Mass(Sodium Ethanoate) + Mass(Carbon Dioxide) + Mass(Water)
= 8.2 g + 2.2 g + 0.9 g = 11.3 g

Step 5: Final Evaluation & Teacher's Tip

Since Total Mass of Reactants (11.3 g) = Total Mass of Products (11.3 g), there is zero change in mass. Hence, these experimental observations are in complete agreement with the Law of Conservation of Mass.

In-Text Q1.3: Which postulate of Dalton's atomic theory is the result of the law of conservation of mass?
Step 1: Target Question Identification

Identifying the specific postulate of John Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808) that provides the theoretical basis for Antoine Lavoisier's Law of Conservation of Mass.

Step 2: Postulate Statement

The postulate is Postulate 2:
"Atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction."

Step 3: Conceptual Explanation

Chemical reactions are merely the rearrangement, bonding, or separation of preexisting atoms. Because atoms can neither be created out of nothing nor destroyed into non-existence, the total number and mass of atoms must remain strictly constant before and after any reaction.

Step 4: Verification

This indivisibility and indestructibility directly guarantees that: Σ Mass_reactants = Σ Mass_products.

Step 5: Teacher's Exam Hack

Always quote the exact keywords: "indivisible particles" and "cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction" to secure full marks.

In-Text Q1.4: Which postulate of Dalton's atomic theory can explain the law of definite proportions?
Step 1: Target Question Identification

Identifying the postulate of Dalton's Atomic Theory that explains Joseph Proust's Law of Definite (Constant) Proportions.

Step 2: Postulate Statement

The postulate is Postulate 6:
"The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound."
(Also supported by Postulate 5: "Atoms combine in the ratio of small whole numbers to form compounds.")

Step 3: Conceptual Reasoning

Since each atom of a given element possesses an invariant, characteristic mass, and since a given compound always contains the exact same ratio of constituent atoms (for example, water always consists of 2 hydrogen atoms bonded to 1 oxygen atom), the proportion by mass between the elements must always be strictly constant.

Step 4: Verification

For H₂O: Ratio of atoms is 2 H : 1 O. Mass of 2 H = 2 u; mass of 1 O = 16 u. Ratio by mass = 2 : 16 = 1 : 8 forever.

Step 5: Teacher's Exam Hack

Remember: Postulate 2 explains Conservation of Mass; Postulate 6 explains Constant Proportions. Do not mix them up in the exam!

"A unit of mass exactly equal to 1/12th (one-twelfth) of the mass of one atom of carbon-12."

Step 3: Mathematical Value

1 u = (Mass of 1 atom of ¹²C) / 12 ≈ 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁴ g = 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg

Step 4: Nomenclature Evolution

Previously abbreviated as a.m.u., modern IUPAC standards mandate the use of u (unified mass).

Step 5: Teacher's Tip

Never omit the specific isotope name: you must explicitly write carbon-12 (not just "carbon").

In-Text Q2.2: Why is it not possible to see an atom with naked eyes?
Step 1: Core Physical Reason

It is impossible to see an atom with naked eyes because atoms are unimaginably tiny (nanoscale dimensions) and far smaller than the wavelength of visible light.

Step 2: Quantitative Dimensions

• The radius of an atom is on the order of 10⁻¹⁰ metres (0.1 nanometre).
• The human eye can resolve objects down to approximately 0.1 millimetres (10⁻⁴ m) at best.
• Therefore, an atom is about 1,000,000 times smaller than the minimum resolution limit of the human eye.

Step 3: Optical Physics Limitation

Visible light has wavelengths between 400 nm and 700 nm (4 × 10⁻⁷ m to 7 × 10⁻⁷ m). Because atoms are hundreds of times smaller than light waves, light simply diffracts around them without creating an optical image.

Step 4: Modern Visualization Technologies

Today, individual atoms can only be mapped using advanced quantum instruments like the Scanning Tunnelling Microscope (STM) and Atomic Force Microscope (AFM).

Step 5: Teacher's Crisp Answer

"Atoms cannot be seen with naked eyes because their atomic radii are extraordinarily tiny (approx. 10⁻¹⁰ m), which is far beyond the resolution capability of the human eye."

In-Text Q3.1: Write down the formulae of: (i) sodium oxide, (ii) aluminium chloride, (iii) sodium sulphide, (iv) magnesium hydroxide.
Step 1: Method Applied

Apply the Criss-Cross Valency Method: identify cation and anion, list their valencies/charges, and cross them over diagonally.

Step 2: Sub-Parts Step-by-Step

(i) Sodium Oxide:
- Cation: Sodium (Na⁺, valency 1) | Anion: Oxide (O²⁻, valency 2)
- Crossing over: Na ← 2, O ← 1 ⇒ Na₂O

(ii) Aluminium Chloride:
- Cation: Aluminium (Al³⁺, valency 3) | Anion: Chloride (Cl⁻, valency 1)
- Crossing over: Al ← 1, Cl ← 3 ⇒ AlCl₃

(iii) Sodium Sulphide:
- Cation: Sodium (Na⁺, valency 1) | Anion: Sulphide (S²⁻, valency 2)
- Crossing over: Na ← 2, S ← 1 ⇒ Na₂S

(iv) Magnesium Hydroxide:
- Cation: Magnesium (Mg²⁺, valency 2) | Polyatomic Anion: Hydroxide (OH⁻, valency 1)
- Crossing over: Mg ← 1, OH ← 2 ⇒ Parentheses required! ⇒ Mg(OH)₂

Step 3: Verification

All compounds are electrically neutral:
- Na₂O: 2(+1) + 1(-2) = 0
- AlCl₃: 1(+3) + 3(-1) = 0
- Na₂S: 2(+1) + 1(-2) = 0
- Mg(OH)₂: 1(+2) + 2(-1) = 0

Step 4: Common Blunder Alert

Never write magnesium hydroxide as MgOH₂. Without brackets, the subscript 2 applies only to hydrogen, leaving only 1 oxygen!

Step 5: Final Clean Summary

(i) Na₂O  |  (ii) AlCl₃  |  (iii) Na₂S  |  (iv) Mg(OH)₂

In-Text Q3.3: What is meant by the term chemical formula?
Step 1: Core Definition

The chemical formula of a compound is a concise, symbolic representation of its chemical composition.

Step 2: Two Essential Pieces of Information Provided

A chemical formula tells us:
1. Qualitative Information: The specific elements that make up the substance (using their standard chemical symbols).
2. Quantitative Information: The exact number or simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element present in one molecule or formula unit of the substance (using numerical subscripts).

Step 3: Illustrative Example

The chemical formula of glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆. This indicates that one single molecule of glucose is composed of 6 atoms of Carbon, 12 atoms of Hydrogen, and 6 atoms of Oxygen chemically bonded together.

Step 4: Significance

It provides an internationally recognized language that allows scientists across the globe to communicate exact chemical structures unambiguously.

Step 5: Teacher's Tip

Always mention both the kinds of elements and the number of atoms in your exam definition.

In-Text Q4.4: How many atoms are present in a: (i) H₂S molecule, and (ii) PO₄³⁻ ion?
Step 1: Conceptual Rule

To find the total number of atoms, count the subscripts of all constituent element symbols. (An electric charge like ³⁻ on an ion represents extra electrons, NOT extra atoms!).

Step 2: Analysis of (i) H₂S Molecule

• Constituent elements: Hydrogen (H) and Sulphur (S)
• Number of Hydrogen atoms = 2
• Number of Sulphur atoms = 1
• Total number of atoms = 2 + 1 = 3 atoms (Triatomic molecule).

Step 3: Analysis of (ii) PO₄³⁻ Ion

• Constituent elements: Phosphorus (P) and Oxygen (O)
• Number of Phosphorus atoms = 1
• Number of Oxygen atoms = 4
• Total number of atoms = 1 + 4 = 5 atoms (Polyatomic ion).
(Note: The 3- superscript denotes 3 excess electrons giving the ion a -3 charge; it does NOT alter the atom count!).

Step 4: Common Student Trap

Many students mistakenly subtract or add 3 due to the ³⁻ superscript. Remember: charges affect electron counts, not atomic nuclei!

Step 5: Final Summary

(i) H₂S molecule contains 3 atoms.  |  (ii) PO₄³⁻ ion contains 5 atoms.

In-Text Q4.1: Calculate the molecular masses of H₂, O₂, Cl₂, CO₂, CH₄, C₂H₆, C₂H₄, NH₃, CH₃OH.
Step 1: Formula & Atomic Masses

Molecular mass = Sum of atomic masses of all atoms in the molecule (expressed in u).
Reference atomic masses: H = 1 u, C = 12 u, N = 14 u, O = 16 u, Cl = 35.5 u.

Step 2: Step-by-Step Calculations

• H₂: 2 × 1 = 2 u
• O₂: 2 × 16 = 32 u
• Cl₂: 2 × 35.5 = 71 u
• CO₂: (1 × 12) + (2 × 16) = 12 + 32 = 44 u
• CH₄: (1 × 12) + (4 × 1) = 12 + 4 = 16 u
• C₂H₆: (2 × 12) + (6 × 1) = 24 + 6 = 30 u
• C₂H₄: (2 × 12) + (4 × 1) = 24 + 4 = 28 u
• NH₃: (1 × 14) + (3 × 1) = 14 + 3 = 17 u
• CH₃OH (Methanol): (1 × 12) + (4 × 1) + (1 × 16) = 12 + 4 + 16 = 32 u

Step 3: Verification

All calculations verify against standard IUPAC relative molecular weights.

Step 4: Units Caution

Always write the unit u. If asked for molar mass, write g/mol.

Step 5: Final Answers

H₂ = 2 u, O₂ = 32 u, Cl₂ = 71 u, CO₂ = 44 u, CH₄ = 16 u, C₂H₆ = 30 u, C₂H₄ = 28 u, NH₃ = 17 u, CH₃OH = 32 u.

In-Text Q4.2: Calculate the formula unit masses of ZnO, Na₂O, K₂CO₃ (Atomic masses: Zn = 65 u, Na = 23 u, K = 39 u, C = 12 u, O = 16 u).
Step 1: Formula Unit Mass Concept

Formula unit mass is calculated identically to molecular mass, but applied to ionic crystals composed of alternating cations and anions.

Step 2: Step-by-Step Calculations

1. Zinc Oxide (ZnO):
= (1 × Atomic mass of Zn) + (1 × Atomic mass of O)
= (1 × 65 u) + (1 × 16 u) = 65 + 16 = 81 u

2. Sodium Oxide (Na₂O):
= (2 × Atomic mass of Na) + (1 × Atomic mass of O)
= (2 × 23 u) + (1 × 16 u) = 46 + 16 = 62 u

3. Potassium Carbonate (K₂CO₃):
= (2 × Atomic mass of K) + (1 × Atomic mass of C) + (3 × Atomic mass of O)
= (2 × 39 u) + (1 × 12 u) + (3 × 16 u) = 78 + 12 + 48 = 138 u

Step 3: Verification

78 + 12 + 48 = 138 u. Arithmetic is verified.

Step 4: Terminological Note

We use the term formula unit mass instead of molecular mass for ZnO, Na₂O, and K₂CO₃ because these compounds form infinite 3D crystalline arrays rather than isolated discrete molecules.

Step 5: Final Answers

ZnO = 81 u  |  Na₂O = 62 u  |  K₂CO₃ = 138 u

In-Text Q4.3: If one mole of carbon atoms weighs 12 grams, what is the mass (in grams) of 1 atom of carbon?
Step 1: Governing Mole Concept Principle

One mole of carbon atoms contains exactly the Avogadro number of atoms (N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ atoms) and weighs exactly 12 grams.

Step 2: Mathematical Relationship

Mass of 1 atom of Carbon = (Molar Mass of Carbon) / (Avogadro Constant, N_A)

Step 3: Step-by-Step Calculation

Mass of 1 C atom = 12 g / (6.022 × 10²³)
= (12 / 6.022) × 10⁻²³ g
= 1.99269 × 10⁻²³ g ≈ 1.99 × 10⁻²³ g

Step 4: Physical Sanity Check

The mass of an atom is submicroscopic, so an exponent of 10⁻²³ g confirms the answer is physically realistic.

Step 5: Final Answer

The mass of 1 single atom of carbon is 1.99 × 10⁻²³ grams (or 1.99 × 10⁻²⁶ kg).

In-Text Q4.4: Which has more number of atoms: 100 grams of sodium or 100 grams of iron (Atomic masses: Na = 23 u, Fe = 56 u)?
Step 1: Governing Concept

The number of atoms in a given sample is directly proportional to the number of moles (n = m / M). For equal given masses, the substance with the lower molar mass will contain a greater number of moles and therefore more atoms!

Step 2: Given Data

• Sample 1: Mass of Sodium (m_Na) = 100 g; Molar mass (M_Na) = 23 g/mol
• Sample 2: Mass of Iron (m_Fe) = 100 g; Molar mass (M_Fe) = 56 g/mol

Step 3: Step-by-Step Calculation for Sodium

Moles of Na (n_Na) = 100 / 23 = 4.348 mol
Number of Na atoms (N_Na) = 4.348 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 2.618 × 10²⁴ atoms

Step 4: Step-by-Step Calculation for Iron

Moles of Fe (n_Fe) = 100 / 56 = 1.786 mol
Number of Fe atoms (N_Fe) = 1.786 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.075 × 10²⁴ atoms

Step 5: Final Comparison & Teacher's Tip

Since 4.348 moles > 1.786 moles (2.618 × 10²⁴ > 1.075 × 10²⁴), 100 grams of sodium contains significantly more atoms than 100 grams of iron (more than double!).
Teacher's Intuition: Because individual sodium atoms are lighter than iron atoms, it takes many more sodium atoms to reach 100 grams!

Exercise Q1: A 0.24 g sample of compound of oxygen and boron was found by analysis to contain 0.096 g of boron and 0.144 g of oxygen. Calculate the percentage composition of the compound by weight.
Step 1: Governing Concept & Formula

The percentage composition by mass of an element in a compound is calculated using the formula:
Percentage of Element = [ (Mass of Element in Sample) / (Total Mass of Sample) ] × 100%

Step 2: Given Data

• Total mass of the compound sample = 0.24 g
• Mass of Boron in the sample = 0.096 g
• Mass of Oxygen in the sample = 0.144 g
(Check: 0.096 g + 0.144 g = 0.240 g, conservation of mass holds!)

Step 3: Calculation of Percentage of Boron

% of Boron = (0.096 / 0.24) × 100%
= (96 / 240) × 100% = (24 / 60) × 100% = 0.40 × 100% = 40.0%

Step 4: Calculation of Percentage of Oxygen

% of Oxygen = (0.144 / 0.24) × 100%
= (144 / 240) × 100% = (36 / 60) × 100% = 0.60 × 100% = 60.0%

Step 5: Verification & Final Answer

Check: 40% + 60% = 100.0%.
Final Answer: The percentage composition of the compound is 40% Boron and 60% Oxygen by weight.

Exercise Q2: When 3.0 g of carbon is burnt in 8.00 g oxygen, 11.00 g of carbon dioxide is produced. What mass of carbon dioxide will be formed when 3.00 g of carbon is burnt in 50.00 g of oxygen? Which law of chemical combination will govern your answer?
Step 1: Governing Law of Chemical Combination

This problem is governed by the Law of Definite Proportions (Proust), which states that in a chemical substance, elements always combine in fixed proportions by mass. (Also supported by the concept of limiting reagent).

Step 2: Analysis of Initial Data

From the first reaction:
3.0 g Carbon + 8.0 g Oxygen → 11.0 g Carbon Dioxide
The mass ratio of combination between Carbon and Oxygen is strictly:
Mass of Carbon : Mass of Oxygen = 3 : 8

Step 3: Evaluating the Second Scenario

• Available Carbon = 3.00 g
• Available Oxygen = 50.00 g
According to the fixed 3 : 8 mass ratio, 3.00 g of carbon requires ONLY 8.00 g of oxygen to react completely and form carbon dioxide.
Carbon is the limiting reactant because it is completely exhausted first.

Step 4: Product Formation & Excess Reactant

• Mass of Carbon Dioxide formed = 3.00 g (C) + 8.00 g (O consumed) = 11.00 g of CO₂
• Mass of unreacted (excess) Oxygen remaining = 50.00 g - 8.00 g = 42.00 g of unreacted O₂ gas.

Step 5: Final Model Answer

Mass of CO₂ formed: Exactly 11.00 grams of carbon dioxide will be formed.
Governing Law: The Law of Definite Proportions (Law of Constant Composition) governs this answer, because carbon and oxygen can only combine in the fixed mass ratio of 3:8 to form carbon dioxide, regardless of how much excess oxygen is present.

Exercise Q3: What are polyatomic ions? Give examples.
Step 1: Core Definition

A polyatomic ion is a group or cluster of two or more covalently bonded atoms that carries an overall net electric charge and behaves as a single indivisible chemical unit in reactions.

Step 2: Physical Origin of the Charge

The net charge arises because the total number of protons in the entire atomic cluster is not equal to the total number of electrons:

Step 3: Cation Examples (Positively Charged)

• Ammonium ion (NH₄⁺): Formed by 1 nitrogen atom and 4 hydrogen atoms carrying a net charge of +1. (Total valence electrons = 5 + 4 - 1 = 8).

Step 4: Anion Examples (Negatively Charged)

• Hydroxide ion (OH⁻): Net charge -1
• Nitrate ion (NO₃⁻): Net charge -1
• Carbonate ion (CO₃²⁻): Net charge -2
• Sulphate ion (SO₄²⁻): Net charge -2
• Phosphate ion (PO₄³⁻): Net charge -3

Step 5: Teacher's Exam Hack

Always write both positive (NH₄⁺) and negative (SO₄²⁻, OH⁻) examples to demonstrate comprehensive mastery.

Exercise Q8: Convert into mole: (a) 12 g of oxygen gas, (b) 20 g of water, (c) 22 g of carbon dioxide.

Exercise Q10: Calculate the number of molecules of sulphur (S₈) present in 16 g of solid sulphur.
Step 1: Governing Master Formula

Number of Molecules (N) = (m / M) × N_A
where m = given mass, M = molar mass of S₈, N_A = Avogadro constant (6.022 × 10²³).

Step 2: Molar Mass of Sulphur Molecule (S₈)

Solid sulphur exists as an octa-atomic molecule (S₈):
M = 8 × 32 g/mol = 256 g/mol

Step 3: Number of Moles of S₈

n = 16 g / 256 g/mol = 1 / 16 = 0.0625 mole

Step 4: Step-by-Step Calculation of Molecule Count

= (1 / 16) × 6.022 × 10²³

Exercise Q11: Calculate the number of aluminium ions present in 0.051 g of aluminium oxide. (Hint: The mass of an ion is the same as that of an atom of the same element. Atomic mass of Al = 27 u).
Step 1: Chemical Formula & Stoichiometric Ratio

The chemical formula of aluminium oxide is Al₂O₃.
This indicates that 1 formula unit of Al₂O₃ contains exactly 2 aluminium ions (Al³⁺) and 3 oxide ions (O²⁻).
Therefore: 1 mole of Al₂O₃ contains 2 moles of Al³⁺ ions.

Step 2: Molar Mass of Aluminium Oxide (Al₂O₃)

Atomic masses: Al = 27 u, O = 16 u
M(Al₂O₃) = (2 × 27) + (3 × 16) = 54 + 48 = 102 g/mol

Step 3: Moles of Al₂O₃ in 0.051 g

n(Al₂O₃) = m / M = 0.051 g / 102 g/mol = 51 / 102000 = 1 / 2000 = 5 × 10⁻⁴ mole

Step 4: Calculating Total Aluminium Ions

Number of formula units of Al₂O₃ = n × N_A = (5 × 10⁻⁴) × (6.022 × 10²³) = 3.011 × 10²⁰ units.
Since each unit contains 2 Al³⁺ ions:
Total Al³⁺ ions = 2 × (3.011 × 10²⁰)
= 6.022 × 10²⁰ aluminium ions

Step 5: Final Evaluation & Teacher's Note

Final Answer: There are 6.022 × 10²⁰ aluminium ions (Al³⁺) present in 0.051 g of aluminium oxide.
Teacher's Tip: Notice the elegant mathematical symmetry: 0.051 g is exactly (1/2000) of 102 g, and multiplying by 2 Al ions cleanly yields 6.022 × 10²⁰ ions!

SA Q1 (2M): State the two laws of chemical combination. Who formulated them?

SA Q3 (3M): State any three major limitations/drawbacks of Dalton's Atomic Theory in light of modern atomic discoveries.
Limitation 1: Divisibility of the Atom [1 Mark]

Dalton stated that atoms are indivisible. Modern physics discovered subatomic particles (electrons, protons, and neutrons), proving that atoms can be divided into smaller constituent parts and can even undergo nuclear fission.

Limitation 2: Existence of Isotopes [1 Mark]

Dalton claimed that all atoms of a given element have identical mass. The discovery of isotopes showed that atoms of the same element can have different masses (e.g., Chlorine has isotopes of mass 35 u and 37 u; Carbon has ¹²C and ¹⁴C).

Limitation 3: Existence of Isobars [1 Mark]

Dalton stated that atoms of different elements must have different masses. The discovery of isobars proved that atoms of different elements can have the exact same mass number (e.g., Argon and Calcium both have a mass number of 40 u).

SA Q5 (3M): Differentiate between Molecular Mass and Formula Unit Mass. Why do we prefer the term formula unit mass for Sodium Chloride (NaCl)?
Definition Comparison [1.5 Marks]

• Molecular Mass: The sum of atomic masses of all atoms present in a discrete, independent molecule held together by covalent bonds (e.g., H₂O, CO₂, CH₄).
• Formula Unit Mass: The sum of atomic masses of all atoms present in the empirical formula unit of an ionic compound.

Reason for Preferring Formula Unit Mass for NaCl [1.5 Marks]

In solid sodium chloride, isolated discrete "NaCl molecules" do not exist! Instead, sodium chloride forms an infinite three-dimensional crystal lattice where each Na⁺ ion is surrounded symmetrically by 6 Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion is surrounded by 6 Na⁺ ions.
Since there is no discrete single molecule, we use the simplest empirical ratio (1 Na⁺ : 1 Cl⁻), called a formula unit, and its mass (23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u) is appropriately designated as Formula Unit Mass.

Part 4: Long Answer Questions (5M with Marking Schemes)

LA Q1 (5M): (a) State Dalton's Atomic Theory. List its six postulates. (b) Explain clearly which postulate accounts for the Law of Conservation of Mass and which accounts for the Law of Definite Proportions.
Part (a): Six Canonical Postulates [3 Marks]

Formulated by John Dalton in 1808:
1. Composition of Matter: All matter is composed of extremely tiny particles called atoms. [0.5 M]
2. Indivisibility & Indestructibility: Atoms are indivisible particles that cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. [0.5 M]
3. Identity of Same Element: Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties. [0.5 M]
4. Distinction of Different Elements: Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties. [0.5 M]
5. Whole-Number Combination: Atoms combine in the ratio of small whole numbers to form compounds. [0.5 M]
6. Constancy in Compounds: The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound. [0.5 M]

Part (b): Theoretical Explanation of Chemical Laws [2 Marks]

• Explanation of Law of Conservation of Mass [1 Mark]:
Governed by Postulate 2 (Atoms cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction). Since a chemical reaction is merely a regrouping or rearrangement of indestructible atoms, the total count and total mass of each type of atom must remain invariant before and after the reaction. Hence, total reactant mass equals total product mass.

• Explanation of Law of Definite Proportions [1 Mark]:
Governed by Postulate 6 (The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound). Because every atom of a given element possesses an identical characteristic mass (Postulate 3) and combines in fixed small whole-number ratios (Postulate 5), the ratio of masses of the combining elements in that compound must forever remain strictly fixed and constant.

LA Q2 (5M): (a) Explain the rules for writing the chemical formula of a compound. (b) Derive the balanced chemical formula for: (i) Aluminium sulphate, (ii) Calcium carbonate, (iii) Ferric oxide, (iv) Ammonium carbonate.
Part (a): Three Golden IUPAC Rules [2 Marks]

1. Charge Neutrality: The valencies or electrical charges on the combining ions must balance so that the net charge of the compound is zero. [0.5 M]
2. Position of Metal: When a compound consists of a metal and a non-metal, the name and symbol of the metal must be written first on the left. [0.5 M]
3. Parenthesis Rule for Polyatomic Ions: When the number of polyatomic ions in the formula is two or more, the formula of the polyatomic ion must be enclosed inside brackets before writing the subscript. If subscript is 1, no brackets are used. [1 M]

Case Study 1: The Industrial Synthesis of Quicklime & Open Systems

In an industrial lime kiln at Rourkela Steel Plant in Odisha, calcium carbonate (limestone, CaCO₃) is calcined at temperatures exceeding 900°C to produce calcium oxide (quicklime, CaO), an essential fluxing agent in steel manufacturing. The chemical decomposition reaction is represented as:
CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)↑
An analytical chemist feeds exactly 500 kg of pure limestone into the industrial calciner. After completion of thermal decomposition, the solid quicklime residue extracted weighs exactly 280 kg, while a large cloud of carbon dioxide gas is safely vented through the industrial scrubber chimney.

Question 1.1: Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide gas vented into the chimney scrubber.
Concept

According to the Law of Conservation of Mass, Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products.

Calculation

Mass(CaCO₃) = Mass(CaO) + Mass(CO₂)
500 kg = 280 kg + Mass(CO₂)
Mass(CO₂) = 500 kg - 280 kg = 220 kg.

Question 1.2: A trainee operator claims that 220 kg of matter was destroyed in the furnace. Refute this claim with scientific reasoning.
Refutation & Scientific Reasoning

The operator's claim is completely false. The industrial lime kiln is an open thermodynamic system. Carbon dioxide is an invisible, odourless gas that escaped through the exhaust chimney into the scrubber. The mass of the gaseous CO₂ (220 kg) plus the solid residue (280 kg) precisely equals the original 500 kg of limestone. Matter was simply transformed into a gaseous state, not destroyed!

M(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100 g/mol.

Unit Conversion & Moles

Given mass (m) = 500 kg = 500 × 1000 g = 500,000 g.
Moles (n) = 500,000 g / 100 g/mol = 5,000 moles (or 5.0 × 10³ mol).

5 Common Student Blunders & Exam Hall Traps

Blunder 1: Confusing Atomic Mass (u) with Molar Mass (g/mol)

Students frequently confuse the units when calculating masses. For instance, writing that 1 atom of oxygen weighs 16 grams!

❌ The Blunder:
"The mass of one oxygen atom is 16 g." (Physically impossible! 16 g of oxygen contains over 600,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 atoms!).
✅ Correct Exam Presentation:
• Mass of 1 single atom of Oxygen = 16 u (or 2.66 × 10⁻²³ g).
• Mass of 1 mole of Oxygen atoms = 16 g (or 16 g/mol).
Blunder 2: Omitting Parentheses in Polyatomic Formulas

When applying the criss-cross method to polyatomic ions, many students omit parentheses, fundamentally altering the chemical identity of the compound.

❌ The Blunder:
Writing CaOH₂ for Calcium hydroxide or AlSO₄₃ for Aluminium sulphate.
✅ Correct Exam Presentation:
Write Ca(OH)₂ and Al₂(SO₄)₃. The subscript outside the bracket multiplies every atom inside the polyatomic radical.
Blunder 3: The Diatomic Gas Trap in Mole Calculations

When an exam question asks: "Calculate the number of moles in 32 g of Oxygen gas", students frequently divide by 16 instead of 32.

❌ The Blunder:
n = 32 / 16 = 2 moles (Using atomic mass for elementary gas).
✅ Correct Exam Presentation:
"Oxygen gas" always exists as diatomic molecules (O₂).
Molar mass M = 2 × 16 = 32 g/mol.
Therefore, n = 32 / 32 = 1.0 mole!
Blunder 4: Misapplying Conservation of Mass in Open Systems

Assuming that the Law of Conservation of Mass has failed when an open reaction loses mass (e.g., burning magnesium, heating baking soda, open lime kilns).

❌ The Blunder:
"Mass decreased after heating limestone, so conservation of mass does not apply to decomposition reactions."
✅ Correct Exam Presentation:
State clearly that the reaction vessel was an open system and gaseous products (CO₂) escaped into the atmosphere. The law is universally obeyed when all products are accounted for in a closed system.
Blunder 5: Confusing Total Atoms with Total Molecules

Forgetting to multiply the number of moles/molecules by the atomicity when the question asks for total number of atoms.

❌ The Blunder:
"1 mole of H₂O contains 6.022 × 10²³ atoms."
✅ Correct Exam Presentation:
1 mole of H₂O contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules.
Since each H₂O molecule has 3 atoms (2 H + 1 O), the total atom count is:
3 × (6.022 × 10²³) = 1.8066 × 10²⁴ atoms!

15 Rapid Revision Memory Points (Exam-Night Cram Kit)

  1. Parmanu: Ancient Indian philosopher Maharishi Kanad named the smallest indivisible particle of matter Parmanu; Democritus called it Atomos (uncuttable).
  2. Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier, 1774): Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction (Σ M_reactants = Σ M_products).
  3. Law of Definite Proportions (Proust, 1799): In a pure chemical compound, the constituent elements are always combined in a fixed, constant ratio by mass (e.g., Water H:O = 1:8; Ammonia N:H = 14:3; Carbon dioxide C:O = 3:8).
  4. Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808): Proposed that matter is composed of tiny indivisible particles called atoms that combine in small whole numbers. Postulate 2 explains Conservation of Mass; Postulate 6 explains Constant Proportions.
  5. Modern Revisions to Dalton: Atoms are divisible into subatomic particles (e⁻, p⁺, n⁰); isotopes have different masses; isobars have identical masses.
  6. Nanoscale Atomic Dimensions: Atomic radius is measured in nanometres (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m). The smallest atom is Hydrogen (r ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m).
  7. IUPAC Symbols (Berzelius): First letter is strictly uppercase; second letter is strictly lowercase (e.g., Co is Cobalt metal; CO is Carbon Monoxide gas).
  8. Latin Roots of Symbols: Sodium (Na, Natrium), Potassium (K, Kalium), Iron (Fe, Ferrum), Copper (Cu, Cuprum), Silver (Ag, Argentum), Gold (Au, Aurum), Mercury (Hg, Hydrargyrum), Lead (Pb, Plumbum).
  9. Unified Atomic Mass Unit (u): Exactly equal to 1/12th of the mass of one atom of Carbon-12 (1 u ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g), adopted internationally in 1961.
  10. Atomicity: The number of atoms in a molecule: Monoatomic (He, Ne, Ar); Diatomic (H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂); Triatomic (O₃); Tetra-atomic (P₄); Octa-atomic (S₈).
  11. Ions & Charges: Cations are positively charged (formed by electron loss, e.g., Na⁺, Ca²⁺, NH₄⁺); Anions are negatively charged (formed by electron gain, e.g., Cl⁻, O²⁻, SO₄²⁻).
  12. Criss-Cross Formula Writing: Cross over valencies diagonally. Use parentheses when a polyatomic ion carries a subscript greater than 1: Al₂(SO₄)₃, Ca(OH)₂.
  13. Molecular Mass vs Formula Unit Mass: Molecular mass is for discrete covalent molecules (e.g., H₂O = 18 u); Formula unit mass is for 3D continuous ionic crystals (e.g., NaCl = 58.5 u).
  14. The Mole (N_A): 1 mole of any substance contains exactly 6.022 × 10²³ elementary entities (Avogadro Constant). The molar mass in grams is numerically identical to the atomic/molecular mass in unified units (u).
  15. Master Stoichiometric Equations:
    n = m / M = N / N_A   ⇒   N = (m / M) × 6.022 × 10²³
