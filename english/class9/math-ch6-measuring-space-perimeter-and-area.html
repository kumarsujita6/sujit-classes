6.1 Perimeter of a Shape

Core Definition: What is Perimeter?

The perimeter of any closed two-dimensional planar shape is the total length around its boundary. Imagine a tiny ant taking a walk along the outer edge of a shape: if it starts at one point and walks all the way around without ever turning back until it returns to its exact starting point, the total distance walked is the perimeter of that shape.

Let us write down the perimeter formulas for familiar straight-edged regular and standard shapes:

  • Square (side a units): A square has 4 equal straight sides. Its perimeter is P = a + a + a + a = 4a units.
  • Equilateral Triangle (side a units): All 3 sides are equal. Its perimeter is P = a + a + a = 3a units.
  • Rectangle (length a units, width b units): Opposite sides are equal. Its perimeter is P = a + b + a + b = 2(a + b) units. (Note: A square is simply a special rectangle where a = b, giving 2(a + a) = 4a).
  • Regular n-sided Polygon (side s units): An n-gon with all sides equal has perimeter P = n × s units.
The Universal Linear Scaling Law of Perimeter

What happens to the perimeter of a shape if we double every one of its side lengths? It doubles as well! If we triple the sides, the perimeter triples. In general, if all linear dimensions of a shape are multiplied by a scale factor k, its perimeter is multiplied by exactly k.

Notice also the invariance of the ratio of perimeter to side:
• For every square, Perimeter ÷ Side = 4a / a = 4 : 1, regardless of how small or large the square is!
• For every equilateral triangle, Perimeter ÷ Side = 3a / a = 3 : 1, always constant!

6.2 Perimeter of a Circle — The C/D Ratio and the History of π

Now consider a circle of radius r units and diameter D = 2r units. The perimeter of a circle is called its circumference (C). How do we find its length? Does the ratio of circumference to diameter stay constant for circles of all sizes, just like the 4:1 ratio for squares?

Home Measurement Experiment: Estimating the C/D Ratio

You can verify this constant ratio at home using a simple cotton reel and thin thread:
1. Measure the diameter D of the cotton reel with a ruler as accurately as possible.
2. Tightly wind thin thread around the reel exactly 20 times without overlapping.
3. Unwrap the thread, stretch it straight, and measure its total length L with a tape measure.
4. Calculate L ÷ (20 × D). This represents the circumference of one loop divided by the diameter.
You will find that your answer is always a number between 3.1 and 3.2, regardless of whether you used a small reel, a coin, a bicycle wheel, or a large circular plate!

The Great Historical Journey of π: From Ancient Approximations to Mādhava's Exact Series

The ratio C ÷ D is an eternal mathematical constant denoted by the Greek letter π (pronounced 'pi' as in pie, not pizza). Here is humanity's extraordinary intellectual journey to unveil it:

  • Mesopotamia (c. 1900 BCE): Ancient Babylonian mathematicians observed that an inscribed regular hexagon within a circle of radius r has side length equal to r. Therefore, the hexagon’s perimeter is 6r = 3 × (2r) = 3D. Because the circle bulges outside the hexagon, the circle's circumference must be strictly larger than the hexagon's perimeter (C > 3D). Hence, π > 3! They estimated π ≈ 3 + 1/8 = 3.125.
  • Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 250 BCE): In his treatise Measurement of a Circle, Archimedes introduced the rigorous method of exhaustion. By "trapping" a circle of radius 1 between an inscribed polygon (inside) and a circumscribed polygon (outside), he proved that the circle’s perimeter lies strictly between their perimeters!
a = r = 1 • Perimeter = 6 × 1 = 6 • Since Hexagon < Circle: 6 < 2π(1) ⇒ π > 3 2. Circumscribed Hexagon (Outer): • Side a' = 2r / √3 = 2 / √3 • Perimeter = 6 × (2/√3) = 4√3 • Since Circle < Outer Hexagon: 2π(1) < 4√3 ⇒ π < 2√3 ≈ 3.464 Hexagon Trapping Inequality: 3 < π < 2√3 ≈ 3.4641
Figure 6.1: Archimedes' Geometric Trapping Method using an Inscribed Hexagon (inner blue) and Circumscribed Hexagon (outer green) around a unit circle (r = 1).

Archimedes did not stop at hexagons. By using angle bisection repeatedly, he calculated the perimeters of 12-gons, 24-gons, 48-gons, and finally 96-sided regular polygons! Through this monumental calculation, Archimedes trapped π within tight boundaries:

3 + 10/71 < π < 3 + 1/7   ⟺   223/71 < π < 22/7   ⟺   3.140845... < π < 3.142857...
  • Ptolemy of Alexandria (c. 150 CE): Derived π ≈ 377 / 120 = 3 + 17/120 ≈ 3.14167 for astronomical tables.
  • Liu Hui (263 CE) & Zu Chongzhi (480 CE) in China: Liu Hui developed the "circle-cutting method". Zu Chongzhi took this to a 24,576-sided polygon, discovering the Yuelü (rough ratio) of 22/7 ≈ 3.1428 and the celebrated Milü (close ratio) of 355 / 113 ≈ 3.1415929. This fraction is accurate to 6 decimal places and remained the most accurate fraction in world history for over 800 years!
  • Āryabhaṭa I (499 CE, India): In the Āryabhaṭīya (Gaṇitapāda, Verse 10), Āryabhaṭa gave his renowned verse:
    "Add 4 to 100, multiply by 8, and then add 62,000; by this rule the circumference of a circle with a diameter of 20,000 can be approached."
    C / D = [(100 + 4) × 8 + 62000] / 20000 = 62832 / 20000 = 3.1416.
    Crucially, Āryabhaṭa used the Sanskrit word āsanna (meaning "approaching" or "approximate"). He was the first mathematician in world history to explicitly recognize that the true ratio cannot be expressed as an exact finite ratio of integers!
  • Brahmagupta (628 CE): Proposed π ≈ √10 ≈ 3.1622. While slightly less accurate than Āryabhaṭa’s value, it was chosen for mathematical simplicity when manipulating radical equations, becoming widely adopted in the Arab world and medieval Europe.
  • Mādhava of Sangamagrāma (c. 1340–1425 CE, Kerala School): Mādhava transformed mathematics forever by moving from finite geometric polygon cutting to infinite series calculus. He discovered the first exact analytical formula for π:
    π / 4 = 1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - 1/7 + 1/9 - 1/11 + ...   =   ∑ [(-1)ⁿ / (2n + 1)]
    This allowed Mādhava to compute π accurately to 11 decimal places (3.14159265358), inaugurating the foundational concepts of modern mathematical analysis!
  • Notation: In 1706, Welsh mathematician William Jones used the Greek letter π (the initial letter of the Greek word perimetros, meaning perimeter) to denote the C/D ratio. Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler popularized it in 1737, making it the universal standard.

6.3 π Is an Irrational Number

Recall the fundamental classification of real numbers:

  • Rational Numbers (ℚ): Numbers that can be written in the form a / b, where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0. In decimal form, every rational number either terminates (e.g., 1/4 = 0.25) or repeats with a periodic pattern (e.g., 1/3 = 0.3333..., 1/7 = 0.142857142857...).
  • Irrational Numbers: Numbers that cannot be written as the ratio of two integers. Their decimal expansions are non-terminating and non-recurring (they go on forever without ever settling into a repeating cycle).
Critical Truth: π ≠ 22/7

In school examinations, we commonly use 22/7 for quick calculations. But remember: π is NOT equal to 22/7!
• 22 / 7 = 3.142857 142857... (a rational repeating decimal).
• π = 3.141592 653589... (an irrational non-repeating number).
Comparing the two: 22/7 - π ≈ 0.001264. Thus, 22/7 is slightly larger than π by about 0.04%. We write π ≈ 22/7, but mathematically π ≠ 22/7.

The French-Swiss mathematician Johann Heinrich Lambert proved in 1761 that π is strictly irrational using the continued fraction expansion of tan(x). Later in 1882, Ferdinand von Lindemann proved that π is transcendental (meaning π is not the root of any non-zero polynomial equation with rational coefficients). This finally proved the ancient Greek conjecture: it is mathematically impossible to "square a circle" using only an unmarked straightedge and compass!

6.4 Length of an Arc of a Circle & Track Stagger Geometry

The circumference of a complete circle of radius r (or diameter d = 2r) is C = 2πr = πd. What is the length of a piece of the circumference (an arc)?

  • Semicircular Arc (180°): If we cut a circle along a diameter into two equal halves, reflecting the figure across the diameter or rotating it by 180° swaps the two arcs. By symmetry, the length of a semicircular arc is exactly half the circumference:
    ℓ_semicircle = (2πr) ÷ 2 = πr = 2πr × (180° / 360°)
  • Quarter-Circle Arc (90°): Rotating the circle by a quarter-turn (90°) maps each quarter-circle onto the next. Thus, each quarter-circle arc has length:
    ℓ_quarter = (2πr) ÷ 4 = πr / 2 = 2πr × (90° / 360°)
  • General Circular Arc Subtending Central Angle θ°: By dividing the 360° full rotation into fractional parts, an arc subtending angle θ° at the centre O has arc length:
    ℓ = (θ° / 360°) × 2πr = (θ° / 180°) × πr
w = 1.22 m: Stagger = 2πw = 2 × (22/7) × 1.22 ≈ 7.665 metres • 200 m race (1 curve): πw ≈ 3.83 m

Why the Track Stagger Does NOT Depend on Track Radius!

Students often intuitively assume that a smaller school track (e.g. a 200 m track) requires a smaller stagger than the Olympics. This is a common trap!
Look at the algebra:
Extra Distance = 2π(R + w) - 2πR = 2πR + 2πw - 2πR = 2πw.
Notice that the track radius R completely cancels out! The required stagger between any two adjacent lanes depends only on the lane width w. For any track with two semicircles of identical lane width w = 1.22 m, the 400 m stagger is always identically 2πw ≈ 7.67 m, regardless of how tight or wide the curves are!

6.5 Problems, Puzzles, and Paradoxes on Perimeter

Example 1: Interlocking Circles Passing Through Centres

Two congruent circles of radius r are located such that each passes through the centre of the other. The centres are A and B, and the circles intersect at C and D. What is the total perimeter of the outer figure formed by the two major arcs?

Step-by-step reasoning:
1. In triangle ABC, AB = r (distance between centres), AC = r (radius of circle A), and BC = r (radius of circle B). Therefore, ΔABC is an equilateral triangle!
2. Thus, ∠CAB = 60°. By identical reasoning on the lower half, ΔABD is equilateral, so ∠DAB = 60°.
3. The total angle subtended by the inner chord CD at centre A is ∠CAD = 60° + 60° = 120°.
4. The inner arc is therefore 120° / 360° = 1/3 of the circumference.
5. The outer exposed arc of circle A subtends 360° - 120° = 240°, which is 240° / 360° = 2/3 of the circle's circumference.
6. By symmetry, the outer exposed arc of circle B is also 2/3 of its circumference.
7. Therefore, the total perimeter is: Total Perimeter = 2 × [ (2/3) × 2πr ] = (8/3) πr units.

Example 2: Semicircle Path vs Three Semicircles Path along PQ

Points P and Q are connected by two alternative paths:
• Path 1: One large semicircle of radius a' spanning diameter PQ.
• Path 2: A chain of three smaller semicircles with radii b', c', d' constructed along the straight segment PQ.
Which path is longer?

Beautiful Algebraic Equality

Let us compute their lengths:
• Length of Path 1 = π × a'.
• Length of Path 2 = πb' + πc' + πd' = π(b' + c' + d').
Now look at the total straight distance PQ:
The diameter of the large semicircle is 2a'.
The sum of diameters of the three small semicircles along the segment is 2b' + 2c' + 2d'.
Since both span the identical straight segment PQ:
2a' = 2b' + 2c' + 2d'  ⇒  a' = b' + c' + d'!
Multiplying both sides by π gives:
πa' = π(b' + c' + d').
Conclusion: The two paths have EXACTLY the same length!

The Famous "Belt Around the Earth" Paradox

Imagine a smooth steel rope tied tightly around the equator of the Earth (radius R ≈ 6371 km). Now suppose we splice in just 1 extra metre of rope so its total circumference increases by 1 metre. If the loosened rope is held uniformly above the ground all around the equator, how high off the ground does it hover? Could a mouse crawl under it?

The surprising calculation:
Let original circumference be C₁ = 2πR.
The new circumference is C₂ = 2π(R + h) = 2πR + 2πh.
The increase in circumference is ΔC = C₂ - C₁ = 2πh = 1 metre.
Therefore, the hover height is: h = 1 / (2π) ≈ 1 / 6.283 ≈ 0.159 metres = 15.9 cm!
Not only a mouse, but a cat could easily walk right under the rope!
Even more mind-boggling: if you did the exact same experiment on a basketball (radius 12 cm) or a giant star, lengthening the rope by 1 metre lifts the rope by exactly 15.9 cm in every single case, because the radius R completely cancels out!

6.6 Area of a Rectangle & The Concept of Unit Space

What is Area?

Area is the measure of the amount of two-dimensional surface enclosed within a closed boundary. Measurement is always relative to a chosen standard unit. For planar area, our fundamental unit of measurement is a 1 unit × 1 unit square. Its area is defined as exactly 1 square unit (1 unit²).

If a rectangle has length a units and width b units, it can be tiled exactly by a × b unit squares. Therefore, the area of a rectangle is:

Area(Rectangle) = Length × Breadth = a × b = ab sq. units

For a square of side a (where length = breadth = a), the area is Area(Square) = a × a = a² sq. units.

The Quadratic Area Scaling Law

While perimeter scales linearly with scale factor k (P' = kP), area scales quadratically with k²!
• If you double the length and breadth of a rectangle (k = 2), its area becomes (2a) × (2b) = 4ab — it quadruples!
• If you triple the dimensions (k = 3), its area becomes 3² = 9 times larger.

6.7 Area of a Parallelogram & The "Thin Parallelogram" Problem

A parallelogram ABCD has base b and perpendicular height (altitude) h. How do we find its area without using coordinates?

We use the geometric principle of shearing (dissection and rearrangement):
1. Drop perpendiculars from vertex A to CD at E, and from vertex B to CD extended at F.
2. The right-angled triangle ΔADE is congruent to ΔBCF (by RHS or hypotenuse-side congruence).
3. Cutting off ΔADE from the left and pasting it onto the right at ΔBCF transforms the parallelogram into a rectangle ABFE with the exact same base b and same height h.
4. Therefore, Area(Parallelogram) = Base × Height = bh sq. units!

Think and Reflect: Can Side Lengths Alone Determine a Parallelogram's Area?

NO! Absolutely not! If a rectangle has side lengths 6 cm and 4 cm, its area is fixed at 6 × 4 = 24 cm².
However, if a parallelogram has side lengths 6 cm and 4 cm, its area can be any value from 0 up to 24 cm²!
Why? Because the four sides can hinge at the vertices. As you squash the parallelogram flatter and flatter, the angle between the sides decreases, and the perpendicular height h = 4 sin θ collapses towards 0. Side lengths alone do NOT lock the area of a parallelogram!

6.8 Area of a Triangle & The Median Bisection Theorem

Every triangle ABC can be paired with an exact congruent copy ΔA'B'C' rotated by 180° to form a complete parallelogram of base b and height h.
Since the two triangles are congruent, their areas are identical.
Therefore:

Area(Triangle) = ½ × Area(Parallelogram) = ½ × Base × Height = ½ bh sq. units

The Median Bisection Theorem

Fundamental Theorem: Median Divides a Triangle into Two Equal Areas

Statement: Any median of a triangle divides it into two triangles of equal area.
Proof: In ΔABC, let D be the midpoint of base BC, so AD is a median.
1. In ΔABD, the base is BD.
2. In ΔACD, the base is DC. Since D is the midpoint of BC, BD = DC.
3. Both triangles share the common vertex A, so they have the exact same perpendicular altitude h drawn from A to the line BC.
4. Area(ΔABD) = ½ × BD × h = ½ × DC × h = Area(ΔACD).
Q.E.D. Note that ΔABD and ΔACD are generally NOT congruent to each other (they have different shapes), yet their enclosed areas are mathematically identical!

Think and Reflect: Scissors Congruence (Wallace-Bolyai-Gerwien Theorem)

Can you cut ΔABD into a finite number of polygonal pieces using straight scissors cuts and rearrange them to perfectly cover ΔACD?
YES! The celebrated Wallace-Bolyai-Gerwien Theorem (proved independently by William Wallace in 1807, Farkas Bolyai in 1832, and P. Gerwien in 1833) states that:
"Any two simple polygons having equal area are always scissors-congruent (equi-decomposable) — they can be dissected into a finite number of straight-edged pieces that can be rearranged to form the other shape."

Think and Reflect: Rectangles with Perimeter 40 — Optimization

Consider all possible rectangles with fixed perimeter P = 2(a + b) = 40 units (so a + b = 20).
1. How many such rectangles exist? Infinitely many (dimensions can be non-integers, e.g. 10.5 × 9.5, 19.9 × 0.1, etc.).
2. Which rectangle has the LARGEST area?
Let length be 10 + x and breadth be 10 - x.
Area = (10 + x)(10 - x) = 100 - x².
Because x² ≥ 0, the area is maximized when x = 0, which gives a = b = 10 (a square!). The maximum area is 10 × 10 = 100 unit².
3. Which rectangle has the SMALLEST area?
As x → 10, length a → 20 and width b → 0. The area ab → 0. There is no strictly positive minimum; the area can be made as close to 0 as you please!

6.8.1 Heron's Formula & Brahmagupta's Formula for Cyclic Quadrilaterals

What if we know the lengths of all three sides a, b, c of a triangle, but we do NOT know its perpendicular height?
The Greek mathematician and engineer Heron of Alexandria (c. 10–70 CE) published his timeless formula in his book Metrica:

Heron's Formula

If a triangle has side lengths a, b, c, we first define its semi-perimeter s:

s = (a + b + c) / 2
Then its area A is given by:
Area = √[ s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c) ]

Why 4 Side Lengths Alone CANNOT Determine a 4-gon's Area

Students often ask: "Can we find the area of any quadrilateral if we are given its four sides a, b, c, d?"
NO! Unlike a triangle (which is rigid by SSS congruence), a quadrilateral made of 4 hinged rods can flex into infinitely many different shapes. The area is maximal when all four vertices lie on a circle (cyclic quadrilateral, Brahmagupta's formula). For a general quadrilateral with opposite angles α and γ, Bretschneider's Formula proves:
Area = √[ (s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d) - abcd cos²((α + γ)/2) ].
Only when α + γ = 180° (a cyclic quadrilateral) does cos(90°) = 0, maximizing the area to Brahmagupta's formula!

The classic mathematical problem of "Squaring a Rectangle" asks:
Given a rectangle ABCD of length a and width b (where a > b), construct a square that has the EXACT SAME AREA (Area = ab).

How to Square a Given Triangle

To square any given triangle T with base b and height h:
1. First construct a rectangle R with length h and width b/2. Its area is h × (b/2) = ½ bh = Area(T).
2. Now apply Baudhāyana’s squaring construction to rectangle R! The resulting square has area exactly equal to the triangle.

6.10 Area of a Circle: Archimedes' Proof & Nīlakaṇṭha's Sector Rearrangement

Just as the perimeter-to-diameter ratio C / D is constant for all circles, early societies observed that the ratio of Perimeter² to Area (P² / A) is an invariant signature for any geometric shape under scaling:

  • Square (side a): P² / A = (4a)² / a² = 16a² / a² = 16 : 1 (constant for all squares).
  • Equilateral Triangle (side a): P² / A = (3a)² / [(√3/4)a²] = 9a² / [(√3/4)a²] = 36 / √3 ≈ 20.78 : 1.
  • Circle: C² / A = (2πr)² / (πr²) = 4π²r² / πr² = 4π ≈ 12.566 : 1.

Notice that 12.566 < 16 < 20.78! The circle has the lowest ratio of boundary to enclosed area in mathematics (the famous Isoperimetric Theorem — a circle encloses the greatest possible area for a given perimeter!).

Ancient Circle Area Formulas

  • Babylonians (before 1500 BCE): Approximated C² / A ≈ 12, giving A ≈ C² / 12.
  • Ancient Egyptians (Rhind Papyrus, c. 1550 BCE): Used A ≈ [ (8/9) d ]² = (64/81) d² = (64/81)(4r²) = (256/81) r² ≈ 3.1605 r².
  • Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra (800 BCE): Yielded an equivalent geometric approximation of 256 / 81 ≈ 3.1605 when constructing a circular altar of equal area to a square altar!

Think & Reflect (Page 1): Does a 200 m school track require a smaller stagger than the 400 m Olympic track for a 4 × 100 m relay race?
Step 1: Given Data & Objective

Compare the lane stagger required between adjacent lanes on a 200 m school track versus a 400 m Olympic track for a race involving full curved running.

Step 2: Formula & Underlying Theorem

For any two concentric circular arcs subtending angle θ with lane width w, the difference in arc length is:
Δ ℓ = (θ / 360°) × 2π(R + w) - (θ / 360°) × 2πR = (θ / 360°) × 2πw.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Calculation

1. In a 400 m track, a full lap has two semicircles, giving total curve angle θ = 360°. Stagger = 2πw.

Step 1: Given Data & Objective

Step 2: Formula & Underlying Theorem

In a regular inscribed hexagon, the central angle of each side is 360° / 6 = 60°. Since the two sides from the center are radii of length r, each triangle is equilateral. Thus, each side of the hexagon equals r.

4. Therefore, Circumference C > Perimeter of Hexagon = 3D.
5. Dividing both sides by D: C / D > 3 ⇒ π > 3.

The inequality is strict (>) because the curved boundary strictly encloses the straight chords.

Step 5: Final Concluding Answer

Because the straight perimeter of the inscribed hexagon is 3D and the circle's perimeter is strictly larger than the inscribed polygon, π = C / D > 3.

Step 2: Formula & Underlying Theorem

1. Inscribed Hexagon: Side a = r = 1. Perimeter = 6 × 1 = 6.

2. Circumscribed Hexagon: The circle of radius 1 is the incircle of this hexagon. The perpendicular distance from the center to each edge is r = 1.

tan(30°) = (half-side) / r ⇒ half-side = 1 × (1 / √3) = 1 / √3.

Perimeter of circumscribed hexagon = 6 × (2 / √3) = 12 / √3 = 4√3.
3. Since the circle lies completely inside the circumscribed hexagon:
2π < 4√3 ⇒ π < 2√3.

The inscribed perimeter gives 2π > 6 ⇒ π > 3; the circumscribed perimeter gives 2π < 4√3 ⇒ π < 2√3. Hence, 3 < π < 2√3.

Represent algebraic expansions as dissections of planar areas.

Step 2: Area Conservation Axiom

The total area of a whole figure is equal to the sum of the areas of its non-overlapping partitioned sub-regions.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Geometric Construction

1. Understanding (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² (Fig. 6.41):
A large square of side (a + b) has total area (a + b)².
Dividing each side into segments a and b partitions the square into 4 regions:
• One square of area a × a = a².
• Two rectangles of area a × b = ab each (total = 2ab).
• One square of area b × b = b².
Sum of parts = a² + 2ab + b².

2. Area Model for (a + b)(a - b) = a² - b²:
• Start with a square of side a (area = a²).
• Cut out a small square of side b from one corner (area = b²).
• The remaining area is an L-shaped gnomon of area a² - b².
• Cut this L-shape into two rectangles:
    Rectangle 1: dimensions a × (a - b).
    Rectangle 2: dimensions b × (a - b).
• Rotate Rectangle 2 and paste it end-to-end to Rectangle 1 along the edge of length (a - b).

• Area of this rectangle = (a + b)(a - b).
• Therefore, (a + b)(a - b) = a² - b²!

• Construct a square of side (a + b + c). Its total area is (a + b + c)².
• Divide each side into three segments of lengths a, b, c.

    • 3 corner/diagonal squares: a², b², and c².
    • 2 symmetric rectangles of area ab.
    • 2 symmetric rectangles of area bc.
    • 2 symmetric rectangles of area ca.

Step 4: Check

Algebraic multiplication confirms identical terms in both cases.

Step 5: Final Answer

Geometrically constructed and verified through spatial dissection.

Question 17 (LA 1 - 5M): Provide a complete, rigorous geometric proof of the circle area formula A = πr² using Archimedean sector dissection and the method of exhaustion.
Step 1: Circle Dissection into 2n Sectors [1 Mark]

Divide a circle of radius r into 2n congruent triangular-like sectors by radiating chords from centre O at equal central angles θ = 360° / (2n). Each sector has two straight radial edges of length r and a curved base arc of length s = (2πr) / (2n) = (πr) / n.

Step 2: Alternating Interlocking Rearrangement [1 Mark]

Lay out the 2n sectors alternately pointing up and pointing down, fitting together like interlocking cog teeth. Cut the last sector in half radially into two half-sectors (angle θ/2) and place one at each extreme end. The resulting figure forms an almost rectilinear rectangle.

Step 3: Base and Height Evaluation in the Limit [1.5 Marks]

• The top boundary consists of n arc bases, and the bottom boundary consists of n arc bases.
Total bottom base length = n × [ (πr) / n ] = πr (exactly half the circumference!).
• The vertical sides are formed by radial cuts of length r.
As n → ∞, each sector central angle θ → 0, the curved circular arc segments become indistinguishable from straight line segments, and the vertical edges become strictly perpendicular to the base.

Step 4: Exhaustion Squeeze Theorem [1 Mark]

By Archimedes' method of exhaustion:
For any regular inscribed polygon of k sides, Area(Inscribed) = ½ × Perimeter × apothem < Area(Circle).
For any circumscribed polygon, Area(Circumscribed) = ½ × Perimeter × r > Area(Circle).
As k → ∞, both perimeters converge to 2πr and the apothem converges to r.

Step 5: Final Conclusion [0.5 Mark]

Area of Circle = base × height = (πr) × r = πr² sq units. (Q.E.D.)

Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

Top 5 Exam Hall Student Blunders & How to Avoid Them

Trap 1: Forgetting the Straight Diameter in Semicircle & Sector Perimeter

The Blunder: When asked to calculate the perimeter of a semicircular protractor or a circular sector, writing Perimeter = πr or Perimeter = (θ/360°) × 2πr.
The Fix: Perimeter is the total boundary around the closed shape! For a semicircle, you must add the straight diameter: P = πr + 2r. For a sector of angle θ, you must add the two bounding radii: P = ℓ + 2r = (θ/360°)2πr + 2r.

Trap 2: Squaring the Diameter Instead of the Radius in Area Formulas

The Blunder: When given a problem stating "a circular pond has a diameter of 14 m", directly plugging 14 into πr² to get π(14)² = 616 m² (which is 4 times too large!).
The Fix: Always write down the radius first: r = D / 2 = 14 / 2 = 7 m, so Area = π(7)² = 154 m². Alternatively, use the diameter formula Area = ¼ πD².

Trap 3: The "Equal Perimeter Implies Equal Area" Fallacy

The Blunder: Assuming that if a wire of length 40 cm is bent into different shapes, all those shapes enclose the exact same area.
The Fix: Memorize the Isoperimetric Hierarchy: For a fixed boundary perimeter:
Area(Narrow Rectangle) < Area(Square) < Area(Regular Hexagon) < Area(Circle). A 40 cm wire yields 9 m² (for 19×1 rectangle), 100 m² (for 10×10 square), and ≈ 127.3 m² (for a circle). The circle is always the supreme area-enclosing shape!

Trap 4: Unit Inconsistency and Confusing Linear cm with Area cm²

The Blunder: Mixing meters and centimeters (e.g., radius in cm, speed in km/h, distance in m) without converting to a common standard unit before performing division.
The Fix: Convert all dimensions into the same SI or CGS unit at Step 1! Remember conversion factors:
• 1 m = 100 cm  |  1 m² = 10,000 cm² = 10⁴ cm²
• 1 hectare = 10,000 m²  |  1 km² = 1,000,000 m² = 100 hectares.

Trap 5: Ignoring Problem-Specific π Values (22/7 vs 3.14)

The Blunder: Using π = 22/7 when the question explicitly instructs "Take π = 3.14", leading to decimal mismatches that cost evaluation marks on board answer keys.
The Fix: Check the header instructions of every question! If the question specifies π = 3.14 or √3 = 1.732, use those exact decimals. If nothing is specified, use π = 22/7.

15 Golden High-Yield Revision Points

  1. Perimeter is the total linear distance around the outer boundary of any 2D planar figure. For a polygon of side lengths s₁, s₂, ..., s_n, P = ∑ s_i.
  2. For all circles of any radius r, the ratio of circumference to diameter is invariant: C / D = C / (2r) = π, where π ≈ 3.14159265... is an irrational transcendental number.
