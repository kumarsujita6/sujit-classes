The Algorithmic Paradigm: Overview & Execution Pipeline

At its core, an algorithm is not magic; it is an unambiguous, step-by-step recipe that takes well-defined inputs, processes them through a sequence of finite, deterministic basic operations, and terminates with a correct output. In this chapter, we transition from brute-force counting to algorithmic computation, observing how mathematical ingenuity shrinks computational effort from linear in value to linear in the number of digits.

Master Mind Map: Taxonomy of the Algorithmic Universe

1. Foundational Concept of Algorithms
  • Definition: A finite, ordered sequence of unambiguous instructions designed to solve a specific problem class.
  • Core Properties: Precision (each step is crystal-clear), Finiteness (must terminate), Input (0 or more values), Output (at least 1 result), and Effectiveness (basic steps are directly executable).
  • Primitive Assumption: Algorithms rely on basic atomic operations we already know how to perform (e.g. single-digit addition, checking divisibility).
2. Arithmetic & Positional Systems
  • Counting vs Addition: Counting dots scales linearly with the value of the number (adding 3347 + 2729 requires 6,076 dot counts ≈ 1 hr 40 min). Positional addition scales linearly with the number of digits (only 4 column operations ≈ 1 minute).
  • Place-Value Weighting: Numbers represented as sums of powers of 10. Aligning right-to-left ensures units add to units, tens to tens, and hundreds to hundreds.
  • Carry Invariance: When adding two base-10 numbers, the carry into any column can NEVER exceed 1 (since max sum = 9 + 9 + 1 = 19).
  • Crucial Step 5: If a final carry of 1 remains after exhausting all columns, writing it at the extreme left prevents catastrophic magnitude loss (e.g., 50 + 70 = 120, not 20).
3. Data Structures & State Variables
  • Data Structure: A structured format for organizing, storing, and managing data efficiently during algorithm execution.
  • Lists: Ordered collections denoted by square brackets (e.g., [1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18]). Inherent sorting enables instant retrieval of maximum common elements.
  • State Variables: Named memory holders (e.g. list-of-divisors, j, carry, most-recent-common-divisor) that allow intermediate values to be stored, updated, and referenced concisely.
4. The GCD Algorithmic Evolution (5 Paradigms)
  • Method 1 (Dual-List Naive Scan): Build full factor lists for m and n by testing all integers from 1 to m and 1 to n (total m + n checks), cross-compare to form common-divisors, select rightmost.
  • Method 2 (Joint Scan to max): Scan j from 1 to max(m, n) simultaneously, testing divisibility for both numbers (reduces checks to max(m, n)).
  • Method 3 (Bounded Scan to min): Observe that no common divisor can exceed the smaller number; scan j from 1 to min(m, n).
  • Method 4 (Single Variable Tracker): Eliminate lists entirely! Track only most-recent-common-divisor starting at 1; update whenever k divides both m and n. Space complexity drops from O(n) to O(1).
  • Method 5 (Reverse Scan from min down to 1): Scan backwards from min(m, n) downwards. The very FIRST number that divides both is guaranteed to be the GCD; terminate immediately!
5. Euclid's Subtraction Algorithm & Geometric Tiling
  • Reduction Principle: For m ≥ n, gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m - n).
  • Bidirectional Proof: If d divides m and n (m = ad, n = bd), then d divides m - n = (a - b)d. Conversely, if d divides n and m - n (n = xd, m - n = yd), then d divides n + (m - n) = m = (x + y)d.
  • Geometric Tiling: If a square tile of side d exactly covers rectangles of length m and n, it must exactly cover the difference segment m - n.
  • Linear Bottleneck: For inputs like gcd(2k + 1, 2), subtraction requires k steps (e.g. gcd(99, 2) takes 49 reductions), meaning effort still scales with the value rather than the digit length.
6. Āryabhaṭa's Modulo Division (Kuṭṭaka) & Modern Computing
  • The Modulo Leap: Repeated subtraction is simply division! Replace m - n with m mod n (the remainder when m is divided by n: m = qn + r, r = m mod n).
  • Logarithmic Convergence: gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m mod n). The example gcd(99, 2) collapses from 49 subtraction steps to just 2 division steps!
  • Continuous Long Division: Āryabhaṭa’s 499 CE Kuṭṭaka (pulveriser) method directly matches the modern Euclidean division algorithm.
  • Historical Heritage: Persian scholar Al-Khwārizmī (820 CE) translated Indian arithmetic into Arabic; European Latin translation Liber Algorismi gave birth to the word Algorithm.

2.1 What is an Algorithm? The Recipe of Mathematical Logic

Every single day, you follow step-by-step instructions without even thinking about it! When you tie your shoelaces, bake a cake, brush your teeth, or follow Google Maps directions to a friend’s birthday party, you are executing a sequence of actions in a specific order. If you put your shoes on before your socks, things go hilariously wrong! Order and clarity matter.

Formal Definition: Algorithm
An algorithm is a finite, step-by-step, unambiguous procedure designed to perform a specific calculation, solve a mathematical problem, or automate a reasoning task.

In computer science and mathematics, an algorithm must satisfy five foundational criteria first formulated systematically by computer scientist Donald Knuth:

Property Formal Requirement Everyday Analogy (Making a Cup of Tea)
1. Finiteness (ସସୀମତା) The algorithm must always terminate after a countable, finite number of steps. It cannot run into an infinite loop forever. You stop boiling water once it reaches 100°C; you do not boil it infinitely until the kitchen burns down!
2. Definiteness (ସ୍ପଷ୍ଟତା / ନିର୍ଦ୍ଦିଷ୍ଟତା) Every step must be precisely, unambiguously defined. There is no room for guesswork or vague intuition. Instead of saying "add some sugar", say "add exactly 5 grams of sugar".
3. Input (ପ୍ରଦତ୍ତ ତଥ୍ୟ) An algorithm takes zero or more well-defined inputs from a specified domain before execution begins. Water, tea leaves, milk, and sugar are the initial raw ingredients supplied.
4. Output (ଉତ୍ପାଦିତ ଫଳାଫଳ) An algorithm must produce at least one correct mathematical result or state change that answers the problem. A piping hot, aromatic cup of tea ready to be served!
5. Effectiveness (କାର୍ଯ୍ୟକ୍ଷମତା) Every elementary operation must be feasible and so basic that a person could in principle do it with paper and pencil in finite time. Stirring with a spoon is a doable primitive step; asking someone to "teleport heat directly into the cup" is not effective.

The Primitive Step Assumption: Notice something profound: every algorithm is built upon smaller, basic building blocks that we already assume we know how to do! For example, when we describe an addition algorithm, we assume you already know single-digit arithmetic (like 7 + 8 = 15). If we had to re-explain how to add 1 + 1 every time we added two large numbers, we would get lost in an endless spiral!

Imagine using unary dot counting to add modern numbers, like your school fees: 473 + 695! You would have to draw 473 dots, draw 695 dots, and count all 1,168 dots one by one! If you sneezed at dot number 842, you would have to wipe the sand and start all over again!

2.3 The Algebraic Architecture of Positional Addition with Carry

Why does adding individual columns right-to-left actually give the true sum of two numbers? Let us open the hood and look at the algebraic gears!

In the Indian decimal (base-10) place-value system, any positive integer is represented as a polynomial linear combination of powers of 10. For instance:

A = a₂·10² + a₁·10¹ + a₀·10⁰
B = b₂·10² + b₁·10¹ + b₀·10⁰

When we add A and B, the Commutative and Associative Laws of Addition permit us to group like powers of 10 together:

A + B = (a₂ + b₂)·10² + (a₁ + b₁)·10¹ + (a₀ + b₀)·10⁰

Now consider what happens in the units column (10⁰). Suppose a₀ + b₀ = 10·c₁ + s₀, where s₀ is the remainder (0 ≤ s₀ ≤ 9) and c₁ is the carry to the tens place (c₁ ∈ {0, 1}).

Substituting this into our equation:

(a₀ + b₀)·10⁰ = (10·c₁ + s₀)·1 = c₁·10¹ + s₀·10⁰

Notice how the term c₁·10¹ has the exact same place-value weight as the tens column! We can seamlessly absorb c₁ into the tens column:

Tens term = (a₁ + b₁ + c₁)·10¹

This identical regrouping cascades column by column, from right to left!

Theorem: Carry Invariance for Two Numbers
Claim: In the addition of any two base-10 numbers, the carry generated at any column can NEVER exceed 1.

Formal Mathematical Proof:
1. The digits aᵢ and bᵢ are base-10 digits, meaning aᵢ ≤ 9 and bᵢ ≤ 9.
2. Suppose the incoming carry from the right column is cᵢ ≤ 1.
3. The maximum possible total column sum is:
S_max = a_max + b_max + c_max = 9 + 9 + 1 = 19
4. Since 19 = 1·10 + 9, the outgoing carry is c_{i+1} = ⌊19 / 10⌋ = 1.
5. For the base case (units column), incoming carry c₀ = 0, so S ≤ 9 + 9 + 0 = 18, which yields c₁ = ⌊18/10⌋ = 1.
6. By mathematical induction, the carry into any column is strictly bounded: cᵢ ∈ {0, 1} for all i ≥ 0. Hence, carry can never be 2, 3, or more! ∎
The Mystery of Step 5: Why It Cannot Be Omitted
Step 5 states: "If the value of carry is 1, write 1 to the left of the bottom row."
Why is this step mandatory? Consider adding 473 + 695:
  • Units column: 3 + 5 = 8 (carry = 0)
  • Tens column: 7 + 9 + 0 = 16 (write 6, carry = 1)
  • Hundreds column: 4 + 6 + 1 = 11 (write 1, carry = 1)
  • Now there are no more digits on the left!
If we stopped here without Step 5, the answer recorded would be 168 instead of 1,168! An entire thousand would vanish into thin air! Step 5 captures the overflow from the highest-order column and expands the number of digits from 3 to 4.

2.4 The Greatest Common Divisor (GCD/HCF) Problem Formulation

Let us now explore one of the most famous problems in all of human mathematics: finding the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD), also called the Highest Common Factor (HCF).

Definition: Divisor and GCD
A natural number d is called a divisor (or factor) of n if n can be divided by d with zero remainder (n mod d = 0).

For two natural numbers m and n, their Greatest Common Divisor, denoted gcd(m, n), is the largest positive integer that divides both m and n without remainder.

How do we solve this problem directly from its basic definition? We break it down into three small, clear steps:

  1. Compute all the divisors of m and store them.
  2. Compute all the divisors of n and store them.
  3. Compare the two collections, filter out the common numbers, and pick the largest one!

Let us trace this for n = 18:

# Execution Trace for divisors(18)
Initially, list-of-divisors = []
j = 1: 18 mod 1 == 0 → list-of-divisors = [1]
j = 2: 18 mod 2 == 0 → list-of-divisors = [1, 2]
j = 3: 18 mod 3 == 0 → list-of-divisors = [1, 2, 3]
j = 4: 18 mod 4 != 0 → list-of-divisors = [1, 2, 3] (no change)
j = 5: 18 mod 5 != 0 → list-of-divisors = [1, 2, 3] (no change)
j = 6: 18 mod 6 == 0 → list-of-divisors = [1, 2, 3, 6]
j = 7..8: do not divide 18 (no change)
j = 9: 18 mod 9 == 0 → list-of-divisors = [1, 2, 3, 6, 9]
j = 10..17: do not divide 18 (no change)
j = 18: 18 mod 18 == 0 → list-of-divisors = [1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18]
# Final result: divisors(18) = [1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18] in ascending order!

2.5 Data Structures in Algorithmic Problem Solving

Notice how in Section 2.4, we did not just compute single numbers; we collected multiple numbers into an ordered sequence enclosed in square brackets: [1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18].

Definition: Data Structure
A data structure is a specialised format for organising, processing, retrieving, and storing data in a computer or mathematical algorithm. Examples include lists, arrays, stacks, queues, trees, and hash tables.

Why do we give names to intermediate values?
Imagine if we wrote an algorithm like this: "Find the divisors of the first number. Then find the divisors of the second number. Now take that thing we found earlier and compare it with the other thing..." It would be hopelessly confusing!

By creating clear variable names like divisors-of-m, divisors-of-n, and common-divisors, we create permanent mental "hooks" (memory registers) that allow us to express complex logical relationships concisely and unambiguously.

The Magic of Inherent Sorting in Lists
Because our algorithm tests candidate divisors j sequentially from 1 up to n, divisors are added to the list in strictly ascending order! As a direct consequence, the common-divisors list is also automatically sorted in increasing order. Therefore, to find the Greatest Common Divisor, we never need to search or sort—we simply pick the rightmost element of the list!

2.6 The Five Classical GCD Algorithms: Evolution & Optimization

One of the most thrilling aspects of mathematics is taking a correct but clumsy algorithm and steadily refining it into an elegant, high-speed masterpiece! Let us watch the five stages of GCD evolution:

Method 1: Dual-List Naive Scan (The Textbook Baseline)

Algorithm gcd_method1(m, n):
  1. divisors_m ← divisors(m)             # tests 1 to m (m checks)
  2. divisors_n ← divisors(n)             # tests 1 to n (n checks)
  3. common ← []
  4. For each x in divisors_m:
       If x in divisors_n: append x to common
  5. Return rightmost element of common

Cost: Requires m + n divisibility tests, plus storing two complete lists in memory, plus cross-checking elements.

Method 2: Joint Scan up to max(m, n)

Why scan from 1 to m, and then start all over again from 1 to n? Combine both scans into a single loop!

Algorithm gcd_method2(m, n):
  1. divisors_m ← [], divisors_n ← []
  2. For j = 1 to max(m, n):
       If j ≤ m and m mod j == 0: append j to divisors_m
       If j ≤ n and n mod j == 0: append j to divisors_n
  3. Cross-compare to form common and return rightmost.

Improvement: Checks drop from m + n down to max(m, n).

Method 3: Bounded Direct Common Scan up to min(m, n)

Can a common divisor of 12 and 18 ever be 15? Impossible! A common divisor cannot exceed the smaller number!

Algorithm gcd_method3(m, n):
  1. common ← []
  2. For j = 1 to min(m, n):
       If (m mod j == 0) and (n mod j == 0):
           append j to common
  3. Return rightmost element of common

Improvement: Loop stops at min(m, n), eliminating thousands of useless checks. No separate lists for m and n!

Method 4: Memory Elimination via Single-Variable Tracking

Why hoard earlier common divisors? When we find that 6 divides both numbers, who cares about 1, 2, and 3? They are no longer candidates for the greatest!

Algorithm gcd_method4(m, n):
  1. most_recent_common_divisor ← 1     # 1 divides every natural number
  2. For k = 2 to min(m, n):
       If (m mod k == 0) and (n mod k == 0):
           most_recent_common_divisor ← k
  3. Return most_recent_common_divisor

Improvement: Memory footprint collapses to O(1) space! No lists allocated at all.

Method 5: Reverse Scanning with Immediate Early Exit

If you are looking for the tallest student in a line arranged by height, do you start from the shortest student? No, you look from the back! Start scanning from min(m, n) down to 1!

Algorithm gcd_reverse_scan(m, n):
  1. For k = min(m, n) down to 1:
       If (m mod k == 0) and (n mod k == 0):
           Return k                 # Immediate termination!

Improvement: For pairs like gcd(1000, 500), this terminates in the very first step (k = 500), avoiding 499 useless calculations!

2.7 Algorithmic Efficiency: The Digit-Scaling vs Value-Scaling Bottleneck

Even with all our clever optimizations in Section 2.6 (Method 4 and Method 5), we still face a severe mathematical hurdle. In the worst case—such as when two large numbers are coprime (gcd(m, n) = 1)—the algorithm must test all integers from min(m, n) all the way down to 1!

If min(m, n) = 1,000,000,000 (a 10-digit number common in modern bank balances and cryptography), Method 4 will execute one billion iterations!

Remember the triumph of base-10 addition: its effort scaled with the number of digits (e.g., 10 columns for a 10-digit number), not the numerical magnitude!

Can we find an algorithm for the Greatest Common Divisor where the effort scales with the number of digits (proportional to log n) rather than the value itself?

The answer is an astounding YES! And the journey begins with an ancient geometric insight.

2.8 Euclid’s Subtraction Algorithm & The Geometric Tiling Proof

Around 300 BCE, the Greek geometer Euclid recorded a brilliant insight in Book VII of his famous work Elements (though historians believe earlier Greek and Babylonian mathematicians also practiced variants).

Fundamental Lemma of Euclid's Subtraction Algorithm
Let m and n be two positive integers with m ≥ n. Then:
gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m - n)

Why is this true? The Complete Bidirectional Proof:
To prove that gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m - n), we must prove that the set of common divisors of {m, n} is identical to the set of common divisors of {n, m - n}. We prove this in two directions:

Direction 1 (Forward): Every common divisor of m and n also divides m - n.

Let d be any common divisor of m and n. By definition of divisibility, there exist natural numbers a and b such that:

m = a·d     and     n = b·d

Subtracting n from m:

m - n = a·d - b·d = (a - b)·d

Since m ≥ n, a ≥ b, so (a - b) is a non-negative integer. Therefore, d divides m - n! Thus, d is a common divisor of n and m - n.

Direction 2 (Converse): Every common divisor of n and m - n also divides m.

Conversely, let d be any common divisor of n and m - n. By definition, there exist natural numbers x and y such that:

n = x·d     and     m - n = y·d

Adding these two equations together:

m = n + (m - n) = x·d + y·d = (x + y)·d

Since x and y are natural numbers, (x + y) is a natural number. Therefore, d divides m! Thus, d is also a common divisor of m and n.

Conclusion: The two pairs (m, n) and (n, m - n) share the exact same set of common divisors! Consequently, their greatest common divisors must be identical: gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m - n). ∎

12. 75 - 75 = 0 → reduce to gcd(75, 0)

The Subtraction Trap: The Terrible Example of gcd(99, 2)
Look what happens if we feed gcd(99, 2) into Euclid’s subtraction algorithm:
gcd(99, 2) → gcd(97, 2) → gcd(95, 2) → gcd(93, 2) → ... → gcd(3, 2) → gcd(2, 1) → gcd(1, 0) = 1
Because 2 is so small, we subtract 2 over and over again 49 agonizing times! In general, for any odd number 2k + 1, computing gcd(2k + 1, 2) takes exactly k subtraction steps. If we calculated gcd(1,000,000,001, 2), it would take 500 million subtraction steps! We are right back to the value-scaling trap!

2.9 Āryabhaṭa’s Modulo Division Algorithm (Kuṭṭaka)

How do we escape the subtraction trap? The breakthrough came from ancient India! In 499 CE, the legendary astronomer and mathematician Āryabhaṭa I introduced the Kuṭṭaka (meaning "pulveriser" or continuous division method) in his masterwork Āryabhaṭīya.

Āryabhaṭa made an astonishingly simple observation: What is repeated subtraction? Repeated subtraction is simply DIVISION!

When you start with 99 and subtract 2 repeatedly, the subtraction stops when the remaining number is smaller than 2. That final remainder is simply 99 mod 2 = 1!

The Āryabhaṭa Modulo Reduction Theorem
Let m ≥ n > 0. By the Division Algorithm, m = q·n + r, where q = ⌊m / n⌋ is the integer quotient and r = m mod n is the remainder (0 ≤ r < n). Then:
gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m mod n)

Formal Verification of the Modulo Lemma:
1. Let d divide both m and n. Then m = a·d and n = b·d.
2. The remainder is r = m - q·n. Substituting:

r = a·d - q·(b·d) = (a - q·b)·d
3. Since a, q, b are integers, (a - q·b) is an integer. Hence d divides r = m mod n.
4. Conversely, let d divide both n and r. Then n = x·d and r = y·d.
5. Then m = q·n + r = q·(x·d) + y·d = (q·x + y)·d. Thus d divides m!
6. Both pairs have identical common divisors: gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m mod n). ∎

The Miraculous Speed of Āryabhaṭa’s Modulo Algorithm

Look at how the terrible example gcd(99, 2) performs under the modulo algorithm:

# Modulo reduction for gcd(99, 2)
Step 1: 99 mod 2 = 1 → reduce to gcd(2, 1)
Step 2: 2 mod 1 = 0 → reduce to gcd(1, 0)
Step 3: Since n = 0, STOP & return 1!
# Total: JUST 2 STEPS instead of 49 steps! A 2,450% efficiency leap!

2.10 Advanced Algorithmic Extensions: Primality Testing & Factorisation

Can we apply our algorithmic framework to investigate the building blocks of all numbers: Prime Numbers?

1. The Primality Testing Algorithm: prime(n)

A natural number p > 1 is prime if its only positive divisors are 1 and p. Thus, a number is prime if and only if the size (length) of its divisor list is exactly 2:

Algorithm prime_naive(n):
  1. If n ≤ 1: Return False                 # 1 is neither prime nor composite
  2. divs ← divisors(n)                     # computes all divisors
  3. If length(divs) == 2: Return True
  4. Else: Return False
Optimization Theorem: The Square-Root Bound for Primality
Theorem: If a natural number n > 1 is composite, it must have at least one prime factor p ≤ √n.

Proof:
1. Since n is composite, n = a·b where 1 < a ≤ b < n.
2. Suppose both a > √n and b > √n.
3. Multiplying these inequalities: a·b > √n · √n = n, which gives n > n, an obvious contradiction!
4. Therefore, at least one factor a ≤ √n.
5. If a is composite, by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic it has a prime factor p ≤ a ≤ √n.
6. Hence, to check if n is prime, we ONLY need to test candidate divisors up to ⌊√n⌋! ∎

Dramatic real-world effect: To test if 1,000,003 is prime:

  • Naive trial tests 1,000,003 numbers.
  • Square-root trial tests only up to √1000003 ≈ 1000 numbers! A 1,000× speedup!

2. Prime Factorisation Representation

According to the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, every integer n > 1 can be uniquely factored into prime powers:

n = p₁^{a₁} × p₂^{a₂} × ... × p_k^{a_k}

For instance, 180 = 2² × 3² × 5¹. In computational data structures, this can be represented as:

  • List of pairs: [[2, 2], [3, 2], [5, 1]]
  • Dictionary / Hash Map: {2: 2, 3: 2, 5: 1}

Comparing Prime Factorisations for GCD and LCM:
Given prime factorisations:

gcd(m, n) = p₁^{min(a₁, b₁)} × p₂^{min(a₂, b₂)} × ...
lcm(m, n) = p₁^{max(a₁, b₁)} × p₂^{max(a₂, b₂)} × ...

Notice that for any exponents: min(a, b) + max(a, b) = a + b! This instantly yields the famous invariant law:

gcd(m, n) × lcm(m, n) = m × n

2.11 A Pinch of History: Indian Roots and the Etymology of "Algorithm"

Where did the word "algorithm" actually come from? The story is a breathtaking saga connecting ancient Indian mathematics, the Golden Age of Islamic scholarship in Baghdad, and medieval Europe!

In the early 9th century, the great Persian mathematician and astronomer Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī (c. 780–850 CE) worked at the famed Bayt al-Ḥikma (House of Wisdom) in Baghdad. He was deeply fascinated by Indian mathematics (Ḥisāb al-Hind). He learned Sanskrit and around 820 CE wrote a revolutionary treatise:

Al-Khwārizmī's Masterpiece
Kitāb al-Jamʿ wa-l-Tafrīq bi-Ḥisāb al-Hind ("The Book of Addition and Subtraction According to the Hindu Calculation").

In this book, Al-Khwārizmī systematically explained the Indian decimal place-value numerals (0 through 9) and the Indian algorithms for the four fundamental arithmetic operations: addition with carry, subtraction with borrowing, multi-digit multiplication, and long division.

While the original Arabic manuscript was lost, in the 12th century it was translated into Latin as Liber Algorismi de numero Indorum ("The Book of Al-Khwārizmī on the Numbers of the Indians").

The Latin scholars Latinized his name Al-Khwārizmī as Algoritmi. Over time, European scholars began calling any calculation using the Indian numerals an algorismus. This contracted into algorism, and eventually evolved into our modern English word algorithm!

The Etymological Heritage
Etymologically, then, the word algorithm originally meant "an Indian method of arithmetic"! Only in the 20th century, with the birth of computer science through Alan Turing, did it expand to mean any well-defined, systematic computational procedure!

Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
Detailed Explanation: The maximum column sum is 19. Since 19 = 1 × 10 + 9, the outgoing carry is ⌊19/10⌋ = 1. To get a carry of 2, the column sum would have to be at least 20, which is impossible with two base-10 digits. Thus Reason R perfectly explains Assertion A.

Answer: (b) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
Detailed Explanation: Both statements are mathematically true. The paired divisor property (R) is true, but it is not the logical cause of the early termination property of the downward scan.
A/R Question 3: Worst-Case Complexity of Euclid's Subtraction Algorithm

Reason (R): For inputs of the form gcd(2k + 1, 2), Euclid's subtraction algorithm requires k successive reduction steps.

Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
Detailed Explanation: For gcd(2k + 1, 2), the algorithm subtracts 2 at each step: (2k - 1, 2), (2k - 3, 2), ..., taking exactly k steps. Since k is proportional to the numerical magnitude N = 2k + 1, the number of operations is linear in the value (O(N)), not in the digit count (O(log N)). Thus R provides the exact mathematical justification for A.
A/R Question 4: Primality Testing up to Square Root

Assertion (A): To determine whether a number n is prime, it is sufficient to test whether any prime number less than or equal to √n divides n.
Reason (R): Every composite number n can be factored as n = a × b where both factors a and b must be strictly greater than √n.

Answer: (c) A is true, but R is false.
Detailed Explanation: Assertion A is completely true. But Reason R is mathematically false! If both a > √n and b > √n, their product would be a × b > √n × √n = n, which contradicts n = a × b! In fact, at least one factor MUST be less than or equal to √n.

Assertion (A): Āryabhaṭa’s modulo division algorithm computes the GCD of two numbers in a number of steps that is proportional to the number of digits in the inputs.

Detailed Explanation: Because r < n and in fact r < m/2 after at most two steps, the product of the two numbers shrinks by at least half every two steps. This geometric decrease guarantees that the total number of divisions is at most 5 times the number of decimal digits in the smaller number (Lamé\'s Theorem). R is the direct reason for A.

LA 2 (5M): Formal Mathematical Proof of the Modulo Reduction Invariant: gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m mod n)
Theorem Statement: Let m and n be positive integers with m ≥ n > 0. Let r = m mod n be the remainder obtained upon dividing m by n. Prove rigorously that:

Step 1: Division Lemma Formulation
By Euclid’s Division Lemma, for any positive integers m and n, there exist unique integers q (quotient) and r (remainder) such that: m = q · n + r, where 0 ≤ r < n.
Rearranging this fundamental identity gives the remainder explicitly as: r = m - q · n.

Step 2: Forward Implication Proof (Every common divisor of {m, n} divides {n, r})
• Let d be any common divisor of m and n. This means d | m and d | n.
• By definition of divisibility, there exist integers a and b such that m = a · d and n = b · d.
• Substitute these expressions into the rearranged remainder equation: r = m - q · n = (a · d) - q · (b · d) = (a - q · b) · d.
• Since a, b, q are integers, the expression (a - q · b) is strictly an integer.
• Therefore, d divides r. Since d divides both n and r, d is a common divisor of the pair {n, m mod n}.

Step 3: Converse Implication Proof (Every common divisor of {n, r} divides {m, n})
• Conversely, let c be any common divisor of n and r. This means c | n and c | r.
• By definition, there exist integers x and y such that n = x · c and r = y · c.
• Substitute these into the division lemma equation: m = q · n + r = q · (x · c) + (y · c) = (q · x + y) · c.
• Since q, x, y are integers, (q · x + y) is strictly an integer.
• Therefore, c divides m. Combined with c | n, c is a common divisor of the pair {m, n}.

Step 4: Divisor Set Equivalence & Geometric Tiling Justification
• Steps 2 and 3 establish a two-way logical equivalence (d | m and d | n ⇔ d | n and d | (m mod n)).
• Consequently, the entire set of common divisors is identical: CD(m, n) = CD(n, m mod n).
• Because the two sets are completely identical, their greatest members must coincide: max(CD(m, n)) = max(CD(n, m mod n)) ⇒ gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m mod n). ∎
• Geometric Tiling Intuition: A square tile of edge length d tiles a line of length m and a line of length n without overlap or gaps if and only if it tiles the leftover segment of length r = m - q · n after laying down q complete blocks of length n.

Therefore, gcd(1050, 420) = 210 cm.
The maximum tile size is a square of 210 cm × 210 cm.

PYQ 1 (2M): Invariant Product Formula Extension to Three Numbers: Does gcd(a,b,c) × lcm(a,b,c) = a × b × c hold?

Question: The formula gcd(a, b) × lcm(a, b) = a × b is universally valid for any two natural numbers. Does the relation gcd(a, b, c) × lcm(a, b, c) = a × b × c hold for three natural numbers? Justify your answer with a counterexample.

Answer: NO, the formula does NOT hold for three numbers!
Counterexample: Let a = 2, b = 4, c = 8.
1. Compute product of numbers: a × b × c = 2 × 4 × 8 = 64.
2. Compute GCD: The greatest common factor dividing 2, 4, and 8 is gcd(2, 4, 8) = 2.
3. Compute LCM: The least common multiple of 2, 4, and 8 is lcm(2, 4, 8) = 8.
4. Multiply GCD and LCM: gcd(a, b, c) × lcm(a, b, c) = 2 × 8 = 16.
5. Comparison: 16 ≠ 64! In fact, gcd × lcm < a × b × c.
Mathematical Reason: For three numbers, common prime factors shared between subsets of two numbers are counted with different multiplicities in a × b × c compared to min and max prime powers.
PYQ 2 (2M): Well-Ordering Principle & Guaranteed Finite Termination of Āryabhaṭa’s Algorithm

Question: Why is Āryabhaṭa’s division modulo algorithm guaranteed to terminate in a finite number of steps for any two positive integers m and n?

Model Answer:
Āryabhaṭa’s algorithm generates a sequence of non-negative integer remainders:
r_0 = n > r_1 > r_2 > r_3 > … ≥ 0
1. Strict Monotonic Decrease: By definition of division by remainder, each remainder strictly satisfies 0 ≤ r_{k+1} < r_k.
2. Finite Descent Property: Any strictly decreasing sequence of non-negative integers must reach zero in at most n steps (by the Well-Ordering Principle of the natural numbers).
3. Definite Stopping Condition: The moment a remainder r_k = 0 is reached, the algorithm terminates immediately and returns r_{k-1}. An infinite loop is mathematically impossible!
PYQ 3 (3M): Algorithmic Search Problem: The Ancient Remainder Riddle

Question: A basket contains a certain number of eggs. When eggs are removed in pairs (2 at a time), 1 egg remains. When removed 3 at a time, 2 remain. When removed 5 at a time, 4 remain. But when removed 7 at a time, exactly 0 remain (no eggs left). Design an algorithmic search procedure to find the smallest number of eggs in the basket, and calculate the answer.

Step-by-Step Solution:
1. Mathematical Formulation: Let n be the number of eggs. We are given:
• n mod 2 = 1 (meaning n is odd)
• n mod 3 = 2 ⇒ n + 1 is divisible by 3
• n mod 5 = 4 ⇒ n + 1 is divisible by 5
• n mod 7 = 0 ⇒ n is a multiple of 7.

2. Algorithmic Search Strategy:
Instead of testing all numbers from 1 upwards, we search exclusively through positive multiples of 7: n = 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, …
At each candidate, test if n mod 2 == 1, n mod 3 == 2, and n mod 5 == 4.

3. Execution Trace:
• n = 7: 7 mod 3 = 1 ≠ 2 (Fail)
• n = 14: Even (Fail)
• n = 49: 49 mod 5 = 4, but 49 mod 3 = 1 ≠ 2 (Fail)
• Notice that n mod 2 = 1, n mod 3 = 2, and n mod 5 = 4 implies n + 1 is a multiple of lcm(2, 3, 5) = 30.
• Therefore, n must be of the form 30k - 1 for integer k:
For k = 1 ⇒ n = 29: 29 mod 7 = 1 ≠ 0
For k = 2 ⇒ n = 59: 59 mod 7 = 3 ≠ 0
For k = 3 ⇒ n = 89: 89 mod 7 = 5 ≠ 0
For k = 4 ⇒ n = 119: 119 / 7 = 17 exactly! (119 mod 7 = 0).

4. Verification:
• 119 = 59 × 2 + 1 ✓
• 119 = 39 × 3 + 2 ✓
• 119 = 23 × 5 + 4 ✓
• 119 = 17 × 7 + 0 ✓.
The minimum number of eggs is 119.
PYQ 4 (3M): Generalized Carry Invariant: Adding m Numbers in Base b Produces Carry ≤ m - 1

Question:
(i) In base 10, prove that when adding m single-digit numbers, the carry passed to the next higher column can never exceed m - 1.
(ii) In the binary system (base 2), what is the maximum carry when adding two binary digits with an incoming carry?

Complete Mathematical Proof:
Part (i): Mathematical Induction on Column Index
• In base 10, each of the m digits satisfies 0 ≤ d_i ≤ 9.
• Let the incoming carry from the previous column be C_{in} ≤ m - 1.
• The maximum possible column sum S is:
S_{max} = (m × 9) + C_{in} ≤ 9m + (m - 1) = 10m - 1.
• The outgoing carry passed to the next column is the integer quotient when divided by 10:
C_{out} = ⌊S_{max} / 10⌋ ≤ ⌊(10m - 1) / 10⌋ = ⌊m - (1/10)⌋ = m - 1.
• Thus, by induction, the carry is strictly bounded by m - 1 for every column! For m = 2 numbers, carry ≤ 2 - 1 = 1. For m = 3 numbers, carry ≤ 3 - 1 = 2. ∎

Part (ii): Binary Addition Carry Bound
• In base 2, valid digits are {0, 1}.
• Sum of two digits plus max incoming carry of 1 is: 1 + 1 + 1 = 3.
• In binary, 3 = 1 × 2¹ + 1 × 2⁰ (written as binary 11).
• The outgoing carry is ⌊3 / 2⌋ = 1.
• Therefore, the binary carry is also strictly bounded by 1.
PYQ 5 (3M): Large Number Modulo Reduction: Compute gcd(13500, 3150) and lcm(13500, 3150)

Question: Apply Āryabhaṭa’s division modulo algorithm to calculate gcd(13500, 3150). Show every intermediate division step as a division lemma equation, and subsequently calculate lcm(13500, 3150).

Step-by-Step Solution:
1. Division Steps:
• Step 1: Divide 13,500 by 3,150:
13500 = 4 × 3150 + 900 (Quotient = 4, Remainder = 900).
Reduce to gcd(3150, 900).

• Step 2: Divide 3,150 by 900:
3150 = 3 × 900 + 450 (Quotient = 3, Remainder = 450).
Reduce to gcd(900, 450).

• Step 3: Divide 900 by 450:
900 = 2 × 450 + 0 (Quotient = 2, Remainder = 0).
Reduce to gcd(450, 0).

• Termination: Since remainder is 0, the last non-zero remainder is the GCD:
gcd(13500, 3150) = 450.

2. LCM Calculation:
Using lcm(a, b) = (a × b) / gcd(a, b):
lcm(13500, 3150) = (13500 × 3150) / 450 = 13500 × 7 = 94,500.

3. Verification:
450 × 94,500 = 42,525,000.
13,500 × 3,150 = 42,525,000. Perfectly verified!

6.1 Top 5 Exam Hall Blunders (Trap Alerts)

Trap Alert 1: Writing Carry = 2 in Two-Number Addition

The Blunder: When adding two large multi-digit numbers, students sometimes write an outgoing carry of 2 or 3 in long column calculations.
The Mathematical Reality: The maximum sum of any two single digits is 9 + 9 = 18. Even with a maximum incoming carry of 1, the total column sum can never exceed 9 + 9 + 1 = 19. Dividing by 10 gives ⌊19/10⌋ = 1. A carry of 2 requires a sum of at least 20, which is physically impossible with two base-10 digits.

Trap Alert 2: Scanning up to n/2 instead of √n for Primality Testing

The Blunder: When testing whether a number n (e.g., 221) is prime, students write algorithms that loop up to n/2 = 110.
The Mathematical Reality: Factors always occur in complementary pairs (d, n/d). If both factors were strictly greater than √n, their product would exceed n! Therefore, at least one factor MUST be ≤ √n. For n = 221, √221 ≈ 14.86. You only need to test primes up to 14 (i.e. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13). Testing up to 110 wastes 90% of computational effort!

Trap Alert 3: Confusing Euclid’s Subtraction with Āryabhaṭa’s Modulo Algorithm

The Blunder: Claiming that Euclid’s subtraction algorithm runs in logarithmic time or has the same step count as Āryabhaṭa’s method.
The Mathematical Reality: Euclid’s subtraction reduces by m - n, which takes k steps for gcd(2k + 1, 2) (proportional to numerical value). Āryabhaṭa’s algorithm reduces by m mod n, which takes only 2 steps (proportional to the number of digits). They are fundamentally distinct in computational complexity!

Trap Alert 4: Forgetting the Stopping Condition and Reporting 0 as the GCD

The Blunder: When reaching the remainder r = 0 in long division, students write gcd = 0 or stop before writing the base case.
The Mathematical Reality: The stopping condition states: If n = 0, report m. The GCD is the last non-zero remainder (or the final non-zero divisor). GCD can never be 0 for positive natural numbers!

Trap Alert 5: Applying the Two-Number Product Formula to Three Numbers

The Blunder: Assuming that gcd(a, b, c) × lcm(a, b, c) = a × b × c.
The Mathematical Reality: This formula holds ONLY for two numbers! For three numbers a = 2, b = 4, c = 8, gcd(2, 4, 8) = 2 and lcm(2, 4, 8) = 8. Their product is 2 × 8 = 16, whereas a × b × c = 2 × 4 × 8 = 64! Never use this formula for 3 numbers in board exams.

6.2 15 Rapid Revision Flash Points (High-Yield Exam Cram Sheet)

1. Algorithm Definition: A finite, deterministic, step-by-step procedure of primitive operations that transforms inputs to outputs.
2. Core Criteria: Every valid algorithm must satisfy Finiteness, Definiteness, Input, Output, and Effectiveness.
3. Place-Value Shift: Aligning base-10 numbers right-to-left ensures that digits sharing the exact same power of 10 (10ᵏ) are added together.
4. Addition Carry Bound: Adding two base-10 numbers produces a carry of at most 1, because 9 + 9 + 1 = 19 < 20.
5. Overflow Step: Step 5 of the addition algorithm dynamically expands the number of digits (k → k + 1) when the leftmost column produces a carry.
6. Data Structure Role: A sorted list allows retrieving the maximum common divisor in O(1) time simply by inspecting the rightmost index.
7. Paired Divisors: Divisors always occur in complementary pairs (d, n/d). The smaller divisor is strictly bounded by √n.
8. Search Space Reduction: Searching divisors up to √n reduces division operations from n to √n (e.g., 10,000 divisions down to 100).
9. Memory Optimization: Replacing divisor lists with a single variable most-recent-common-divisor achieves O(1) auxiliary space.
10. Euclid’s Subtraction Principle: gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m - n). Geometrically, any tile measuring m and n also tiles their difference m - n.
11. Subtraction Flaw: On inputs like gcd(2k + 1, 2), Euclid’s subtraction takes k steps, scaling linearly with the value rather than the digits.
12. Āryabhaṭa’s Modulo Kuṭṭaka: Replacing subtraction with remainder division gcd(n, m mod n) shrinks numbers exponentially fast in O(log N) steps.
13. GCD-LCM Invariant: For any two positive integers a and b, gcd(a, b) × lcm(a, b) = a × b.
14. Prime Power Synthesis: In prime factorisation, GCD takes the minimum exponent for each prime factor; LCM takes the maximum exponent.
15. Historical Etymology: The word algorithm originates from Algoritmi, the Latinized name of Al-Khwārizmī, who popularized Indian decimal arithmetic worldwide in 820 CE.
