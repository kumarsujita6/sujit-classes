2.1 The Grand Transition: From Concrete Arithmetic to Symbolic Algebra

Think back to your earliest mathematics classes. You learned to count pebbles, measure lengths with rulers, and perform arithmetic operations on definite numbers:

5 + 4 = 9,     12 × 3 = 36,     100 - 45 = 55

In arithmetic, every number is concrete, rigid, and fixed. The number 5 always means exactly five units—never four, never six. But what happens when you want to state a truth that applies to every possible number? Or what happens when an auto-rickshaw driver charges you a base fare plus a fixed charge for every kilometer traveled, where the distance traveled is not yet known before the journey starts?

This is where Algebra steps onto the stage. Algebra is generalized arithmetic. Instead of working only with specific numbers, we use letters of the alphabet (such as x, y, z, or a, b, c) to stand for unknown quantities or variables that can take many different numerical values.

Historical Heritage: Bharatiya Ganita and the Invention of Unknowns

India holds a glorious place in the world history of algebra. In ancient Indian mathematics, scholars made a fundamental philosophical and practical distinction:

  • Vyakta-gaṇita (ବ୍ୟକ୍ତ ଗଣିତ): The mathematics of manifest, known, concrete numbers—what we call Arithmetic today. (From the Sanskrit root vyakta, meaning clearly visible, manifest, or expressed).
  • Avyakta-gaṇita (ଅବ୍ୟକ୍ତ ଗଣିତ): The mathematics of unmanifest, unknown, or hidden quantities—what we call Algebra today. (From avyakta, meaning unmanifest or hidden).

In 628 CE, the legendary Indian mathematician-astronomer Brahmagupta composed his masterwork, the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta. Brahmagupta was the first mathematician in history to systematically formulate algebraic equations using symbols for unknown quantities. He designated the primary unknown as Yāvattāvat (meaning "as many as" or "as much as"), abbreviated by the syllable yā. When an equation involved multiple unknown quantities, Brahmagupta ingeniously represented the other unknowns using names of distinct colors:

  • Kālaka (କାଳକ): Black (abbreviated kā)
  • Nīlaka (ନୀଳକ): Blue (abbreviated nī)
  • Pītaka (ପୀତକ): Yellow (abbreviated pī)
  • Lohitaka (ଲୋହିତକ): Red (abbreviated lo)

Five centuries later, in 1150 CE, the great astronomer Bhāskarācārya II (Bhāskara II) wrote his celebrated algebraic treatise titled Bījagaṇita (ବୀଜଗଣିତ). The word Bīja means "seed" or "element of cause"—algebra is the seed from which mathematical analysis grows! Bhāskara II established rigorous arithmetic rules for positive quantities (dhana or fortune) and negative quantities (ṛṇa or debt), and provided complete methods for solving linear and quadratic equations.

Later, in the 9th century, the Persian scholar Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala (from which the English word algebra derives, meaning "restoration of broken parts"). In Europe during the late 16th and 17th centuries, François Viète introduced vowel letters for unknowns and consonant letters for known parameters, until René Descartes in 1637 formalized our modern convention: using letters from the end of the alphabet (x, y, z) for variables and letters from the beginning (a, b, c) for constants.

2.2 Constants, Variables, Terms, and Coefficients

To construct a building, we need bricks, cement, and steel. Similarly, to construct algebraic polynomials, we need four fundamental architectural components:

1

Constant (ଧ୍ରୁବକ)

A symbol or number that possesses a fixed numerical value under all conditions. Examples include:
7,  -18,  3/5,  √2,  π ≈ 3.14159...
The value of a constant never changes throughout a mathematical problem.

2

Variable (ଚଳରାଶି)

A symbol (usually a letter such as x, y, z, t, u, v) that can be assigned varying numerical values. It represents an unknown quantity or an independent parameter that can change freely across its domain.

3

Term (ପଦ)

A single mathematical unit consisting of either a constant, a variable, or the product of a constant and variable powers. In an algebraic expression, terms are separated strictly by plus (+) or minus (-) signs.
Example: In 3x² - 5x + 8, the three terms are 3x², -5x, and 8.

4

Coefficient (ସହଗ)

The numerical multiplier attached to a variable term.
For instance, in the term -7x³, the coefficient of x³ is -7. If no number is visibly written, the coefficient is 1 (e.g., in x, coefficient is 1; in -x, coefficient is -1).

Memory Hack: The Taxi Cab Metaphor for Terms and Coefficients

Imagine stepping into an auto-rickshaw in Cuttack or Bhubaneswar. The meter displays:

Total Fare = (₹15 × Distance in km) + ₹30

Let d be the distance traveled in kilometers. The total fare is represented by the linear polynomial: F(d) = 15d + 30.

  • Variable (d): The distance you choose to travel. It changes for every trip (could be 2 km, 5.5 km, or 10 km).
  • Coefficient (15): The rate per kilometer. It multiplies the variable directly.
  • Constant (30): The base flag-down charge. You must pay this even before the wheels start rolling (at d = 0).

2.3 Rigorous Definition of a Polynomial in One Variable

Not every combination of numbers and letters qualifies to be called a polynomial. The word polynomial comes from Greek poly (meaning "many") and Latin nomen / binomial (meaning "term" or "name").

Formal Definition: Polynomial in One Variable

An algebraic expression in a single variable x is said to be a Polynomial in One Variable if and only if it can be written in the standard form:

p(x) = aₙ xⁿ + aₙ₋₁ xⁿ⁻¹ + aₙ₋₂ xⁿ⁻² + ... + a₂ x² + a₁ x + a₀

where:

  1. aₙ, aₙ₋₁, ..., a₁, a₀ are Real Numbers (called the coefficients of the polynomial).
  2. The leading coefficient aₙ ≠ 0 (when defining a polynomial of degree n).
  3. THE GOLDEN RULE: The exponents n, n-1, ..., 2, 1, 0 of the variable x MUST BE WHOLE NUMBERS (i.e., non-negative integers: 0, 1, 2, 3, ...).

The Non-Negative Integer Exponent Rule (Why Whole Numbers?)

Why do mathematicians enforce this strict rule that exponents cannot be negative or fractional? In advanced mathematics, polynomials must be well-behaved smooth functions defined for every single real number without division-by-zero errors or imaginary numbers from square roots of negatives.

Algebraic Expression Variable Powers Is it a Polynomial? Mathematical Reason / Justification
3x² - 5x + 7 2, 1, 0 YES All exponents (2, 1, 0) belong to whole numbers (𝕎). Coefficients (3, -5, 7) are real numbers.
√5 x + 9 1, 0 YES The variable x has exponent 1. The coefficient √5 is an irrational real number, which is 100% permitted!
x + 1/x 1, -1 NO Since 1/x = x⁻¹, the exponent is -1, which is a negative integer (not a whole number).
3√x + 5 1/2, 0 NO Since √x = x^(1/2), the exponent is 1/2 (fractional/rational number), not an integer.
y² + √2 2, 0 YES The variable y has exponent 2. The constant term is √2 = √2 y⁰. Valid polynomial in variable y.
5 / (x² + 3) Variable in denominator NO This is a rational algebraic expression. The variable cannot be freed from the denominator to form non-negative powers.
t³ - ∛t + 8 3, 1/3, 0 NO The term ∛t = t^(1/3) has exponent 1/3, which is not an integer.
0 No variable term YES This is the special Zero Polynomial. Its degree is not defined.
-11 0 YES This is a Constant Polynomial (-11 = -11 x⁰). Its degree is 0.
Danger Trap: Radical on Variable vs. Radical on Coefficient

Do not confuse a square root on a number with a square root on a variable:

  • √3 x + 5 ⟹ IS A POLYNOMIAL. The radical is over 3, not x. The variable power is 1.
  • 3√x + 5 ⟹ IS NOT A POLYNOMIAL. The radical is directly over x, giving x^(1/2).

2.4 Classification of Polynomials: Terms vs. Degree

Polynomials are classified through two independent lenses:

Lens A: Classification by Number of Non-Zero Terms

1. Monomial (ଏକପଦୀ ରାଶି):

A polynomial consisting of exactly one non-zero term.
Examples: 5x,  -8x³,  14,  (2/3)y².

2. Binomial (ଦ୍ୱିପଦୀ ରାଶି):

A polynomial consisting of exactly two non-zero terms.
Examples: 2x + 7,  y² - 16,  5x³ + 4x.

3. Trinomial (ତ୍ରିପଦୀ ରାଶି):

A polynomial consisting of exactly three non-zero terms.
Examples: x² + 5x + 6,  4y³ - 2y + 9.

Lens B: Classification by Degree (ଘାତ)

The Degree of a Polynomial is the highest exponent of the variable in any of its terms having a non-zero coefficient.

Type of Polynomial Degree Standard General Form Restrictions Concrete Example
Zero Polynomial (ଶୂନ ପଲିନୋମିଆଲ୍) UNDEFINED (Not Defined) p(x) = 0 No non-zero terms exist 0
Constant Polynomial (ଧ୍ରୁବକ ପଲିନୋମିଆଲ୍) 0 p(x) = c c ≠ 0 7 = 7x⁰,  -15,  π
★ LINEAR POLYNOMIAL (ରୈଖିକ ପଲିନୋମିଆଲ୍) ★ 1 p(x) = ax + b a ≠ 0, a, b ∈ ℝ 3x - 5,  -8x,  √2 x + 1
Quadratic Polynomial (ଦ୍ୱିଘାତୀ ପଲିନୋମିଆଲ୍) 2 p(x) = ax² + bx + c a ≠ 0 2x² + 7x - 4
Cubic Polynomial (ତ୍ରିଘାତୀ ପଲିନୋମିଆଲ୍) 3 p(x) = ax³ + bx² + cx + d a ≠ 0 x³ - 6x² + 11x - 6
Why is the Degree of the Zero Polynomial NOT Defined?

Consider any constant non-zero number, say 7. We can rewrite it unambiguously as:
7 = 7 × x⁰.
Since the power of x is zero and the coefficient 7 is non-zero, the degree of 7 is unambiguously 0.

Now consider the number 0. Notice that:

0 = 0·x⁰ = 0·x¹ = 0·x² = 0·x¹⁰ = 0·x¹⁰⁰⁰

Every power of x multiplied by 0 results in 0! There is no unique, well-defined highest exponent. Therefore, in formal mathematics, the degree of the zero polynomial is declared UNDEFINED (or Not Defined).

2.5 Deep Dive: Linear Polynomials in One Variable

Now we arrive at the heart of our chapter: Linear Polynomials.

Core Definition: Linear Polynomial in One Variable

A polynomial p(x) in one variable x is called a Linear Polynomial if its degree is exactly 1.

p(x) = ax + b,     where a, b ∈ ℝ and a ≠ 0

Why is the condition "a ≠ 0" absolutely non-negotiable?

Let us test what happens if someone sets a = 0:

p(x) = (0)·x + b = 0 + b = b

If a = 0, the variable term x disappears entirely! The polynomial collapses into p(x) = b, which is a constant polynomial of degree 0 (if b ≠ 0) or the zero polynomial (if b = 0). The polynomial completely loses its degree 1 status and is no longer linear! Therefore, the leading coefficient a must never be zero.

Two Structural Manifestations of a Linear Polynomial:

Case 1: When b = 0 (Monomial Linear Polynomial)

The polynomial takes the form p(x) = ax.
It has only 1 term (a monomial).
Its graph always passes through the origin (0, 0) because when x = 0, p(0) = a(0) = 0.
Examples: p(x) = 3x,  g(t) = -5t.

Case 2: When b ≠ 0 (Binomial Linear Polynomial)

The polynomial takes the form p(x) = ax + b.
It has 2 distinct terms (a binomial).
Its graph does not pass through the origin; instead, it intersects the y-axis at the vertical offset (0, b).
Examples: p(x) = 2x - 6,  h(u) = -4u + 12.

2.6 Value of a Polynomial vs. Zero of a Polynomial

A. Value of a Polynomial at x = k

If p(x) is a polynomial in variable x, and k is any real number, the value obtained by substituting x = k wherever x appears in p(x) is called the value of p(x) at x = k, and is denoted by p(k).

Step-by-Step Evaluation Example

Let p(x) = 4x - 12. Let us evaluate its value at three different inputs:

  • At x = 5:   p(5) = 4(5) - 12 = 20 - 12 = 8
  • At x = 0:   p(0) = 4(0) - 12 = 0 - 12 = -12
  • At x = 3:   p(3) = 4(3) - 12 = 12 - 12 = 0   (Notice this special result!)

B. Zero (or Root) of a Polynomial

Notice in the example above that when we plugged in x = 3, the entire output polynomial became exactly zero! This special input value is crowned with a prestigious title:

Definition: Zero of a Polynomial

A real number k is called a zero (or root) of a polynomial p(x) if:

p(k) = 0

In plain words: The zero of a polynomial is the numerical value of the variable that makes the polynomial equal to zero.

Rigorous Algebraic Derivation of the Zero of p(x) = ax + b

How do we find the zero of any arbitrary linear polynomial? Let us derive the general formula from first principles:

Let p(x) = ax + b, where a ≠ 0.

To find the zero, we set the polynomial equal to 0:
    p(x) = 0
    ax + b = 0

Subtracting b from both sides:
    ax = -b

Since a ≠ 0, we can safely divide both sides by a:
    x = -b / a
The Golden Universal Zero Formula for Linear Polynomials
Zero of (ax + b) = -b / a = - (Constant Term) / (Coefficient of x)

The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Polynomials:
Because a ≠ 0, the division -b/a yields one and only one unique real number. Therefore:
Every linear polynomial in one variable has EXACTLY ONE real zero!

Comparative Summary: Zeros of Different Polynomial Families

Polynomial Type Standard Form How Many Zeros Exist? Explanation & Behavior
Linear Polynomial p(x) = ax + b,   a ≠ 0 EXACTLY ONE (1) Unique Zero The unique zero is given by x = -b/a.
Constant Non-Zero Polynomial p(x) = c,   c ≠ 0 ZERO Zeros (NO Real Zero) Since c ≠ 0, p(x) can never equal 0 for any input x.
Zero Polynomial p(x) = 0 INFINITELY MANY Zeros Every real number k satisfies p(k) = 0!

2.7 Geometric Meaning of the Zero of a Linear Polynomial

Why do we call it a "linear" polynomial? The name itself gives away the magnificent secret: When we represent the algebraic relationship on a Cartesian coordinate plane by letting y = p(x), its visual picture is a STRAIGHT LINE!

How Geometry Reveals the Algebraic Zero

Consider the linear polynomial p(x) = 2x - 4. Let us define y = 2x - 4 and compute a table of values:

x y = 2x - 4 Coordinate Point (x, y)
0 2(0) - 4 = -4 (0, -4) ⟹ y-intercept
2 2(2) - 4 = 0 (2, 0) ⟹ x-intercept!
3 2(3) - 4 = 2 (3, 2)
-1 2(-1) - 4 = -6 (-1, -6)

Observe the point where y = 0. On the Cartesian coordinate plane, points where y = 0 lie precisely on the x-axis! Therefore, the straight line cuts across the x-axis at the point (2, 0).

The Grand Geometric Principle

The zero of a linear polynomial p(x) = ax + b is the x-coordinate (abscissa) of the point where the straight line y = ax + b intersects the x-axis!

Intersection Point on x-axis = (-b/a, 0)   ⟹   Zero = -b/a

2.8 The Bridge: From Linear Polynomials to Linear Equations

Students often ask: "What is the difference between an algebraic polynomial and an algebraic equation?" This distinction is vital for board exams:

Linear Polynomial: p(x) = ax + b

An algebraic expression without an equals sign (=). It does not assert an equality; it defines a rule or function that outputs a new value for every input value of x.
Example: p(x) = 3x - 15.

Linear Equation in 1 Variable: ax + b = 0

Formed by setting the polynomial equal to zero. It is a mathematical statement of equality that is true for only one specific value of x (namely the zero, x = -b/a).
Example: 3x - 15 = 0 ⟹ x = 5.

Linear Equation in 2 Variables: ax + by + c = 0

An equation involving two variables x and y where a² + b² ≠ 0. It possesses infinitely many solutions, each corresponding to an ordered pair (x, y) on the line.
Example: 2x + 3y - 6 = 0.

Special Lines Parallel to the Coordinate Axes:

  • Horizontal Line: y = k (or 0·x + 1·y - k = 0). Parallel to the x-axis. Every point on this line has the same y-coordinate k. Its slope is 0.
  • Vertical Line: x = h (or 1·x + 0·y - h = 0). Parallel to the y-axis. Every point on this line has the same x-coordinate h. Its slope is undefined.
  • Equation of the X-axis: Since every point on the x-axis has a y-coordinate of 0, the equation of the x-axis is y = 0.
  • Equation of the Y-axis: Since every point on the y-axis has an x-coordinate of 0, the equation of the y-axis is x = 0.

2.9 Real-World Mathematical Modeling with Linear Polynomials

Why do engineers, economists, and climate scientists use linear polynomials every single day? Because in countless natural and human-made systems, when one factor changes at a steady, uniform rate, the resulting relationship is purely linear!

Model 1: The Auto-Rickshaw Fare Structure (Economic Modeling)

In an urban transport system, an e-rickshaw charges a fixed flag-down fare of ₹25 for boarding, plus ₹12 for each kilometer traveled.
Let d be the distance traveled in km, and C(d) be the total fare in ₹.
The cost polynomial is: C(d) = 12d + 25.
Here, leading coefficient a = 12 represents the rate of fare accrual per km (slope), and constant term b = 25 represents the zero-distance flag-down fare (y-intercept).

Model 2: Temperature Scale Conversion (Physical Science Modeling)

The relationship between temperature in degrees Fahrenheit (F) and degrees Celsius (C) is governed by the famous linear polynomial:
F(C) = (9/5)C + 32 = 1.8C + 32.
• At water's freezing point (C = 0°C): F(0) = 1.8(0) + 32 = 32°F (y-intercept).
• At water's boiling point (C = 100°C): F(100) = 1.8(100) + 32 = 212°F.
• Zero of the polynomial F(C) = 0: (9/5)C + 32 = 0 ⟹ C = -160/9 ≈ -17.78°C (the temperature at 0°F).
• Unique temperature where Fahrenheit equals Celsius (F = C):
    C = (9/5)C + 32 ⟹ C - (9/5)C = 32 ⟹ -(4/5)C = 32 ⟹ C = -40°.

The "OPPOSITE OVER LEADING" Root Trick

To find the zero of ax + b in 2 seconds without writing equations:
1. Take the constant term b and flip its sign (take the Opposite: -b).
2. Divide it by the Leading coefficient a.
Example: For 5x - 35: Opposite of -35 is +35. Divide by 5 ⟹ 35 / 5 = 7!

5.4 Long Answer Questions (5 Marks Step-by-Step Marking Scheme)

LA 1 (5M) Universal Proof: Linear Polynomial ax + b has Exactly One Real Zero

Problem: State the general form of a linear polynomial in one variable. Rigorously prove that every non-zero linear polynomial has one and only one real zero. Contrast this property with that of a constant non-zero polynomial and the zero polynomial.

Marking Scheme Breakdown

1. General Definition (1 Mark):
A linear polynomial in one variable x is defined as p(x) = ax + b, where a, b ∈ ℝ and a ≠ 0. The degree is strictly 1.

2. Proof of Existence of Zero (1.5 Marks):
A real number k is a zero of p(x) if and only if p(k) = 0.
Setting ak + b = 0:
ak = -b.
Since a ≠ 0, we can divide both sides by a without violating the laws of arithmetic:
k = -b / a.
Since a, b ∈ ℝ and a ≠ 0, the quotient -b/a is guaranteed to be a unique real number. Thus, a zero always exists.

3. Proof of Uniqueness (1 Mark):
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there exist two distinct zeros k₁ and k₂ such that p(k₁) = 0 and p(k₂) = 0.
Then: ak₁ + b = 0  and  ak₂ + b = 0.
Subtracting the second equation from the first:
(ak₁ + b) - (ak₂ + b) = 0 - 0
a(k₁ - k₂) = 0.
Since a ≠ 0, by the zero-product property of real numbers, we must have:
k₁ - k₂ = 0 ⟹ k₁ = k₂.
This contradicts the assumption of distinctness. Hence, the zero is strictly unique.

4. Contrast with Constant & Zero Polynomials (1.5 Marks):
• Constant Non-Zero Polynomial p(x) = c (c ≠ 0): Has NO real zero, because for any real input x, p(x) = c ≠ 0.
• Zero Polynomial p(x) = 0: Has infinitely many zeros; every real number k satisfies p(k) = 0.

LA 3 (5M) Determining Coefficients a and b from Simultaneous Values of p(x)

Problem: A polynomial is given by p(x) = x³ + ax² + bx - 6. If p(1) = 0 and p(-2) = 0, find the values of a and b. Then find the third zero of the polynomial.

Marking Scheme Breakdown

1. Formulate Equation 1 from p(1) = 0 (1.5 Marks):
p(1) = 1³ + a(1)² + b(1) - 6 = 0
1 + a + b - 6 = 0 ⟹ a + b - 5 = 0 ⟹ a + b = 5     ...(Eq. 1)

2. Formulate Equation 2 from p(-2) = 0 (1.5 Marks):
p(-2) = (-2)³ + a(-2)² + b(-2) - 6 = 0
-8 + 4a - 2b - 6 = 0
4a - 2b - 14 = 0 ⟹ 4a - 2b = 14
Dividing by 2: 2a - b = 7     ...(Eq. 2)

3. Solve Simultaneous Linear System (1 Mark):
Adding Eq. 1 and Eq. 2:
(a + b) + (2a - b) = 5 + 7
3a = 12 ⟹ a = 4.
Substitute a = 4 into Eq. 1:
4 + b = 5 ⟹ b = 1.

4. Finding the Third Zero (1 Mark):
The polynomial is p(x) = x³ + 4x² + x - 6.
Since (x - 1) and (x + 2) are factors:
(x - 1)(x + 2) = x² + x - 2.
Dividing x³ + 4x² + x - 6 by x² + x - 2 gives (x + 3).
Setting x + 3 = 0 ⟹ x = -3.
The third zero is x = -3.

Case Study 1 Bhubaneswar Smart City Green E-Auto Fare Structure

Case Context: Under the National Clean Air Programme, the Bhubaneswar Urban Transport Authority launched a fleet of zero-emission electric autorickshaws (Mo E-Ride). To ensure affordability and transparency, the transport authority formulated the following regulated tariff:

  • A fixed boarding/flag-down charge of ₹20 for the first kilometer.
  • For all subsequent distance, a uniform rate of ₹12 per kilometer.

Let the total distance covered be x km (where x ≥ 1), and let the total fare payable by the commuter be ₹y.

Sub-Questions & Complete Solutions:

Question (i): Formulate the Linear Equation (1 Mark)

Total distance = x km.
Distance beyond first km = (x - 1) km.
Total fare y = 20 + 12(x - 1) = 20 + 12x - 12
y = 12x + 8  (or in standard form: 12x - y + 8 = 0).

Question (ii): Calculate Fare for a Trip from Master Canteen to KIIT University (8 km) (1 Mark)

Here, x = 8 km.
y = 12(8) + 8 = 96 + 8 = ₹104.
The commuter pays ₹104.

Question (iii): If a passenger paid ₹140, what was the total journey distance? (1 Mark)

Here, y = 140.
140 = 12x + 8
12x = 140 - 8 = 132
x = 132 / 12 = 11 km.
The journey distance was 11 km.

Question (iv): Interpret the Slope and y-intercept in Physical Terms (1 Mark)

• Slope (m = 12): Represents the marginal cost (rate of fare increase) per additional kilometer traveled.
• y-intercept (8): Represents the mathematical base offset of the linear tariff model.

Case Study 2 Rooftop Solar Panel Efficiency & Temperature Loss Linear Model

Case Context: An environmental research lab in Sambalpur studied the performance of monocrystalline photovoltaic (PV) solar panels. Under standard laboratory testing conditions (STC) at 25°C, a solar panel produces an electric power output of 400 Watts. However, as the panel surface heats up under intense summer sunlight, its power output decreases linearly according to the mathematical model:

P(T) = -1.6 T + 440

where T is the panel surface temperature in degrees Celsius (°C) and P is the electrical power output in Watts (W).

Sub-Questions & Complete Solutions:

Question (i): Verify the power output at Standard Testing Condition (T = 25°C) (1 Mark)

Substitute T = 25:
P(25) = -1.6(25) + 440 = -40 + 440 = 400 Watts.
Verified! Matches the manufacturer's STC specification exactly.

Question (ii): What is the power output on an extreme peak summer day when T = 65°C? (1 Mark)

Substitute T = 65:
P(65) = -1.6(65) + 440 = -104 + 440 = 336 Watts.
The output drops to 336 Watts (a 16% reduction due to thermal heating).

Question (iii): Find the theoretical zero of the power polynomial P(T) and explain its physical significance (1 Mark)

Set P(T) = 0:
-1.6 T + 440 = 0 ⟹ 1.6 T = 440 ⟹ T = 440 / 1.6 = 275°C.
Physical Meaning: At a theoretical temperature of 275°C, semiconductor thermal excitation completely degrades voltage, reducing solar electrical power generation to zero.

Question (iv): What type of slope does this polynomial possess, and what does the sign indicate? (1 Mark)

The slope is a = -1.6 W/°C, which is negative.
The negative sign indicates an inverse relationship: as temperature increases, electrical power generation steadily decreases.

PYQ 3 (3M) If (2k - 1, k) is a solution of 10x - 9y = 12, find k

Since (2k - 1, k) is a solution, substituting x = 2k - 1 and y = k must satisfy the equation:
10(2k - 1) - 9(k) = 12
20k - 10 - 9k = 12
11k - 10 = 12
11k = 12 + 10 = 22
k = 22 / 11 = 2.

PYQ 4 (3M) Show that the linear polynomial p(x) = ax + b where a ≠ 0 has exactly one zero

Board Question: State whether the following statement is True or False, and provide complete mathematical justification: "A linear polynomial can have two distinct real zeros."

Model Answer

The statement is FALSE.
Justification:
A linear polynomial in one variable is given by p(x) = ax + b with a ≠ 0.
For any zero x, we have ax + b = 0 ⟹ ax = -b.
Since a ≠ 0, division by a is uniquely defined under the field axioms of real numbers:
x = -b / a.
Since b and a are fixed real constants, -b/a can take only one unique numerical value.
Geometrically, a non-horizontal, non-vertical straight line y = ax + b can intersect the horizontal line y = 0 (the x-axis) at at most one single point. Hence, a linear polynomial can NEVER have two distinct real zeros.

Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

5 Common Exam Hall Student Blunders & Topper Corrections

Trap 1: Confusing Zero of a Polynomial with the Number 0

Student Blunder: Thinking that the "zero" of a polynomial always means the input x = 0.
Bad Approach: Writing "The zero of p(x) = 2x - 10 is 0."
Topper Correction: The zero is the value of x that makes p(x) equal 0.
Setting 2x - 10 = 0 ⟹ 2x = 10 ⟹ x = 5. The zero is 5, NOT 0! Note: A polynomial can have 0 as a zero (e.g. p(x) = 3x), but zero is not always 0.

Trap 2: Stating Degree of the Zero Polynomial as 0

Student Blunder: Writing "Degree of polynomial 0 is 0."
Topper Correction: Degree 0 belongs to non-zero constant polynomials (e.g. p(x) = 8 = 8x⁰). For the Zero Polynomial p(x) = 0, since 0 = 0·x¹ = 0·x² = 0·x¹⁰⁰, the degree is strictly UNDEFINED (Not Defined).

Trap 3: Sign Inversion in the Linear Zero Formula x = -b/a

Student Blunder: Forgetting the negative sign and writing x = b/a, or mishandling existing negative signs.
Example: For p(x) = 4 - 3x: Here a = -3, b = 4.
Correct zero: x = -b/a = -4/(-3) = +4/3. Many students carelessly write -4/3.

Trap 4: Declaring an Expression Non-Polynomial Because of a Radical Coefficient

Student Blunder: Disqualifying √5 x + 3 as a polynomial because of √5.
Topper Correction: The whole number rule applies ONLY to the exponents of variables. Coefficients are allowed to be any real numbers, including irrationals (√2, √5, π). Only radicals over variables (like √x = x^(1/2)) disqualify expressions.

Trap 5: Interchanging Intercepts (x-intercept vs y-intercept)

Student Blunder: Writing the x-intercept as (0, -b/a) instead of (-b/a, 0).
Topper Correction: Remember:
• On the x-axis, y = 0 ⟹ Point is (-b/a, 0).
• On the y-axis, x = 0 ⟹ Point is (0, b).

15 High-Yield Final Exam Revision Bullet Points

  1. Algebraic Heritage: In ancient India, algebra was known as Avyakta-gaṇita (mathematics of unknown quantities), where Brahmagupta introduced Yāvattāvat (abbreviated yā) for unknown quantities.
  2. Polynomial Definition: An algebraic expression in variable x is a polynomial if and only if all exponents of x are whole numbers (𝕎 = {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}).
  3. Disqualifiers: Negative exponents (x⁻¹ = 1/x), fractional exponents (x^(1/2) = √x), and variables in denominators disqualify an expression from being a polynomial.
  4. Degree Definition: The highest exponent of the variable with a non-zero coefficient is called the degree of the polynomial.
  5. Degree of Constant Polynomial: A non-zero constant polynomial p(x) = c (c ≠ 0) has degree 0.
  6. Degree of Zero Polynomial: The zero polynomial p(x) = 0 has degree UNDEFINED (Not Defined).
  7. Linear Polynomial Form: A polynomial of degree 1 is called a linear polynomial; its standard form is p(x) = ax + b, where a, b ∈ ℝ and a ≠ 0.
  8. Why a ≠ 0: If a = 0, the polynomial collapses to p(x) = b, becoming a constant polynomial of degree 0, losing its linear status.
  9. Value of Polynomial: p(k) is the numerical value obtained by replacing variable x with real number k in p(x).
  10. Zero of Polynomial: A real number k is a zero (or root) of p(x) if and only if p(k) = 0.
  11. Unique Zero Formula: Every linear polynomial p(x) = ax + b (a ≠ 0) has one and only one real zero, given by x = -b/a = -(Constant Term) / (Coefficient of x).
  12. Geometric Representation: The graph of every linear polynomial y = ax + b on the Cartesian coordinate plane is a STRAIGHT LINE.
  13. Geometric Meaning of Zero: The zero of p(x) = ax + b is the x-coordinate (abscissa) of the point where the straight line intersects the x-axis: (-b/a, 0).
  14. Intercepts & Axes: The y-intercept is (0, b). The equation of the x-axis is y = 0; the equation of the y-axis is x = 0.
  15. Temperature Landmark: In the linear physics model F = (9/5)C + 32, the two temperature scales read the identical numerical value at exactly -40° (i.e., -40°C = -40°F).
