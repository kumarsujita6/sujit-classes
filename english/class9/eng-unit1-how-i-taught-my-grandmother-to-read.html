Part A: Prose Memoir

“How I Taught My Grandmother to Read” — Sudha Murty

Biographical & Contextual Setting Sudha Murty (born 1950 in Shiggaon, Haveri district, Karnataka) is a celebrated author, educator, engineer, and philanthropist. This autobiographical essay recounts an unforgettable episode from her childhood when she was twelve years old, living with her grandparents in a secluded village in north Karnataka. The narrative is set against an era before television and mobile phones, when the arrival of the weekly bus carrying newspapers and magazines was the village's sole window to the outer world.

1. The Village Atmosphere & The Weekly Arrival of ‘Karmaveera’

The story opens with young Sudha describing life in her north Karnataka village. Because transportation was poor in those days, the morning papers and weekly magazines did not arrive at dawn; they reached the village only by the afternoon bus around one o'clock. The entire village eagerly anticipated this arrival, but nobody waited more passionately than Sudha's sixty-two-year-old grandmother, Krishtakka (affectionately called Avva).

At that time, a popular Kannada weekly magazine titled ‘Karmaveera’ was publishing a serialised novel named ‘Kashi Yatre’ written by Triveni. Triveni was a remarkably gifted and prolific Kannada novelist whose psychological stories captured the delicate emotions, joys, and struggles of ordinary people, particularly women. Her writing style was easy, realistic, and deeply moving.

The Plot of Triveni's Serialised Novel: ‘Kashi Yatre’ Kashi Yatre tells the story of an elderly Hindu woman whose lifelong dream was to visit Kashi (Varanasi / Banaras) to worship Lord Vishweshwara and bathe in the sacred Ganga. In traditional Indian belief, a pilgrimage to Kashi and dying on its holy soil is regarded as the supreme path to spiritual liberation (moksha). The old woman in the novel saved every solitary copper coin, enduring severe privations for years to finance her holy journey.

However, right when she had accumulated sufficient funds, she encountered a young orphan girl in her village who had fallen in love with a virtuous young man. Because the orphan had no parents and no dowry money to host the wedding feast, the marriage was on the verge of collapsing. In a climactic moral choice, the elderly protagonist surrendered her entire pilgrimage savings to the orphan girl, declaring: “Serving an unhappy, living human being brings far greater spiritual merit (punya) than worshipping Lord Vishweshwara in a stone temple at Kashi.”

2. Krishtakka's Empathy & The Ritual of Weekly Reading

Sudha's grandmother, Krishtakka, had never gone to school and was entirely illiterate. She could neither read nor write a single letter of the Kannada script. Therefore, whenever the weekly bus delivered Karmaveera, Sudha had to sit with her grandmother and read the latest episode of Kashi Yatre aloud. Krishtakka would abandon all her domestic chores—milking cows, cooking meals, and tending to the courtyard—to listen with rapt attention and unwavering concentration.

Krishtakka identified completely with the protagonist of Kashi Yatre. Like the fictional heroine, Krishtakka had spent her entire lifetime in her village, had never visited Kashi, and harboured an intense spiritual yearning for salvation. She memorised every twist and dialogue of the serial. Later in the evening, when women gathered at the temple courtyard, Krishtakka would recount the entire episode from memory with vivid emotional depth, leading vibrant discussions on what the protagonist would do next.

3. The Complication: Sudha's Absence & The Agony of Helplessness

The central crisis of the narrative occurs when twelve-year-old Sudha leaves the village to attend a cousin's wedding in a neighbouring village. Weddings in rural India were lavish, multi-day celebrations filled with feasts, playful games with cousins, and laughter. Sudha ended up staying away for an entire week instead of a couple of days.

During Sudha's absence, Wednesday arrived, and with it, the afternoon bus carrying the latest issue of Karmaveera. The latest episode of Kashi Yatre had reached the most crucial, breathtaking turning point. Krishtakka took the magazine into her trembling hands. She opened the pages, stared at the printed Kannada letters, ran her fingers over the ink, and gazed at the illustrations. But the silent characters remained an impenetrable mystery. She could not decipher a single syllable.

Krishtakka experienced an excruciating sense of humiliation, vulnerability, and utter dependency. She contemplated asking someone else in the village to read it to her, but her pride and innate sense of dignity held her back. She felt deeply ashamed of exposing her illiteracy to neighbours. She felt trapped inside her own mind, orphaned from the story she loved so dearly.

4. The Climax: Tears on the Terrace & The Iron Vow

When Sudha finally returned home from the wedding, she found her grandmother sitting alone on the open terrace in the dark, weeping silently. Sudha was shocked and deeply distressed. In their village, elders were pillars of strength who consoled others; seeing her beloved Avva cry was unthinkable. Sudha rushed to her, asking if she was unwell or if someone had offended her.

Krishtakka gently held Sudha's hand and wiped her tears, narrating her painful backstory:

Krishtakka's Painful Backstory: The Structural Deprivation of Women “When I was a little girl, I lost my mother. My father was a busy man who soon remarried. In those days, nobody thought of educating girls; schooling was considered unnecessary for female children. So I was married off at a very tender age. Soon I had children, and later grandchildren. I spent my youth cooking, cleaning, feeding cows, and looking after the family. I never found the time, nor did anyone encourage me, to touch an alphabet. Now, when you were away, I held the magazine and wept. I realised how helpless and dependent an illiterate person is. Having money is meaningless if you cannot read the words that nourish your soul. I have decided: I will learn the Kannada alphabet starting tomorrow!”

Sudha was initially taken aback. Krishtakka was sixty-two years old! Her hair was grey, her hands were wrinkled, and she wore thick spectacles. Sudha playfully teased her, wondering how an elderly grandmother burdened with kitchen chores could master an intricate script. Krishtakka looked into Sudha's eyes with solemn resolve and proclaimed the golden axiom of lifelong education:

The Universal Axiom of Lifelong Learning “For learning, there is no age bar. If you have the determination, you can conquer any mountain. I will work harder than any young student. And I have set a strict deadline for myself: the day of Saraswati Puja during Dussehra (Vijayadashami). By that sacred day, I must be able to read Kashi Yatre independently!”

5. The Classroom of Dedication: Reverse Mentorship

From the next morning, an extraordinary educational partnership blossomed. The traditional social roles were completely reversed: the twelve-year-old schoolgirl became the Guru (Teacher), and the sixty-two-year-old grandmother became the humble, tireless Shishya (Disciple). Sudha assigned alphabets on a slate, set daily homework, and taught phonetics.

Krishtakka proved to be a student of miraculous diligence. While churning curd, boiling milk, peeling vegetables, or cleaning the cattleshed, she would recite the Kannada vowels and consonants under her breath. She traced characters in the air and repeated words until her pronunciation was flawless. Her focus was unshakeable. She treated every slate pencil mark as an act of sacred devotion.

6. The Climax on Vijayadashami: Guru Vandana & Ultimate Liberation

The festival of Dussehra (Vijayadashami) arrived. In Indian tradition, Vijayadashami marks the triumph of light over darkness, wisdom over ignorance, and is the auspicious day dedicated to Goddess Saraswati—the patron deity of learning, arts, and letters. On this day, new educational ventures (Vidyarambham) are consecrated, and disciples honour their teachers.

Krishtakka called Sudha into the family pooja room. With affectionate maternal warmth, she presented Sudha with a gift: a beautiful dress material for a frock. Then, in an instant that stunned young Sudha, Krishtakka did something totally unprecedented in Indian family life: she bent down and touched Sudha's feet!

Sudha jumped backward in alarm and embarrassment. In Indian culture, elders never touch the feet of children; youngsters touch the feet of elders to seek blessings. Krishtakka placed her hands on Sudha's shoulders and offered an unforgettable philosophical clarification:

Krishtakka's Sublime Philosophy of the Teacher “I am not touching the feet of my twelve-year-old granddaughter. Our scriptures instruct us that a teacher must always be respected, regardless of gender or age. Today, you are my Guru. You taught me with endless patience, love, and affection. You brought me out of the darkness of illiteracy into the light of letters. It is my sacred duty to touch the feet of my Guru on Vijayadashami day!”

Overcome with emotion, Sudha bowed down and touched her grandmother's feet in return, seeking her blessings. Then Sudha reached behind and presented her secret surprise: a brand-new, hardbound copy of the completed novel ‘Kashi Yatre’ by Triveni, which had just been published in book form.

With trembling fingers and eyes glistening with tears of triumph, Krishtakka opened the book. Right then and there, without stumbling, she read the title: “Kashi Yatre by Triveni”, along with the publisher's address, with flawless fluency and radiant independence. The student had passed with flying colours, and so had her young teacher!

Part B: Patriotic Ode

“Bharat Our Land” — Subramania Bharati

Poet Profile & Historical Context Subramania Bharati (1882–1921), revered as Mahakavi Bharati, was an iconic Tamil poet, visionary journalist, social reformer, and passionate Indian independence activist. Bharati's poetry played an instrumental role in igniting the spirit of patriotism and awakening the national consciousness during the Indian freedom struggle. “Bharat Our Land” is an English lyrical translation/adaptation of his celebrated patriotic verses celebrating India's matchless geographic splendour, ancient spiritual wisdom, heroic legacy, and visionary renaissance.

Stanza 1: Physical Majesty & Philosophical Grandeur

The mighty Himavant is ours — there's no equal anywhere on earth. The generous Ganga is ours — which other river can match her grace? The sacred Upanishads are ours — what scriptures else to name with them? This sunny golden land is ours — she's peerless, let's praise her!

Detailed Line-by-Line Exegesis:

  • Line 1 (“The mighty Himavant is ours — there's no equal anywhere on earth”): The poet begins with the possessive pronoun “ours”, instilling an immediate sense of collective ownership and national pride. Himavant (the Himalayas) represents not merely a physical mountain range, but an impenetrable geographic fortress, a climate regulator, and a sacred spiritual abode of silence and meditation. The hyperbole “there's no equal anywhere on earth” establishes unquestionable planetary supremacy.
  • Line 2 (“The generous Ganga is ours — which other river can match her grace?”): The river Ganga is personified as “generous” (bountiful, motherly, life-sustaining) because her fertile alluvial plains have nourished millions of people for millennia. The rhetorical question “which other river can match her grace?” forces the reader to acknowledge that no waterway possesses such sacred spiritual resonance and physical sustenance combined.
  • Line 3 (“The sacred Upanishads are ours — what scriptures else to name with them?”): The poem transitions from physical geography to intellectual and metaphysical transcendence. The Upanishads represent the crown jewel of Vedic philosophy, exploring profound existential questions concerning the soul (Atman), ultimate reality (Brahman), and universal cosmic unity. The second rhetorical question asserts that no other philosophical treatises on earth match their intellectual brilliance.
  • Line 4 & Refrain (“This sunny golden land is ours — she's peerless, let's praise her!”): “Sunny golden land” evokes a dual imagery: the sunlit warmth that yields bountiful golden grain fields, and the spiritual radiance of enlightenment that dispels ignorance. The word ‘peerless’ means matchless, having no equal. The recurring refrain acts as a joyful chorus uniting all citizens in celebratory devotion.

Stanza 2: Heroic Heritage, Sages & Moral Law

Gallant warriors have lived here, Many a sage has sanctified this land. The divinest music has been heard here, And here all auspicious things are found. Here Brahma-knowledge has taken root, And the Buddha preached his dhamma here. Of hoary antiquity is Bharat, she's peerless, let's praise her!

Detailed Line-by-Line Exegesis:

  • Lines 1–2 (“Gallant warriors have lived here, Many a sage has sanctified this land”): Bharati celebrates the perfect harmony of Kshatra-tejas (physical chivalry, courage, and protection of the weak) and Brahma-tejas (spiritual contemplation, ascetism, and ethical holiness). India's soil is sacred because it was purified by rishis (sages) seeking truth and guarded by fearless warriors fighting for righteousness.
  • Lines 3–4 (“The divinest music has been heard here, And here all auspicious things are found”): Refers to India's timeless musical traditions (the spiritual ragas of Carnatic and Hindustani classical music, Sama Veda chanting) which sought communion with the divine. “Auspicious things” (mangalata) implies an ethos steeped in sacred beauty, moral rectitude, and positive universal blessings.
  • Lines 5–6 (“Here Brahma-knowledge has taken root, And the Buddha preached his dhamma here”): “Brahma-knowledge” (Brahma-vidya) is the ultimate realization that the individual soul and cosmic consciousness are one. Simultaneously, the poet invokes Gautama Buddha, who walked this very soil preaching Dhamma—the path of universal compassion (Karuna), absolute non-violence (Ahimsa), and the alleviation of human sorrow. Thus, metaphysical wisdom and active ethical compassion walk hand-in-hand.
  • Lines 7–8 (“Of hoary antiquity is Bharat, she's peerless, let's praise her!”): “Hoary antiquity” denotes an ancient, timeless history stretching back thousands of continuous, unbroken years. While ancient civilizations of Greece, Rome, and Mesopotamia faded into museum relics, the living culture of Bharat continues to breathe and thrive.

Stanza 3: Vision of Fearlessness, Social Awakening & Perennial Abundance

Danger shall not scare us any longer, And poverty shall not sear our souls. Self-interest shan't drive us to meanness, And cowardly indiff'rence shall cease for ever. Here our land o'erflows with milk and honey, And perennial is the supply of fruit and corn. Ours is the famed Aryan land of Bharat: she's peerless, let's praise her!

Detailed Line-by-Line Exegesis:

  • Lines 1–2 (“Danger shall not scare us any longer, And poverty shall not sear our souls”): Writing during the height of British colonial exploitation, Bharati injects steel into the demoralised Indian populace. External intimidation, imperial tyranny, and economic destitution shall no longer paralyze the nation. To ‘sear’ means to scorch or burn painfully; poverty might cause bodily hardship, but it will never crush the immortal soul and dignity of India.
  • Lines 3–4 (“Self-interest shan't drive us to meanness, And cowardly indiff'rence shall cease for ever”): The poet issues a stirring moral indictment against narrow selfishness, communal divisiveness, and social apathy. A great civilisation cannot survive if citizens retreat into petty egoism or watch their brethren suffer with indifferent silence. National rejuvenation demands empathy, mutual solidarity, and civic courage.
  • Lines 5–6 (“Here our land o'erflows with milk and honey, And perennial is the supply of fruit and corn”): A celebratory biblical and classical idiom denoting extraordinary agricultural abundance, fertile cattle wealth, and natural prosperity. “Perennial” indicates unending, eternal supply—nature has blessed Bharat with all six seasons, rich rivers, and fruitful soils.
  • Lines 7–8 (“Ours is the famed Aryan land of Bharat: she's peerless, let's praise her!”): The word ‘Aryan’ is used here in its classical Sanskrit and civilisational connotation: derived from ‘Arya’, meaning noble, cultured, righteous, and honourable. It has zero racial connotations; it signifies a land founded upon noble dharmic ideals and spiritual integrity. The final crescendo of the refrain seals the anthem with unyielding national conviction.

Q5: Why did the grandmother touch the feet of her twelve-year-old granddaughter? Explain the traditional custom that was reversed.
Step 1: Core Concept

Reversal of Cultural Hierarchies in Honour of the Guru-Shishya Ethic.

Step 2: Textual Evidence

“I am touching the feet of a teacher, not my granddaughter; a teacher who taught me so well, with so much of affection... our scriptures say that a teacher should be respected, irrespective of the gender and age.”

Step 3: Analytical Explanation

In traditional Indian culture, the custom of Charansparsh (touching feet) is strictly hierarchical: youngsters touch the feet of elders to seek blessings, and it is culturally unthinkable for a 62-year-old matriarch to bow to a 12-year-old girl. However, on Vijayadashami, Krishtakka deliberately reversed this social hierarchy. She explained that she was not bowing to her young grandchild, but to the sacred institution of the Guru. Sudha had patiently illuminated her mind, dispelled her darkness of illiteracy, and conferred upon her the blessing of independence. In the realm of learning, the Guru reigns supreme over physical age.

Step 4: Topper Value Keywords

Charansparsh reversal, Guru Vandana on Vijayadashami, teacher transcendent over age, sacredness of knowledge, scriptural mandate.

Step 5: Final Model Answer

The grandmother touched her twelve-year-old granddaughter's feet as an act of profound Guru Vandana (honouring the preceptor) on the sacred day of Vijayadashami. In Indian tradition, youngsters invariably touch the feet of elders; an elder bowing to a child is culturally unprecedented. However, Krishtakka justified her action by citing the scriptures: a teacher must be venerated irrespective of age or gender. She touched Sudha's feet not as her grandmother, but as a devoted disciple paying homage to the dedicated teacher who had gifted her the light of literacy.

Young people, accustomed to high-speed interfaces, frequently exhibit impatience or mockery. Sudha's model demonstrates that young students must treat their elders with boundless empathy, patience, and warmth, dedicating unhurried time to bridge the generational digital divide.

2. The Power of Grit and Lifelong Learning:
Modern students frequently succumb to academic anxiety and defeatism when confronted with challenging mathematics, science concepts, or language skills. Krishtakka's example proves that age, past deprivations, and physical limitations cannot stop a person armed with resolute determination. Her mantra—“For learning, there is no age bar”—teaches students that intellectual mastery requires daily discipline, slate-by-slate practice, and a clear deadline.

3. Humility and the Sanctity of Knowledge:
Finally, Krishtakka's touching of Sudha's feet highlights that education is not an instrument of arrogance, but an agent of profound humility. Knowledge is a sacred trust meant to empower others, foster mutual respect, and create an inclusive society where no one is left in the dark.

Case Study 1: Extract from ‘How I Taught My Grandmother to Read’

Sub-Questions & Comprehensive Model Solutions:

(i) Why was the narrator shocked to find her grandmother weeping? (1M)
Answer: The narrator was shocked because Krishtakka was known throughout the family as an exceptionally strong, resilient woman who had never broken down even during the most severe family crises.

(ii) Identify the primary reason behind the grandmother's anguish. (1M)
Answer: Her utter inability to read the latest issue of Karmaveera during Sudha's absence, which made her feel deeply humiliated, helpless, and trapped by her illiteracy.

(iii) What philosophical truth about wealth and literacy does Krishtakka realize? (1M)
Answer: She realizes that material wealth and money are futile if one lacks literacy; financial affluence cannot grant intellectual independence, autonomy, and inner dignity.

(iv) Find a word from the extract that means ‘held firmly’. (1M)
Answer: ‘Gripped’.

Case Study 2: Extract from ‘Bharat Our Land’
“Danger shall not scare us any longer, / And poverty shall not sear our souls. / Self-interest shan't drive us to meanness, / And cowardly indiff'rence shall cease for ever. / Here our land o'erflows with milk and honey, / And perennial is the supply of fruit and corn...”

Sub-Questions & Comprehensive Model Solutions:

(i) What tone does the poet adopt in this extract? (1M)
Answer: A resolute, fearless, visionary, and inspiring patriotic tone calling for national awakening and moral courage.

(ii) What does the idiom ‘overflows with milk and honey’ signify? (1M)
Answer: It signifies extraordinary agricultural prosperity, abundance of natural nourishment, cattle wealth, and joyful fertility across the land.

(iii) Why does the poet condemn ‘cowardly indifference’? (1M)
Answer: Because apathy and indifference prevent citizens from standing up against injustice, foreign oppression, and social suffering, weakening the nation from within.

(iv) Explain the poetic device in ‘sear our souls’. (1M)
Answer: Alliteration (repetition of initial consonant /s/ sound) and Metaphor (comparing poverty to a scorching iron that burns without destroying the inner soul).

The 5 Most Fatal Student Blunders in Board Exams

Trap 1: Conflating Author Sudha Murty, Novelist Triveni, and Krishtakka

× Student Blunder: Writing that “Sudha Murty wrote Kashi Yatre about her own pilgrimage” or that “Krishtakka was the author of the weekly magazine.”
✓ Topper Correction: Keep three distinct layers clear:
1. Sudha Murty is the real-life 12-year-old child narrator and author of this autobiographical memoir.
2. Krishtakka (Avva) is Sudha's 62-year-old illiterate grandmother living in the Karnataka village.
3. Triveni (Anasuya Shankar) is the popular real-life Kannada novelist who wrote the fictional serialized novel ‘Kashi Yatre’ inside the weekly magazine ‘Karmaveera’.
4. The Protagonist of Kashi Yatre is the fictional elderly character within Triveni's novel who gave her pilgrimage savings to an orphan girl.

Trap 2: Misinterpreting Why Krishtakka Touched Sudha's Feet

× Student Blunder: Writing that “Krishtakka touched Sudha's feet because Sudha was an avatar of Goddess Saraswati” or “because it was a Dussehra custom for elders to worship youngest children.”
✓ Topper Correction: In Indian culture, elders never touch the feet of children. Krishtakka touched Sudha's feet solely as an act of Guru Vandana. She explicitly stated: “I am touching the feet of a Teacher, not my granddaughter. Our scriptures say a teacher must be respected, irrespective of gender and age.” Always cite this scriptural Guru-Shishya justification.

Trap 3: Imputing 20th-Century Racial Dogma to the Word ‘Aryan’

× Student Blunder: Equating the line “Ours is the famed Aryan land of Bharat” with Nazi racial ideology or foreign invaders.
✓ Topper Correction: In classical Indian literature and Subramania Bharati's nationalist Tamil verse, ‘Arya’ is a Sanskrit ethical term meaning noble, cultured, righteous, and honourable. It has zero biological or racial connotations. It denotes a civilisational ethos established on truth, dharma, and ethical nobility.

Trap 4: Incorrect Word Order in Reported Speech Conversions

× Student Blunder: Converting questions with inverted word order: “Sudha asked Avva why was she crying” or adding unnecessary connectors like “asked that why...”
✓ Topper Correction: Indirect interrogative clauses must take assertive word order (Subject + Verb): “Sudha asked Avva why she was crying.” Never use ‘that’ before a ‘wh-’ question word!

Trap 5: Vague Emotionalism in Long Answer Questions (LAQs)

× Student Blunder: Writing rambling essays on “grandmothers are very sweet and loving” without concrete textual citations.
✓ Topper Correction: CBSE evaluators award marks for specific textual anchors: cite the 1:00 PM afternoon bus, the weekly magazine Karmaveera, the novelist Triveni, the orphan girl's marriage in Kashi Yatre, Krishtakka's age (62) and Sudha's age (12), the Vijayadashami/Saraswati Puja deadline, and the exchange of the frock material and hardbound book.
