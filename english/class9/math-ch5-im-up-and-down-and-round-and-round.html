2.1 The Concept of a Circle: Locus, Centre, Radius, and Plane Partition

In everyday language, children think of a circle as a round shape like a coin, a bangle, or the wheel of a bullock cart. But in rigorous mathematics, a circle is not the flat solid disc inside; a circle is only the boundary curve.

Formal Mathematical Definition of a Circle

A circle is the collection (or locus) of all points in a plane that are at a fixed, constant distance from a fixed point in the same plane.
• The fixed point is called the centre of the circle (usually denoted as O or C).
• The fixed constant distance is called the radius of the circle (usually denoted as r).

Partition of the Plane: A circle drawn on a flat sheet of paper divides the entire plane into three disjoint regions:

  • The Interior of the circle: All points P such that the distance from the centre is less than the radius (OP < r).
  • The Circle itself: All points P lying on the boundary curve such that the distance from the centre equals the radius (OP = r).
  • The Exterior of the circle: All points P outside the circle such that the distance from the centre is strictly greater than the radius (OP > r).

The union of the circle and its interior is called the circular region or circular disc.

2.2 Infinite Symmetries: Reflectional and Continuous Rotational Invariance

A regular triangle has 3 lines of symmetry; a square has 4 lines of symmetry; a regular hexagon has 6 lines of symmetry. What happens to a circle?

1. Reflectional Symmetry (Line Symmetry): If you fold a circular paper disc exactly in half along any line passing through its centre, the two halves overlap perfectly. Because infinitely many straight lines can pass through a single point (the centre), a circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry. Every line of symmetry of a circle is a diameter line.

2. Rotational Symmetry: If you rotate a square about its centre, it matches its original position only at angles of 90°, 180°, 270°, and 360°. But if you rotate a circle about its centre O through any angle whatsoever (whether 1°, 0.5°, 37.4°, or 120°), it looks completely identical to its starting position. Thus, a circle has continuous rotational symmetry of infinite order.

2.3 Linear Elements: Chords, Diameters, Secants, and Tangents

Linear Element Definition & Characteristics Key Mathematical Property
Chord (ଜ୍ୟା) A line segment joining any two distinct points on the circumference of a circle. Connects two points A and B on the circle. Its length satisfies 0 < AB ≤ 2r.
Diameter (ବ୍ୟାସ) A chord that passes directly through the centre of the circle. Longest chord of the circle. Diameter d = 2r. It bisects the circle into two semicircles.
Secant (ଛେଦକ ରେଖା) A line that intersects the circle at two distinct points and extends indefinitely in both directions. Contains a chord as its internal line segment.
Tangent (ସ୍ପର୍ଶକ) A line in the plane that touches the circle at exactly one single point (Point of Contact). Perpendicular to the radius drawn through the point of contact.

2.4 Arcs, Sectors, and Segments: The Curved Anatomy

When you cut or divide a circle, you get curved pieces (arcs) and enclosed plane areas (sectors and segments):

  • Arc (ଚାପ): A continuous piece of a circle between two points. If points P and Q divide the circle into two unequal parts, the shorter curved length is the Minor Arc (denoted as arc PQ or ⌒PQ), and the longer curved length is the Major Arc (often written as arc PRQ, where R is a point on the major arc). When PQ is a diameter, both arcs are equal and each is called a Semicircle (ଅର୍ଦ୍ଧବୃତ୍ତ).
  • Circumference (ପରିଧି): The total perimeter or complete boundary length of the circle. For a circle of radius r, Perimeter = 2πr.
  • Segment (ବୃତ୍ତଖଣ୍ଡ): The region between a chord and either of its corresponding arcs.
    • Minor Segment: Region bounded by chord AB and the minor arc AB.
    • Major Segment: Region bounded by chord AB and the major arc AB.
  • Sector (ବୃତ୍ତକଳା): The slice-of-cake region between two radii joining the centre to the endpoints of an arc and the arc itself.
    • Minor Sector: Enclosed by two radii and the minor arc (central angle θ < 180°).
    • Major Sector: Enclosed by two radii and the major arc (central angle 360° - θ > 180°).

2.5 Historical Bharatiya Roots: Sulba Sūtras to Brahmagupta & Aryabhata

The study of circles in India extends back thousands of years to the Vedic and Harappan eras:

  • The Sulba Sūtras (c. 800–500 BCE): Indian master geometers such as Baudhāyana, Āpastamba, and Kātyāyana designed sacred brick fire altars (Yajña-vedis) of exact circular and square shapes. In these texts, the problem of Caturasra-karaṇa (transforming a circle into an equivalent square) and Vṛtta-karaṇa (circling the square, transforming a square into an equivalent circle) was solved with remarkable trigonometric and geometric precision using cords and pegs.
  • Aryabhata I (476–550 CE): In the Āryabhaṭīya (Gaṇitapāda, Verse 10), Aryabhata gave the world-famous approximation for π:
    "Add 4 to 100, multiply by 8, and then add 62,000. By this rule, the circumference of a circle with a diameter of 20,000 can be approached."
    π ≈ (100 + 4) × 8 + 62000 / 20000 = 832 + 62000 / 20000 = 62832 / 20000 = 3.1416. Aryabhata explicitly used the word āsanna (approximated), establishing for the first time in history that π is incommensurable (irrational).
  • Acharya Brahmagupta (598–668 CE): In the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta, Brahmagupta formulated the crown jewel of ancient geometry: the formula for the area of a cyclic quadrilateral (a four-sided polygon whose vertices all lie on a circle):
    Area = √[(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)(s - d)], where s = (a + b + c + d) / 2.

3.2 The 12 Foundational Circle Theorems of Ganita Manjari (Comprehensive Master Table)

Theorem # Formal Statement Given (Hypothesis) To Prove (Conclusion) Proof Framework & Congruence Criterion
Theorem 1 There is one and only one circle passing through three given non-collinear points. Three non-collinear points A, B, C in a plane. Only 1 circle passes through A, B, and C; its centre is the circumcentre O. Perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC intersect at a unique point O. By equidistant property, OA = OB = OC = r.
Theorem 2 Equal chords of a circle subtend equal angles at the centre. Circle with centre O; chords AB = CD. ∠AOB = ∠COD SSS Congruence: In △AOB and △COD, OA = OC (radii), OB = OD (radii), AB = CD (given). Thus △AOB ≅ △COD ⟹ ∠AOB = ∠COD (CPCTC).
Theorem 3 (Converse of Th 2) If the angles subtended by the chords of a circle at the centre are equal, then the chords are equal. Circle with centre O; ∠AOB = ∠COD. AB = CD SAS Congruence: In △AOB and △COD, OA = OC (radii), ∠AOB = ∠COD (given), OB = OD (radii). Thus △AOB ≅ △COD ⟹ AB = CD (CPCTC).
Theorem 4 The perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord. Circle with centre O; chord AB; OM ⊥ AB. AM = MB (M is midpoint of AB). RHS Congruence: In right △OMA and △OMB, ∠OMA = ∠OMB = 90°, OA = OB (radii), OM = OM (common). Thus △OMA ≅ △OMB ⟹ AM = MB (CPCTC).
Theorem 5 (Converse of Th 4) The line drawn through the centre of a circle to bisect a chord is perpendicular to the chord. Circle with centre O; chord AB; M is midpoint of AB (AM = MB). OM ⊥ AB (∠OMA = ∠OMB = 90°). SSS Congruence: In △OMA and △OMB, OA = OB (radii), AM = MB (given), OM = OM (common). △OMA ≅ △OMB ⟹ ∠OMA = ∠OMB. Linear pair sum = 180° ⟹ each = 90°.
Theorem 6 Equal chords of a circle (or of congruent circles) are equidistant from the centre. Chords AB = CD; OM ⊥ AB, ON ⊥ CD. OM = ON (Distance d₁ = d₂). Baudhāyana-Pythagoras theorem: OM² = r² - (AB/2)², ON² = r² - (CD/2)². Since AB = CD, OM² = ON² ⟹ OM = ON. (Alternatively RHS congruence).
Theorem 7 (Converse of Th 6) Chords equidistant from the centre of a circle are equal in length. OM ⊥ AB, ON ⊥ CD; distances OM = ON. AB = CD Baudhāyana-Pythagoras: (AB/2)² = r² - OM², (CD/2)² = r² - ON². Since OM = ON, (AB/2)² = (CD/2)² ⟹ AB = CD.
Theorem 8 (Central Angle Theorem) The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is double the angle subtended by it at any point on the remaining part of the circle. Arc AB subtending ∠AOB at centre O and ∠APB at point P on the remaining circumference. ∠AOB = 2 × ∠APB Join PO and extend to Q. Exterior angle of isosceles △AOP gives ∠AOQ = 2∠OAP. Exterior angle of △BOP gives ∠BOQ = 2∠OBP. Sum gives ∠AOB = 2∠APB. Proved for 3 cases (minor arc, semicircle, reflex major arc).
Theorem 9 Angles in the same segment of a circle are equal. Points P and Q in the same major (or minor) segment subtended by chord AB. ∠APB = ∠AQB By Theorem 8, ∠AOB = 2∠APB and ∠AOB = 2∠AQB. Therefore, 2∠APB = 2∠AQB ⟹ ∠APB = ∠AQB.
Theorem 10 The angle in a semicircle is a right angle (90°). AB is a diameter of circle with centre O; C is any point on the semicircle. ∠ACB = 90° Central angle of diameter AB is a straight angle: ∠AOB = 180°. By Theorem 8, ∠ACB = (1/2)∠AOB = (1/2)(180°) = 90°.
Theorem 11 The sum of either pair of opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral is 180° (supplementary). Quadrilateral ABCD inscribed in a circle. ∠A + ∠C = 180° and ∠B + ∠D = 180° Join centre O to B and D. Central angle ∠BOD = 2∠A. Reflex central angle = 2∠C. Sum of angles around point O is 360° ⟹ 2∠A + 2∠C = 360° ⟹ ∠A + ∠C = 180°.
Theorem 12 (Converse of Th 11) If the sum of a pair of opposite angles of a quadrilateral is 180°, then the quadrilateral is cyclic. Quadrilateral ABCD with ∠B + ∠D = 180°. Vertices A, B, C, D are concyclic (lie on a circle). Proof by contradiction. Assume the circle through A, B, C does not pass through D. Let it meet AD at D'. Then ABCD' is cyclic ⟹ ∠D' = 180° - ∠B = ∠D, contradicting exterior angle of △DD'C. Hence D must lie on the circle.

Part A: In-Text Hands-On Activities & "Think and Reflect" Prompts

Ganita Manjari emphasises experiential, discover-by-doing learning. Below are the complete step-by-step solutions to every in-text investigation and reflective prompt in Chapter 5:

In-Text Activity 1: Locating the Centre of an Unmarked Circular Paper Disc by Paper-Folding
Step 1: Given Materials & Objective

Take an unmarked circular sheet of paper or paper disc without any marked centre point. Find the exact mathematical centre O purely by folding.

Step 2: Underlying Mathematical Principle

By Theorem 4 and Theorem 5, the perpendicular bisector of any chord of a circle passes directly through the centre of the circle. Therefore, the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of any two non-parallel chords must be the exact centre O.

Step 3: Folding Procedure (Hands-On Steps)
  1. Fold the circular disc across any random straight line such that part of the curved boundary folds over itself. Crease this fold firmly and unfold. This crease forms a Chord AB.
  2. Now fold the circle such that endpoint A falls exactly onto endpoint B. The crease formed by this fold is the perpendicular bisector of chord AB (Line L₁). By Theorem 5, the centre O lies somewhere along Line L₁. In fact, this crease line is a diameter of the circle!
  3. Next, fold another crease across a different direction to create a second Chord CD (not parallel to AB).
  4. Fold endpoint C onto endpoint D to produce the perpendicular bisector of chord CD (Line L₂), which is a second diameter.
Step 4: Geometric Deduction

End-of-Chapter Question 22: “There is no chord of a circle that is longer than its diameter.” How do you justify this statement?
Step 1: Statement Breakdown

We must show that for any chord AB in a circle of radius r, Length(AB) ≤ 2r.

Let O be the centre. For any chord AB not passing through O, points A, B, O form △OAB.

AB < OA + OB
Since OA = r and OB = r:
AB < r + r = 2r.

AB = 2√(r² - d²), where d ≥ 0 is the perpendicular distance from O to AB.

Step 4: When Equality Holds

AB = 2r if and only if d = 0, which by definition means the chord passes through the centre—making it a diameter.

Step 5: Final Conclusion

Both proofs strictly establish that no chord can exceed 2r. The diameter is uniquely the longest chord of any circle.

End-of-Chapter Question 24: How would you use an inscribed triangle in a semicircle to justify that the angle in a semicircle is 90°?

Area = √[(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)] = √[2 × 1.5 × 2 × 1.5] = √(4 × 2.25) = 2 × 1.5 = 3.0 m².

Question 23 (PYQ 1): Concentric Circles Intersected by a Secant Line — Prove AB = CD
Step 1: Given Information

Two concentric circles with common centre O. A straight line L intersects the outer circle at A and D, and the inner circle at B and C (in order A, B, C, D).

Step 2: To Prove

AB = CD.

Step 3: Construction

Draw OM perpendicular to line L (OM ⊥ AD), meeting the line at M.

Step 4: Deduction Using Theorem 4

1. BC is a chord of the inner circle, and OM ⊥ BC.
By Theorem 4, OM bisects BC:
BM = MC --- (Equation 1).
2. AD is a chord of the outer circle, and OM ⊥ AD.
By Theorem 4, OM bisects AD:
AM = MD --- (Equation 2).
3. Subtracting Equation 1 from Equation 2:
AM - BM = MD - MC
Looking at the collinear points A-B-M and M-C-D:
AM - BM = AB
MD - MC = CD.

Step 5: Final Conclusion

Therefore, AB = CD. (Q.E.D.)

Question 24 (PYQ 2): Perpendicular Chords Intersecting at P — Prove PA² + PB² + PC² + PD² = 4r²
Step 1: Given Information

In a circle with centre O and radius r, two chords AB and CD are perpendicular to each other, intersecting at an interior point P (AB ⊥ CD).

Step 2: Construction

Drop perpendiculars OM ⊥ AB and ON ⊥ CD.
Since OM ⊥ AB, ON ⊥ CD, and AB ⊥ CD, quadrilateral OMPN has three right angles, so it is a rectangle!
Hence, PM = ON and PN = OM.

Step 3: Segment Breakdown

By Theorem 4, M bisects AB (AM = MB) and N bisects CD (CN = ND).
• PA = AM + PM = AM + ON
• PB = MB - PM = AM - ON
• PC = CN + PN = CN + OM
• PD = ND - PN = CN - OM.

Step 4: Algebraic Sum of Squares

Using the algebraic identity (x + y)² + (x - y)² = 2(x² + y²):
• PA² + PB² = (AM + ON)² + (AM - ON)² = 2(AM² + ON²).
• PC² + PD² = (CN + OM)² + (CN - OM)² = 2(CN² + OM²).
Adding both:
PA² + PB² + PC² + PD² = 2[(AM² + OM²) + (CN² + ON²)].
In right △OMA: AM² + OM² = OA² = r².
In right △ONC: CN² + ON² = OC² = r².
Therefore:
PA² + PB² + PC² + PD² = 2[r² + r²] = 2[2r²] = 4r².

Step 5: Final Conclusion

Hence proved: PA² + PB² + PC² + PD² = 4r² = (2r)² = Diameter². (Q.E.D.)

Question 25 (PYQ 3): Rhombus Side as Diameter Passes Through Diagonal Intersection
Step 1: Given Information

Let ABCD be a rhombus. Let its diagonals AC and BD intersect at point O. A circle is drawn taking side AB as a diameter.

Step 2: To Prove

The circle passes through point O (the point of intersection of the diagonals).

Step 3: Property of Rhombus Diagonals

A fundamental geometric property of any rhombus is that its diagonals bisect each other at right angles (90°).
Therefore:
∠AOB = 90°.

Step 4: Converse of Semicircle Right Angle Theorem (Theorem 10)

By Theorem 10, the angle subtended by a diameter at any point on the circumference of a circle is 90°.
Conversely, if a line segment AB subtends a right angle (90°) at a point O (∠AOB = 90°), then point O MUST lie on the circle described on AB as diameter!

Step 5: Final Conclusion

Because ∠AOB = 90°, point O lies on the circle with diameter AB. Hence proved: The circle described on any side of a rhombus as diameter always passes through the point of intersection of its diagonals. (Q.E.D.)

Exam Hall Trap Alerts & 15 High-Yield Revision Points

5 Fatal Exam Hall Blunders Students Make (And How to Avoid Them)

Trap 1: Doubling Instead of Halving in the Central Angle Theorem

The Blunder: When given that central angle ∠AOB = 70°, students mistakenly write the inscribed angle at the circumference as 70° × 2 = 140°!
The Fix: Always remember that the central angle is the BIG angle; the angle on the circle is the SMALL angle.
Inscribed Angle = Central Angle / 2 = 70° / 2 = 35°.

Trap 2: Forgetting to Halve the Chord Length in Pythagoras Calculations

The Blunder: Given chord AB = 16 cm and distance d = 6 cm, students write r² = 6² + 16²!
The Fix: The right triangle is formed by the half-chord AM, not the entire chord!
AM = 16 / 2 = 8 cm ⟹ r² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 ⟹ r = 10 cm.

Trap 3: Confusing Minor Segment Angles with Major Segment Angles

The Blunder: Assuming that because an arc is "minor", the angle inscribed in the minor segment must be "acute".
The Fix: The minor arc subtends an acute central angle (< 180°), so the angle inscribed in the major segment is acute. But the chord subtends the reflex central angle into the minor segment, making the angle in a minor segment strictly OBTUSE (> 90°)!

Trap 4: Missing the "Same Side" vs "Opposite Sides" Condition for Parallel Chords

The Blunder: When asked for the distance between two parallel chords, blindly adding their individual distances without checking which side of the centre they lie on.
The Fix:
• If chords are on opposite sides of centre: Total Distance = d₁ + d₂.
• If chords are on the same side of centre: Total Distance = |d₁ - d₂|.

Trap 5: Applying Brahmagupta's Formula to Non-Cyclic Quadrilaterals

The Blunder: Using Brahmagupta's formula Area = √[(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)] on any general four-sided polygon.
The Fix: Brahmagupta's formula is valid ONLY for quadrilaterals whose vertices all lie on a circle (cyclic quadrilaterals). For non-cyclic quadrilaterals, Bretschneider's formula applies, requiring an extra term subtracting abcd · cos²((A+C)/2).

15 Golden High-Yield Revision Points

  1. A circle is the locus of all coplanar points at a constant distance (radius r) from a fixed point (centre O).
  2. A circle possesses infinitely many lines of symmetry (every diameter is an axis of reflection) and continuous rotational symmetry.
  3. Through any three non-collinear points, there passes exactly one unique circle (the circumcircle, Theorem 1).
  4. Through three collinear points, exactly zero circles can pass.
  5. The circumcentre O of a triangle lies inside an acute triangle, at the midpoint of the hypotenuse in a right triangle, and outside an obtuse triangle.
  6. The perpendicular drawn from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord (Theorem 4), proved via RHS congruence.
  7. The line joining the centre of a circle to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to the chord (Theorem 5), proved via SSS congruence.
  8. Chords of equal length subtend equal angles at the centre (Theorem 2) and are equidistant from the centre (Theorem 6).
  9. The metric relationship for any chord of length l at perpendicular distance d from the centre is the Baudhāyana-Pythagoras formula: r² = d² + (l/2)² ⟺ l = 2√(r² - d²).
  10. The diameter is strictly the longest chord of a circle (length = 2r), located at distance d = 0 from the centre.
  11. The Central Angle Theorem (Theorem 8) states that the angle subtended by an arc at the centre is double the angle subtended by it at any point on the remaining circumference (∠AOB = 2 × ∠APB).
  12. Angles in the same segment of a circle are always equal (Theorem 9: ∠APB = ∠AQB).
  13. The angle inscribed in a semicircle is always a right angle (Theorem 10: ∠ACB = 90°).
  14. The opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary (Theorem 11: ∠A + ∠C = 180° and ∠B + ∠D = 180°).
  15. The exterior angle at any vertex of a cyclic quadrilateral is equal to the interior opposite angle (∠Ext = ∠Int.Opp).
