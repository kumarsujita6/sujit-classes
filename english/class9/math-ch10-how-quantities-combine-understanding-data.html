1.1 Architectural Conceptual Blueprint

In everyday life and advanced sciences, numerical quantities rarely exist in isolation. We frequently mix solutions, blend crop yields from farms of different sizes, combine test scores from classes with different enrollments, and merge quarterly sales from multiple store branches. The central theme of Chapter 10 in Ganita Manjari is moving beyond raw, simple arithmetic means to understand how quantities combine in the real world.

The Fundamental Guiding Principle of Chapter 10

"Averages cannot be combined by simply averaging them unless their underlying group sizes or weights are identical."
Whenever two or more quantities come together, each component pulls the combined outcome toward its own value in direct proportion to its weight, volume, frequency, or relative significance. This is known as the Law of Weighted Aggregation.

2.1 The Foundational Law: Arithmetic Mean and the Power of the Sum

In primary and middle school, data handling begins with the simple arithmetic mean (commonly termed the average). When given a set of N numerical observations x₁, x₂, x₃, ..., x_N, the arithmetic mean x̄ (pronounced x-bar) is defined as the grand total of all observations divided by the total count of observations:

Arithmetic Mean (x̄) = (Sum of all observations) ÷ (Total number of observations)
x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + ··· + x_N) / N  =  (∑ xᵢ) / N

While this formula is widely memorized, students often overlook its most powerful algebraic capability: The Inversion Principle. If you know the average and the number of data points, you can immediately determine the exact sum of all the values without knowing a single individual value!

Total Sum = N × x̄  =  (Total Number of Items) × (Mean Value)
Teacher's Secret: The Total Sum is the "Universal Currency"

Whenever data is modified—whether a new student joins a class, an athlete plays an extra match, an outlier is deleted, or a clerical recording error is corrected—NEVER work directly with the averages. Always convert the original average back into the Total Sum, add or subtract the adjustment, and then divide by the updated total count!

Mathematical Demonstration: Dynamic Adjustment of an Average

Consider a wildlife biologist tracking a migratory white stork. Over the first 20 days of autumn migration, the bird's average daily flight distance is recorded as 44.5 km. On the 21st day, riding strong thermal wind currents, the stork covers an impressive 55 km. What is its new 21-day average daily travel distance?

Step 1: Calculate the 20-Day Total Distance (Sum):
Total Distance (20 days) = N × x̄ = 20 × 44.5 km = 890 km.

Step 2: Add the 21st Day's Flight:
New Total Distance (21 days) = 890 km + 55 km = 945 km.

Step 3: Divide by the Updated Denominator (N + 1 = 21):
New Average = 945 km ÷ 21 = 45.0 km.
Notice how the 21st day's above-average flight of 55 km pulled the overall average up from 44.5 km to 45.0 km.

2.2 The Fallacy of the "Average of Averages"

Perhaps the single most persistent blunder in high school mathematics, competitive entrance tests, and everyday statistical reporting is the naive belief that "the average of two averages is simply their arithmetic mean".

The Universal Fallacy Trap

If Group 1 has an average score of a, and Group 2 has an average score of b, the combined average of the two groups combined is NOT equal to (a + b) / 2, UNLESS both groups contain the exact same number of members (n₁ = n₂)!

Concrete Proof & School Classroom Scenario

Suppose an inter-school mathematics assessment is conducted across two sections of Class 9:

  • Section A: Contains n₁ = 30 students with an average test score of x̄₁ = 72 marks.
  • Section B: Contains n₂ = 25 students with an average test score of x̄₂ = 76 marks.

A careless observer might calculate: (72 + 76) ÷ 2 = 148 ÷ 2 = 74.0 marks. Let us see why this naive calculation is mathematically false!

1. Total marks scored by Section A: Sum_A = n₁ × x̄₁ = 30 × 72 = 2160 marks.
2. Total marks scored by Section B: Sum_B = n₂ × x̄₂ = 25 × 76 = 1900 marks.
3. Grand Total marks scored by all students combined: Total Sum = 2160 + 1900 = 4060 marks.
4. Total number of students in both sections: Total Students = n₁ + n₂ = 30 + 25 = 55 students.
5. True Combined Average:

True Combined Average = 4060 ÷ 55 = 73.8181... ≈ 73.82 marks

Notice that the true average is 73.82, which is significantly lower than 74.0! Why? Because Section A has 30 students while Section B has only 25 students. Section A carries more "weight" (more students), so it exerts a stronger gravitational pull on the final score toward 72.

General Algebraic Formula for Combined Mean of Multiple Groups

If k distinct groups have sample sizes n₁, n₂, ..., n_k and respective means x̄₁, x̄₂, ..., x̄_k, the grand combined mean x̄_comb is given by:

x̄_comb = (n₁x̄₁ + n₂x̄₂ + n₃x̄₃ + ··· + n_k x̄_k) / (n₁ + n₂ + n₃ + ··· + n_k)  =  (∑ nᵢ x̄ᵢ) / (∑ nᵢ)

In the special case where all group sizes are equal (n₁ = n₂ = ··· = n_k = n), the factor n cancels out from the numerator and denominator, reducing the expression to the simple arithmetic mean: (x̄₁ + x̄₂ + ··· + x̄_k) / k.

2.3 The General Weighted Mean Formula & Mathematical Properties

The principle of the combined mean naturally generalizes into the Weighted Arithmetic Mean. In many practical scenarios, different data values do not contribute equally to a final decision. Instead, each data value xᵢ is assigned a non-negative numerical coefficient wᵢ called its weight.

Weighted Mean (x̄_w) = (w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + w₃x₃ + ··· + w_k x_k) / (w₁ + w₂ + w₃ + ··· + w_k)  =  (∑ wᵢ xᵢ) / (∑ wᵢ)

There are two primary categories of weights encountered in data analysis:

  • Frequency Weights (Objective Counts): The weight represents the physical number of units, people, days, or items (e.g. number of students in a class, number of days in a month, volume in milliliters). Here, wᵢ = nᵢ.
  • Significance Weights (Subjective / Policy Weights): The weight represents the relative importance, credit value, or curricular priority assigned by a policymaker, institution, or consumer (e.g. Final Exam carrying weight 5, Internals carrying weight 3; or Credit ratings in university courses).

Fundamental Mathematical Invariants of Weighted Means

  1. The Boundedness Invariant: The weighted mean can never lie outside the range of the constituent values. That is:
    min(x₁, x₂, ..., x_k) ≤ x̄_w ≤ max(x₁, x₂, ..., x_k)
    If all values are positive, the weighted average is strictly bounded between the smallest and largest values.
  2. Scale Invariance of Weights (Ratio Invariance): If all weights are multiplied by an arbitrary non-zero constant c > 0, the weighted mean remains completely unchanged:
    (∑ (c·wᵢ) xᵢ) / (∑ (c·wᵢ)) = [ c ∑ wᵢ xᵢ ] / [ c ∑ wᵢ ] = (∑ wᵢ xᵢ) / (∑ wᵢ) = x̄_w
    Therefore, weight ratios such as 3 : 4 : 5, 30 : 40 : 50, and 0.25 : 0.333 : 0.417 yield the identical numerical weighted mean!
  3. The Zero Balanced Deviation Principle: The sum of weighted deviations of all observations from their weighted mean is identically zero:
    ∑ wᵢ (xᵢ − x̄_w) = ∑ wᵢ xᵢ − x̄_w ∑ wᵢ = ∑ wᵢ xᵢ − [ (∑ wᵢ xᵢ) / (∑ wᵢ) ] ∑ wᵢ = 0
    This is the exact mathematical reason why the weighted mean represents the physical center of gravity or fulcrum!

2.4 Mixtures, Concentrations, and Physical Proportions

One of the most practical and fascinating applications of combining quantities is in chemistry, agriculture, cooking, and medicine: determining the concentration of a blended mixture.

Definition of Concentration

The concentration of a constituent in a solution or mixture is the fraction, percentage, or ratio of the active substance (solute) relative to the total quantity (solution):

Concentration (C) = (Amount of Pure Solute) ÷ (Total Amount of Mixture)

When multiple solutions of varying volumes V₁, V₂, ..., V_k and respective concentrations C₁, C₂, ..., C_k are poured together into a common vessel:

  1. Total pure solute contributed by each component is: Sᵢ = Vᵢ × Cᵢ.
  2. The grand total amount of pure solute is: S_total = ∑ (Vᵢ × Cᵢ).
  3. The grand total volume of the mixture is: V_total = ∑ Vᵢ.
  4. The final combined concentration is the volume-weighted mean:
    C_final = (V₁C₁ + V₂C₂ + ··· + V_k C_k) / (V₁ + V₂ + ··· + V_k)  =  (∑ Vᵢ Cᵢ) / (∑ Vᵢ)

Comparative Case Study: The Asymmetric Kashayam Blend (NCERT In-Text Exploration)

Consider two different ways of blending three medicinal spice syrups (Kashayam) having concentrations of 5%, 10%, and 15%:

Recipe Variant Volume at 5% Volume at 10% Volume at 15% Total Volume Total Pure Solute Final Concentration
Blend A (Heavy at 15%) 100 mL 200 mL 300 mL 600 mL (100×0.05) + (200×0.10) + (300×0.15) = 5 + 20 + 45 = 70 mL 70 ÷ 600 = 11.67%
Blend B (Heavy at 5%) 300 mL 200 mL 100 mL 600 mL (300×0.05) + (200×0.10) + (100×0.15) = 15 + 20 + 15 = 50 mL 50 ÷ 600 = 8.33%

Both blends use identical total volumes (600 mL) and identical concentration ingredients (5%, 10%, 15%). Yet Blend A yields an 11.67% solution, while Blend B yields only 8.33%! Blend A allocated the largest volume (300 mL) to the most concentrated ingredient (15%), skewing the weighted outcome heavily upward.

2.5 Historical Heritage: Ancient Indian Roots in Brahmagupta & Śrīdharācārya

Centuries before modern European statisticians formalized weighted averages, classical Indian mathematicians developed rigorous algebraic and geometric techniques to combine non-uniform quantities in engineering, civil works, and metallurgy.

1. Brahmagupta (c. 598–668 CE): Khātagaṇita (Excavation of Irregular Trenches)

In his monumental treatise Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (Chapter 12, Ganita section), Brahmagupta addressed a vital civil engineering challenge faced by builders of public irrigation canals, ditches, and temple moats: How to calculate the earthwork volume of an excavation whose depth varies unpredictably along its length and width.

If an excavation pit (khāta) has varying depths d₁, d₂, d₃, ..., d_k measured across different cross-sectional areas or surface spans A₁, A₂, A₃, ..., A_k, Brahmagupta instructed engineers not to take an unweighted average of the extremes, but to compute the area-weighted mean depth (Sama-Vedha):

Mean Depth (d̄_w) = (A₁d₁ + A₂d₂ + ··· + A_k d_k) / (A₁ + A₂ + ··· + A_k)
Excavation Volume = (Total Surface Area) × (d̄_w)

2. Śrīdharācārya (c. 750–850 CE): Suvarṇa-Gaṇita (Gold Alloy Purity & The Varṇa Scale)

In his celebrated arithmetic manual Pāṭīgaṇita (and its abridgment Triśatikā), the great mathematician Śrīdharācārya formulated verses specifically for goldsmiths and royal treasurers calculating the purity of mixed gold ingots.

In ancient India, gold purity was measured on a 16-point scale known as Varṇa (वर्ण). Completely pure, unalloyed gold was designated as 16 Varṇa (equivalent to modern 24-karat gold or 100% fineness). When lower-grade gold coins or ornaments with purities v₁, v₂, ..., v_k and weights (masses) m₁, m₂, ..., m_k are melted together into a single composite ingot, Śrīdharācārya established the Suvarṇa-Varṇa formula:

Combined Purity in Varṇa (v̄_w) = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ + ··· + m_k v_k) / (m₁ + m₂ + ··· + m_k)  =  (∑ mᵢ vᵢ) / (∑ mᵢ)

This is the exact modern formula for the mass-weighted arithmetic mean!

2.6 Combining Scores Across Different Maximum Marks (Multi-Scale Normalization)

In modern education systems (CBSE, ICSE, and state boards), a student's final report card mark is formed by aggregating diverse assessments: internal unit tests, term projects, laboratory vivas, and the comprehensive annual written board exam.

The Deadly Raw Score Addition Trap

You can NEVER add raw marks when the assessments have different maximum marks! Adding 35 out of 50 to 80 out of 100 as (35 + 80) ÷ (50 + 100) automatically gives unequal, unintended weights to the tests and distorts the policy ratio.

The Standard Normalization Algorithm

To compute a weighted composite score when maximum marks differ:

  1. Step 1 (Normalize to a Common Metric): Convert every component score into an unweighted fraction or percentage:
    pᵢ = (Raw Score Scored ÷ Maximum Marks) × 100%
  2. Step 2 (Apply Assigned Policy Weights): Multiply each percentage by its allocated weight ratio wᵢ.
  3. Step 3 (Divide by the Sum of Weights):
    Final Composite Percentage = [ ∑ wᵢ × ((Raw Score Scored ÷ Max Marks) × 100) ] ÷ [ ∑ wᵢ ]

This is exactly the core problem presented in Exercise Set 10.2 (Savitri's Kashmiri marks), where her scores are:

  • Internal Tests: 35 out of 50  ⇒  (35/50) × 100 = 70.0%
  • Project: 44 out of 60  ⇒  (44/60) × 100 = 73.33%
  • Final Exam: 80 out of 100  ⇒  (80/100) × 100 = 80.0%
  • Weight Ratio: 3 : 4 : 5

Her annual percentage score is:

Annual Score (%) = [ (70.0 × 3) + (73.333 × 4) + (80.0 × 5) ] ÷ (3 + 4 + 5)
= [ 210.0 + 293.333 + 400.0 ] ÷ 12  =  903.333 ÷ 12  =  75.28%

2.7 Business & Multi-Tier Profit Combinations

In retail economics and commercial accounting, businesses sell multiple lines of merchandise with vastly differing profit margins. A store owner cannot evaluate overall business profitability by averaging the percentage margins of their inventory items.

As featured in Exercise 10.3, consider a stationery shop owner who registers:

  • Sale of Books: ₹8000 total sales with a 30% profit margin.
  • Sale of Book Covers: ₹1000 total sales with a 50% profit margin.

The naive average of profits is (30% + 50%) ÷ 2 = 40%. However, books generate 8 times more revenue than covers!

Profit from Books = 30% of ₹8000 = (30/100) × 8000 = ₹2400
Profit from Covers = 50% of ₹1000 = (50/100) × 1000 = ₹500
Total Profit = ₹2400 + ₹500 = ₹2900
Total Sales Revenue = ₹8000 + ₹1000 = ₹9000
Overall Profit Margin = (Total Profit ÷ Total Revenue) × 100 = (2900 ÷ 9000) × 100 = 32.22%

Because books make up 88.9% of all sales, the composite profit is 32.22%—far closer to 30% than to 50%!

2.8 Advanced Data Visualization: Stacked vs. 100% Stacked Bar Charts

Data visualization is not merely about making aesthetically pleasing graphics; it is about conveying mathematical truth without cognitive bias. In Chapter 10, the curriculum progresses through three major chart types:

1. Clustered (Grouped) Column Chart

Multiple bars are placed immediately adjacent to one another for each primary category.

  • Prime Advantage: Extremely easy to compare identical sub-categories across different groups (e.g. comparing Student A's study hours directly against Student B's study hours).
  • Key Limitation: The reader cannot easily gauge the aggregate total of each group without performing mental addition.

2. Regular Stacked Bar Chart

All sub-categories for a group are stacked vertically atop each other within a single compound column.

  • Prime Advantage: Shows both the overall total volume (indicated by the topmost summit of the column) and the internal composition of parts.
  • How to Read Internal Segments: The size of any intermediate segment is calculated as:
    Segment Size = (Upper Boundary Reading) − (Lower Boundary Reading)
  • Key Limitation: Because upper segments do not share a common horizontal baseline, comparing middle components between different bars is visually challenging.

3. 100% Stacked Bar Chart

Every column is artificially scaled to extend to the exact same total height representing 100%. Each slice represents the percentage share of that category relative to that group's own total:

Segment Percentage = (Absolute Value of Component ÷ Total Value of that Column) × 100%
  • Prime Advantage: Allows immediate, clean visual comparison of relative proportions across disparate groups regardless of how radically different their total absolute volumes are. It serves as a linear, rectangular substitute for multiple pie charts.
  • The Irreversibility Theorem: You can always convert an absolute stacked bar chart into a 100% stacked bar chart (by computing percentages). However, you CANNOT convert a 100% stacked bar chart back into an absolute stacked bar chart unless the grand total absolute value of each column is independently supplied! Once normalized to 100%, absolute volume information is permanently lost.

3.3 Golden Invariants & Mathematical Principles of Chapter 10

Whenever analyzing compound data, five fundamental mathematical invariants must always remain uncompromised:

Invariant 1: The Boundedness Theorem (Convex Hull Property)

For any non-negative set of weights where at least one weight is positive, the weighted mean x̄_w is strictly bounded: x_min ≤ x̄_w ≤ x_max. It is physically and mathematically impossible for a mixture of 5% and 15% solutions to result in a 4% or 16% concentration!

Invariant 2: Linear Transformation Homogeneity

If every observation xᵢ is subjected to a linear scale-and-shift transformation yᵢ = a · xᵢ + b, the new weighted average satisfies the exact same transformation: ȳ_w = a · x̄_w + b.

Invariant 3: Scale Invariance of Weights

The value of a weighted average depends solely on the ratios between the weights, never on their absolute magnitudes. Multiplying all weights by any positive scalar c > 0 leaves x̄_w identical.

Invariant 4: Zero Deviation Equilibrium

The sum of weighted residuals from the weighted mean is always zero: ∑ wᵢ (xᵢ − x̄_w) = 0. Every data point above the weighted average is perfectly balanced by the data points below it, weighted by their respective magnitudes.

Invariant 5: Irreversibility of 100% Normalization

Mapping absolute data to relative percentage distributions (f: (x₁, x₂, ..., x_k) ↦ (p₁, p₂, ..., p_k)) is a many-to-one transformation. A 100% stacked bar chart showing a 50% share could represent 5 out of 10 people, or 5,000,000 out of 10,000,000 people. You cannot reverse this mapping without the absolute denominator!

In-Text Prompt 10.1: The Average of Averages Debate

Problem Statement: In a school assembly, Ramesh claims: "Section 9A scored an average of 70 marks in Mathematics, and Section 9B scored an average of 80 marks. Therefore, the overall average of Class 9 must be exactly (70 + 80) ÷ 2 = 75 marks." Deepali argues: "That is only true if both sections have identical numbers of students." Who is correct, and what is the exact algebraic condition?

Step 1: Given Information & Identification

Section 9A has mean x̄₁ = 70 with unknown count n₁. Section 9B has mean x̄₂ = 80 with unknown count n₂. Ramesh proposes x̄_comb = (x̄₁ + x̄₂) / 2 = 75.

Step 2: Underlying Principle & Conceptual Rationale

The true combined mean of two datasets is given by the weighted average: x̄_comb = (n₁x̄₁ + n₂x̄₂) / (n₁ + n₂). Equating this to the unweighted mean (x̄₁ + x̄₂) / 2 yields the necessary and sufficient algebraic condition.

Step 3: Step-by-Step Algebraic Substitution & Proof

Set the combined mean equal to 75:

(70n₁ + 80n₂) / (n₁ + n₂) = 75
⇒ 70n₁ + 80n₂ = 75(n₁ + n₂)
⇒ 70n₁ + 80n₂ = 75n₁ + 75n₂
⇒ 80n₂ − 75n₂ = 75n₁ − 70n₁
⇒ 5n₂ = 5n₁  ⇒  n₁ = n₂

If n₁ = 40 and n₂ = 10, the true average is: [(40 × 70) + (10 × 80)] ÷ 50 = (2800 + 800) ÷ 50 = 3600 ÷ 50 = 72.0 marks, not 75!

Step 4: Final Conclusion & Mathematical Stance

Deepali is completely correct. The combined average equals 75 if and only if both sections have the exact same number of students (n₁ = n₂). If student counts differ, the section with more students exerts greater influence.

Step 5: Teacher's Verification Check & Pitfall Warning

Exam Pitfall: Never compute the arithmetic mean of two averages on an exam paper unless the problem explicitly states that the group sizes are identical.

In-Text Activity 10.2: The 30 cm Ruler & Coin Fulcrum Experiment

Activity Setup: Take a uniform 30 cm wooden ruler. Place a triangular eraser at the 15 cm mark to serve as a fulcrum. Place a stack of 3 identical coins at the 6 cm mark. Where must a stack of 1 coin be placed to achieve perfect horizontal equilibrium?

Step 1: Given Information & Identification

Mass on left m₁ = 3 units at coordinate x₁ = 6 cm. Fulcrum (Center of Mass / Weighted Mean) is fixed at x̄_w = 15 cm. Mass on right m₂ = 1 unit at unknown coordinate x₂.

Step 2: Underlying Principle & Conceptual Rationale

By Archimedes' Principle of the Lever (Torque Equilibrium) and the Zero Weighted Deviation property of the weighted mean: m₁(x̄_w − x₁) = m₂(x₂ − x̄_w).

Step 3: Step-by-Step Algebraic Substitution & Calculation
Distance to left mass d₁ = 15 cm − 6 cm = 9 cm
Left Torque = m₁ × d₁ = 3 units × 9 cm = 27 unit·cm
Right Torque must equal Left Torque:
1 unit × (x₂ − 15 cm) = 27 unit·cm
⇒ x₂ − 15 = 27  ⇒  x₂ = 42 cm

Because the 30 cm ruler ends at 30 cm, a single coin cannot balance 3 coins placed at 6 cm on a 30 cm ruler pivoted at the center! To balance on the ruler, the 3-coin stack must be moved closer to the fulcrum (e.g. at 11 cm: 3 × 4 cm = 12, so 1 coin is placed at 15 + 12 = 27 cm).

Step 4: Final Conclusion

The experiment physically demonstrates that weight and distance from the center are inversely related. To balance a larger quantity, a smaller quantity must be located significantly farther away from the center.

Step 5: Teacher's Verification Check

Verify via weighted mean formula: x̄_w = [(3 × 11) + (1 × 27)] ÷ (3 + 1) = (33 + 27) ÷ 4 = 60 ÷ 4 = 15 cm. Perfect match!

5.2 Assertion & Reasoning Questions (CBSE Pattern)

Directions: Each question consists of an Assertion (A) and a Reason (R). Choose from:
(A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
(C) A is true, but R is false.
(D) A is false, but R is true.

AR Question 1: Combined Averages of Unequal Classes

Assertion (A): If Section 1 has 40 students with an average score of 80, and Section 2 has 20 students with an average score of 50, the combined average of the two sections is 70 marks.
Reason (R): The combined average of two groups is calculated using the weighted mean formula x̄_comb = (n₁x̄₁ + n₂x̄₂) / (n₁ + n₂), which accounts for the difference in sample sizes.

Correct Option: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Detailed Analysis:
Total score = (40 × 80) + (20 × 50) = 3200 + 1000 = 4200 marks. Total students = 40 + 20 = 60. Combined average = 4200 ÷ 60 = 70.0 marks. Both statements are mathematically true, and Reason R provides the exact algebraic justification for Assertion A.

AR Question 2: Information Loss in 100% Stacked Bar Charts

Assertion (A): From a 100% stacked bar chart comparing the household expenses of two families, one cannot determine which family spends more money on food.
Reason (R): In a 100% stacked bar chart, all column heights are normalized to 100%, displaying only percentage proportions while completely omitting the absolute financial totals.

Correct Option: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Detailed Analysis:
If Family X spends 50% on food out of a ₹20,000 monthly income, their food expenditure is ₹10,000. If Family Y spends 30% on food out of a ₹100,000 monthly income, their food expenditure is ₹30,000. Family Y spends three times more money, yet Family X has a taller segment in a 100% stacked chart! Reason R correctly explains why Assertion A is true.

AR Question 3: Boundedness of Weighted Means

Assertion (A): When mixing two fertilizer solutions with nitrogen fractions of 0.12 and 0.28, it is impossible to obtain a final mixture with a nitrogen fraction of 0.32.
Reason (R): A weighted average of non-negative quantities is a convex combination and must strictly lie within the closed interval bounded by the minimum and maximum constituent values: x_min ≤ x̄_w ≤ x_max.

Correct Option: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Detailed Analysis:
Here x_min = 0.12 and x_max = 0.28. Therefore, any valid physical mixture must satisfy 0.12 ≤ x̄_w ≤ 0.28. A concentration of 0.32 violates the Boundedness Theorem. Reason R is the universal theorem explaining Assertion A.

AR Question 4: Multi-Scale Raw Marks Addition

Assertion (A): If a student scores 40 out of 50 in an internal test and 70 out of 100 in the final exam, their overall composite performance cannot be determined by calculating (40 + 70) / (50 + 100) = 110 / 150 ≈ 73.33%.
Reason (R): When assessments have different maximum marks, directly adding raw marks unintentionally weights each test by its maximum mark rather than by the prescribed institutional weight ratio.

Correct Option: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Detailed Analysis:
In the raw sum, the 100-mark exam automatically receives twice the influence of the 50-mark test, regardless of whether the teacher intended them to have equal weights or a 1:1 ratio. Proper scoring requires normalizing to percentages first: 40/50 = 80%, 70/100 = 70%, which on an equal 1:1 ratio yields (80 + 70)/2 = 75.0%. Reason R is the exact mathematical explanation.

AR Question 5: Invariance under Uniform Scaling of Weights

Assertion (A): If every weight wᵢ in a weighted mean calculation is doubled (wᵢ' = 2wᵢ), the calculated weighted mean doubles in value.
Reason (R): The weighted mean formula is homogeneous of degree zero with respect to weights: (∑ 2wᵢ xᵢ) / (∑ 2wᵢ) = 2(∑ wᵢ xᵢ) / [2(∑ wᵢ)] = (∑ wᵢ xᵢ) / (∑ wᵢ).

Correct Option: (D) A is false, but R is true.

Detailed Analysis:
Assertion A is FALSE because doubling all weights does NOT double the weighted mean—it leaves the weighted mean completely unchanged! The factor of 2 cancels out from the numerator and denominator. Reason R is a mathematically correct theorem that directly disproves Assertion A.

SA 2 (2 Marks): The Round-Trip Average Speed Fallacy

Question: A delivery van travels 120 km from Sambalpur to Bargarh at an average speed of 60 km/h, and returns over the same 120 km route at an average speed of 40 km/h. Why is the average speed for the round trip NOT 50 km/h? Compute the true average speed.

Conceptual Reason: The van spends more time traveling at the slower speed than at the faster speed. Average speed is total distance divided by total time (harmonic mean), not the arithmetic mean of speeds! [1 Mark]
Time outbound = 120 km ÷ 60 km/h = 2 hours
Time return = 120 km ÷ 40 km/h = 3 hours
Total Time = 2 + 3 = 5 hours; Total Distance = 120 + 120 = 240 km
True Average Speed = 240 km ÷ 5 h = 48.0 km/h [1 Mark]

The school policy dictates that these four components carry weight in the ratio 2 : 2 : 3 : 3.

(b) Calculate the student's final composite percentage score for the year. [2.0 Marks]

• Project/Practical: (27 ÷ 30) × 100% = 90.0% [0.5 Mark]
• Mid-Term: (64 ÷ 80) × 100% = 80.0% [0.25 Mark]

Part (b): Weighted Composite Calculation [2.0 Marks]
Weights: w₁ = 2, w₂ = 2, w₃ = 3, w₄ = 3. Sum of weights = 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 10 [0.5 Mark]
Weighted Sum = (80 × 2) + (90 × 2) + (80 × 3) + (85 × 3)
= 160 + 180 + 240 + 255 = 835 [1.0 Mark]
Final Percentage = 835 ÷ 10 = 83.5% [0.5 Mark]

Part (c): Erroneous Raw Sum Calculation & Contrast [1.5 Marks]
Total raw scored = 32 + 27 + 64 + 85 = 208 marks
Total raw maximum = 40 + 30 + 80 + 100 = 250 marks [0.5 Mark]
Raw percentage = (208 ÷ 250) × 100% = 83.2% [0.5 Mark]
Error = 83.5% − 83.2% = +0.3% distortion. Raw addition unlawfully penalized the student's outstanding 90% score in projects because the project carried only 30 raw marks! [0.5 Mark]
LA 2 (5 Marks): Chemical Fertilizer Continuous Industrial Blending Plant

Examination Problem: An agricultural chemical fertilizer plant in Paradeep, Odisha, feeds three liquid nitrogen streams into a continuous blending tank:
• Stream 1 discharges at 120 L/min with 15% nitrogen.
• Stream 2 discharges at 180 L/min with 25% nitrogen.
• Stream 3 discharges at 100 L/min with 10% nitrogen.
(a) Find the total flow rate of the mixed liquid leaving the plant. [1.0 Mark]
(b) Calculate the overall nitrogen concentration percentage in the discharge. [2.5 Marks]
(c) If the agronomist requires the final output concentration to be raised to exactly 20% by adding an ultra-rich 50% nitrogen solution through a fourth auxiliary injector, at what flow rate (in L/min) must Stream 4 be pumped? [1.5 Marks]

Marking Rubric & Step-by-Step Model Answer:

Part (a): Total Discharge Flow Rate [1.0 Mark]
Total Flow Rate Q = 120 + 180 + 100 = 400 L/min [1.0 Mark]

Part (b): Current Nitrogen Concentration [2.5 Marks]
Pure nitrogen flow rate from each stream:
• Stream 1 = 120 × 0.15 = 18 L/min pure nitrogen [0.5 Mark]
• Stream 2 = 180 × 0.25 = 45 L/min pure nitrogen [0.5 Mark]
• Stream 3 = 100 × 0.10 = 10 L/min pure nitrogen [0.5 Mark]
Total pure nitrogen flow = 18 + 45 + 10 = 73 L/min [0.5 Mark]
Current Nitrogen % = (73 ÷ 400) × 100% = 73 ÷ 4 = 18.25% [0.5 Mark]

Let auxiliary stream flow rate be Q₄ L/min at 50% (0.50). Desired concentration = 20% (0.20).
Total pure nitrogen = 73 + 0.50 Q₄
Total mixture volume = 400 + Q₄
Set up balance equation:
(73 + 0.50 Q₄) ÷ (400 + Q₄) = 0.20 [0.5 Mark]
⇒ 73 + 0.50 Q₄ = 0.20(400 + Q₄)
⇒ 73 + 0.50 Q₄ = 80 + 0.20 Q₄
⇒ 0.50 Q₄ − 0.20 Q₄ = 80 − 73
⇒ 0.30 Q₄ = 7  ⇒  Q₄ = 7 ÷ 0.30 = 70 ÷ 3 = 23.33 L/min [1.0 Mark]
LA 3 (5 Marks): Historical Synthesis: Brahmagupta vs. Śrīdharācārya

Examination Problem: Classical Indian mathematicians pioneered weighted averaging in distinct practical domains.
(a) State Brahmagupta's rule for computing the earthwork excavation of an irregular ditch (Khātagaṇita), and explain how it represents an area-weighted average. [2.0 Marks]
(b) State Śrīdharācārya's rule for melting gold alloys (Suvarṇa-Varṇa). [1.0 Mark]
(c) Solve the following authentic problem adapted from Śrīdharācārya's Pāṭīgaṇita: A royal mint melter combines 6 suvarṇas of 10-varṇa gold, 8 suvarṇas of 12-varṇa gold, and an unknown quantity x of 15-varṇa gold. If the resulting bullion has a certified purity of exactly 13 varṇa, find the unknown mass x. [2.0 Marks]

Marking Rubric & Step-by-Step Model Answer:

Part (a): Brahmagupta's Khātagaṇita [2.0 Marks]
Brahmagupta stated in Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta that when an excavation has irregular depth along its expanse, taking the simple average of extreme depths gives an erroneous volume. Instead, the trench is partitioned into cross-sections of measured area Aᵢ with corresponding depth dᵢ. The true mean depth is the area-weighted average: d̄_w = (∑ Aᵢ dᵢ) / (∑ Aᵢ), and the total excavation volume is Volume = A_total × d̄_w. [2.0 Marks]

Part (b): Śrīdharācārya's Suvarṇa-Varṇa [1.0 Mark]
Śrīdharācārya established that when multiple gold ingots of masses mᵢ and purities vᵢ (measured on a 16-varṇa scale) are melted together, the resulting purity is: v̄_w = (∑ mᵢ vᵢ) / (∑ mᵢ). [1.0 Mark]

Part (c): Solving the Pāṭīgaṇita Problem [2.0 Marks]
Given: m₁ = 6, v₁ = 10; m₂ = 8, v₂ = 12; m₃ = x, v₃ = 15. Combined purity v̄ = 13.
Sum of Purity Units = (6 × 10) + (8 × 12) + (x × 15) = 60 + 96 + 15x = 156 + 15x [0.5 Mark]
Total Mass = 6 + 8 + x = 14 + x [0.5 Mark]
Equating to combined purity:
(156 + 15x) ÷ (14 + x) = 13 [0.5 Mark]
⇒ 156 + 15x = 13(14 + x)
⇒ 156 + 15x = 182 + 13x
⇒ 15x − 13x = 182 − 156
⇒ 2x = 26  ⇒  x = 13 suvarṇas [0.5 Mark]

PYQ 1: Finding Missing Group Frequency from Combined Mean

CBSE Standard Question: The average monthly salary of workers in a manufacturing unit is ₹12,000. The average salary of 20 senior technicians is ₹15,000, and the average salary of junior staff is ₹10,000. Find the total number of junior staff employed in the unit.

Step 1: Identify variables: n₁ = 20, x̄₁ = 15,000; n₂ = x (unknown), x̄₂ = 10,000; Grand Mean x̄ = 12,000.
Step 2: Set up combined mean equation:
[ (20 × 15,000) + (x × 10,000) ] ÷ (20 + x) = 12,000
Step 3: Solve for x:
300,000 + 10,000x = 12,000(20 + x)
⇒ 300,000 + 10,000x = 240,000 + 12,000x
⇒ 300,000 − 240,000 = 12,000x − 10,000x
⇒ 60,000 = 2,000x  ⇒  x = 30 junior staff
Verification: 20 × (15,000 − 12,000) = 20 × 3,000 = 60,000; 30 × (12,000 − 10,000) = 30 × 2,000 = 60,000. Equilibrium satisfied!

Board Question: The mean of 100 observations was calculated as 40.0. Later, it was found that two observations 30 and 20 were misread as 3 and 72 respectively. Find the true, corrected mean.

Step 1: Incorrect Sum = 100 × 40.0 = 4000
Step 2: Sum of incorrect values subtracted = 3 + 72 = 75
Step 3: Sum of correct values added = 30 + 20 = 50
Step 4: Corrected Sum = 4000 − 75 + 50 = 3975
Step 5: Corrected Mean = 3975 ÷ 100 = 39.75
PYQ 3: Successive Dilution and Concentration

Probable Exam Question: A vessel contains 40 liters of a 15% alcohol solution. How many liters of pure alcohol (100% concentration) must be added to increase the strength of the solution to exactly 32%?

Step 1: Initial pure alcohol = 15% of 40 L = 0.15 × 40 = 6.0 liters
Step 2: Let x liters of pure alcohol (100% = 1.0) be added.
New total pure alcohol = 6.0 + x
New total volume = 40 + x
Step 3: Desired concentration = 32% = 0.32:
(6.0 + x) ÷ (40 + x) = 0.32
⇒ 6 + x = 0.32(40 + x)

6.1 Exam Hall Trap Alerts: 5 Common Student Blunders

Examiners specifically construct multiple-choice options and word problems to catch common misconceptions. Guard yourself against these five traps:

Trap 1: The "Average of Averages" Direct Addition Trap

Student's Blunder: When asked to combine the average test score of Class A (mean 70, 30 students) and Class B (mean 80, 20 students), the student calculates (70 + 80) ÷ 2 = 75.

The Mathematical Truth: You can NEVER average two averages directly unless the group sizes are identical! The true combined mean must use the sum of products: [(30 × 70) + (20 × 80)] ÷ 50 = (2100 + 1600) ÷ 50 = 3700 ÷ 50 = 74.0.

Teacher's Memory Shield: Remember the "Total Sum Rule": Always convert averages back into sums before combining!

Trap 2: The Raw Score Addition Trap Across Unequal Maximum Marks

Student's Blunder: A student scores 35 out of 50 in Unit Tests and 80 out of 100 in the Final Exam. The student computes their overall score as (35 + 80) ÷ (50 + 100) = 115 ÷ 150 = 76.67%.

The Mathematical Truth: Adding raw marks accidentally weights the Final Exam twice as heavily as the Unit Test simply because of the arbitrary test length! If the policy mandates equal weighting (1:1), each assessment must first be converted into a percentage (35/50 = 70%; 80/100 = 80%), giving a true composite score of (70 + 80) ÷ 2 = 75.0%.

Teacher's Memory Shield: Normalize to percentages BEFORE applying institutional weight ratios!

Trap 3: Confusing Relative Percentage Share with Absolute Quantity in 100% Stacked Charts

Student's Blunder: Looking at a 100% stacked bar chart where School A's sports participation segment is 40% and School B's segment is 25%, the student concludes: "School A has more student athletes than School B."

The Mathematical Truth: A 100% stacked bar chart displays only proportions, never absolute counts! If School A has 100 total students (40 athletes) and School B has 1,000 total students (250 athletes), School B has more than six times as many athletes as School A despite having a smaller percentage bar!

Teacher's Memory Shield: A percentage is a ratio, not a headcount. Never compare absolute sizes from a 100% stacked chart without knowing the bar totals!

Trap 4: The Pure Solvent Dilution Fallacy

Student's Blunder: When 200 mL of pure water is added to 300 mL of a 10% saline solution, the student calculates the final concentration as (10% + 100%) ÷ 2 or forgets that water carries a concentration of 0%.

The Mathematical Truth: Pure solvent (water) contains 0% solute (C_water = 0%). However, it adds directly to the denominator! The new concentration is: (300 × 0.10 + 200 × 0.0) ÷ (300 + 200) = 30 ÷ 500 = 6.0%.

Teacher's Memory Shield: Remember that pure water adds ZERO pure solute, but INCREASES the denominator volume!

Trap 5: The "New Average" Denominator Neglect Trap

Student's Blunder: The average of 10 observations is 50. A new observation of value 72 is added. The student adds 72 to the old sum (500 + 72 = 572) but divides by the old count 10 (572 ÷ 10 = 57.2).

The Mathematical Truth: When a new observation is introduced, the total count increases from N to N + 1! The correct division is 572 ÷ 11 = 52.0.

Teacher's Memory Shield: Always increment the denominator by 1 when a new observation is added!

6.2 15 High-Yield Rapid Revision Points

  1. Arithmetic Mean Foundation: Defined as x̄ = (∑ xᵢ) / N. It represents the equalized share of a numerical quantity distributed across all observations.
  2. Sum Inversion Principle: The grand total of any dataset is instantly recoverable via Total Sum = N × x̄. All dynamic data modifications must operate through this total sum.
  3. The Average of Averages Condition: The combined mean of two groups equals the simple average of their means ((x̄₁ + x̄₂)/2) if and only if both groups have identical sizes (n₁ = n₂).
  4. General Combined Mean Formula: For k groups, the combined mean is the frequency-weighted average: x̄_comb = (∑ nᵢ x̄ᵢ) / (∑ nᵢ).
  5. Gravitational Attraction of Weights: In a weighted average, the final outcome is always pulled closer to the group with the larger weight, sample size, or volume.
  6. Boundedness Property (Convex Combination): The weighted average is strictly bounded between the lowest and highest values: x_min ≤ x̄_w ≤ x_max.
  7. Mixture Concentration Law: When blending liquid solutions or chemical mixtures, final concentration is the volume-weighted mean of constituent concentrations: C_final = (∑ Vᵢ Cᵢ) / (∑ Vᵢ).
  8. Dilution Invariant: Adding pure water to an aqueous solution increases the total volume while adding 0% solute, reducing the concentration inversely proportional to the volume expansion factor.
  9. Brahmagupta's Khātagaṇita (7th Century CE): Earthwork excavation volume of irregular canals and ditches is calculated using the area-weighted mean depth (Sama-Vedha): d̄_w = (∑ Aᵢ dᵢ) / (∑ Aᵢ).
  10. Śrīdharācārya's Suvarṇa-Gaṇita (8th Century CE): Gold bullion purity on the ancient 16-point Varṇa scale is calculated as the mass-weighted average of ingots: v̄_w = (∑ mᵢ vᵢ) / (∑ mᵢ).
  11. Multi-Scale Assessment Normalization: Scores from tests with unequal maximum marks must be normalized into individual percentages before applying assigned institutional weight ratios.
  12. Commercial Profit Margin Aggregation: Overall business profit percentage is revenue-weighted: Total Profit ÷ Total Revenue × 100%, not the average of item margins.
  13. Clustered vs. Stacked Bar Charts: Clustered column charts excel at comparing individual sub-categories across groups; Stacked bar charts excel at showing both the aggregate total volume and internal component makeup.
  14. 100% Stacked Bar Chart Function: Normalizes all columns to 100% to display relative percentage shares, serving as a rectangular equivalent of multiple pie charts for comparing compositional distributions.
  15. The Irreversibility Principle: Converting an absolute stacked bar chart into a 100% stacked bar chart is always possible; converting a 100% stacked bar chart back into an absolute stacked bar chart is impossible without knowing the absolute total of each bar.
